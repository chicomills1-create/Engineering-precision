HVAC & Mechanical Systems

Mechanical (HVAC) Engineering Services

Heating, cooling, and ventilation systems sized from real load calculations — designed for occupant comfort, energy-code compliance, and equipment that lasts because it isn't oversized.

Load Calculations First, Equipment Second

Most comfort complaints, short-cycling equipment, and humidity problems trace back to one root cause: systems sized by square-footage rules of thumb instead of engineering. Every mechanical design we produce starts with a room-by-room heating and cooling load calculation — actual wall, roof, and glazing constructions, real orientation and shading, occupancy and equipment gains, and the local design-day conditions. Only then do we select equipment, and we select it at the calculated load, derated for altitude and climate where applicable.

System selection is matched to the building, not to habit: packaged rooftop units for single-story commercial, split and VRF systems where zoning and part-load efficiency matter, dedicated outdoor air systems where ventilation loads dominate, and hydronic systems where the scale justifies them. Duct design uses static-regain or equal-friction methods with documented pressure-drop budgets, so the fan we schedule actually delivers the airflow on the plans.

Ventilation is engineered to ASHRAE 62.1 (or the mechanical code's equivalent tables): outdoor-air rates by occupancy, demand-controlled ventilation where the energy code requires it, and exhaust systems — restaurant hoods, restroom exhaust, process exhaust — designed with makeup air so the building isn't fighting itself. Kitchen and Type I hood projects include the hood, duct, fire-wrap, and makeup-air package the fire marshal will ask about.

Compliance, Controls, and Replacements

Mechanical permit sets include the energy-code documentation reviewers demand: equipment efficiencies against the adopted IECC or Title 24 requirements, economizer applicability, duct insulation and sealing notes, and controls narratives covering thermostatic zoning, setback, and demand-controlled ventilation. Structural coordination is built in — rooftop unit weights and anchorage go to our structural team, not to a note that says 'by others.'

We also handle the smallest common scope in mechanical engineering: like-for-like and upsized equipment replacements. Many jurisdictions now require permits, load justification, and energy compliance even for changeouts, and we turn those packages around quickly so a failed unit doesn't become a failed inspection.

Designing for the Climate the Building Actually Lives In

A mechanical design that works in coastal California will underperform in Phoenix and fail differently in Minneapolis. Design-day conditions drive everything: condenser capacity derates steeply at 115°F ambient, so equipment selected from nominal ratings comes up short exactly when the building needs it most; cold-climate heat pumps need balance-point analysis and supplemental heat sizing; humid-climate buildings need latent capacity and sometimes reheat that a dry-climate design would never include. Because we engineer across 49 states, checking the actual ASHRAE design conditions for the project site — not a national default — is built into our load calculation workflow.

Altitude, economizer requirements, and ventilation strategy shift by geography too. Above roughly 2,500 feet, air density corrections change fan and equipment selections. Energy codes make airside economizers mandatory in some climate zones and pointless in others. Humid regions punish over-ventilated buildings with mold risk; arid regions reward evaporative pre-cooling that would be useless in the Southeast. These are not exotic optimizations — they are the difference between a system that meets its design intent and one that generates comfort complaints for the life of the building.

The engagement itself is straightforward: backgrounds and use description in, fixed-fee proposal out, typically within a day. Design includes coordination with our own structural engineers for equipment support and anchorage and with our electrical engineers for connected loads — so the mechanical schedule, the structural roof framing check, and the electrical panel schedule always agree. After permit we review equipment submittals against scheduled capacities (a substituted unit that is two percent short on paper can be twenty percent short at design conditions), answer RFIs, and verify test-and-balance reports so the airflows the drawings promised are the airflows the building delivers.

Indoor air quality and pressurization round out the discipline. Buildings need to be engineered slightly positive relative to outdoors in most climates — negative buildings pull unconditioned, unfiltered, humid air through every crack in the envelope, which is how conference rooms grow musty and drywall grows mold. We balance outdoor air against exhaust room by room, specify filtration appropriate to the use (MERV 13 has become the de facto floor for many owners since 2020), and design kitchen, laboratory, and process exhaust with the capture velocities and makeup-air paths that keep contaminants moving out rather than drifting into adjacent spaces. These details never appear in a rule-of-thumb design, and they are precisely the ones occupants notice. When an existing building has chronic odor, humidity, or stuffiness complaints, a pressurization and airflow diagnosis is usually where the answer lives, and it is a standalone scope we take on regularly — measurement, root-cause analysis, and a corrective design the owner can permit and build.

Mechanical Engineering FAQs

Why does oversizing matter if bigger seems safer?
An oversized system satisfies the thermostat before it dehumidifies or mixes the air, so you get short cycles, clammy spaces, uneven temperatures, and premature compressor wear — plus a larger electrical service than the building needed. Right-sizing from a load calculation avoids all of it.
Do I need an engineer for an equipment changeout?
Increasingly, yes. Many jurisdictions require permits, load justification, and energy-code compliance for replacements — especially upsizing. We produce changeout packages quickly, including the structural anchorage check for rooftop units.
Can you design a kitchen exhaust system?
Yes — Type I and Type II hoods, grease duct routing and fire protection, exhaust fan sizing, and the makeup-air system, coordinated as one package with the fire and building reviewers in mind.
What is a DOAS and do I need one?
A dedicated outdoor air system conditions ventilation air separately from the heating/cooling terminals. It shines in buildings with high occupancy or strict humidity control — schools, gyms, medical — and we'll tell you honestly when your project doesn't need one.
Do your mechanical designs include controls?
Every set includes a controls narrative and points summary appropriate to the system — thermostatic zoning, scheduling and setback, economizer logic, and demand-controlled ventilation where the energy code requires it.
