Why municipalities hire Apex Grid Engineering

Most municipal engineering moves at the speed of the incumbent on-call firm — which is to say, slowly. Plan reviews stretch for months, RFQ responses read like they were written by committee, and the engineers who sealed the drawings are three layers removed from the people answering your questions. Apex Grid Engineering was built as the alternative: a veteran-owned firm with licensed professional engineers in 49 states plus the District of Columbia, organized around one idea — municipalities should get permit-ready, responsible-charge engineering on a schedule that respects public money.

Our civil, structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing engineers work as one coordinated team under a single engineer of record. That integration is the speed moat: no inter-firm handoffs between the water-plant process design and the structural design of the basins, no coordination gaps between the lift station's electrical service and its controls. For a public works director managing a bond-funded capital program, the difference shows up as fewer RFIs, fewer change orders, and drawings that survive plan review the first time.

Water and wastewater treatment plant design

Municipal wastewater treatment is where Apex Grid's process, civil, structural, and MEP engineers converge. We design new treatment plants and plant upgrades end to end: influent pumping and headworks, preliminary and primary treatment, biological processes (activated sludge, BNR for nutrient removal, MBR where footprint or effluent quality demands it), secondary clarification, filtration, disinfection (UV or chlorine), solids handling and dewatering, and odor control. Every design starts from the NPDES permit — the effluent limits for BOD, TSS, ammonia, nitrogen, and phosphorus dictate the process train, and we engineer backward from compliance.

Upgrades are the larger market: plants built in the 1970s and 1980s under the Clean Water Act construction-grants era are hitting the end of their structural and mechanical lives at the same time nutrient limits are tightening. We specialize in the phased upgrade — keeping a plant in compliance and in service while basins are rebuilt, blowers replaced, and controls modernized. Our structural engineers assess the existing concrete structures, our process engineers re-rate capacity, and our MEP engineers modernize the electrical distribution, MCCs, SCADA, and HVAC that keep the plant running.

  • NPDES permit-driven process selection
  • BNR and ENR nutrient-removal design
  • MBR and advanced treatment
  • Phased upgrades with plants kept in service
  • Solids handling, dewatering, and odor control
  • SCADA, MCC, and electrical modernization

Municipal drinking water systems

On the drinking water side, we engineer the full system: supply (wells, surface-water intakes, and increasingly advanced treatment for potable reuse), treatment plants (conventional, membrane, and advanced oxidation trains selected for the source water), storage (ground and elevated tanks sized for equalization, fire flow, and emergency reserve per AWWA standards), and distribution (hydraulic modeling, pressure-zone design, pump stations, and water-age management). Disinfection strategy — chlorine, chloramine, UV, ozone — is designed around disinfection-byproduct rules and the system's specific chemistry.

Two national programs are reshaping municipal water engineering: lead service line replacement, now mandated on aggressive timelines in many states, and PFAS regulation, which is pushing treatment plants toward granular activated carbon, ion exchange, or reverse-osmosis retrofits. Both are engineering-heavy, funding-eligible, and schedule-sensitive — exactly the work our integrated team is built for.

  • AWWA-standard storage, pumping, and distribution design
  • Membrane and advanced treatment trains
  • Lead service line replacement programs
  • PFAS treatment retrofits (GAC, ion exchange, RO)
  • Disinfection and DBP compliance strategy
  • Hydraulic modeling and water-age management

Bridges, culverts, and municipal structures

Apex Grid's structural engineers design and assess the structures municipalities own: short- and medium-span bridges, culverts, retaining walls, pump-station structures, tank foundations, and treatment-plant basins. Bridge work follows AASHTO LRFD — load rating, rehabilitation design, and scour countermeasures — coordinated with the state DOT's standards and the National Bridge Inspection Standards rhythm that governs every public bridge in the country. Concrete durability in wastewater environments (sulfide attack, chemical exposure) is a specialty: we specify the mixes, coatings, and details that keep structures serviceable for their design lives.

For buildings and process structures, ASCE 7 governs: risk-category-driven seismic and wind design, flood-resistant detailing in FEMA-mapped floodplains, and the load combinations that keep critical facilities — pump stations, emergency operations, water treatment — standing through the design event. Municipalities own structures that cannot fail during the storm they were built for; we engineer them that way.

  • AASHTO LRFD bridge design and load rating
  • NBIS-aligned inspection support
  • Scour analysis and countermeasures
  • ASCE 7 seismic, wind, and flood design
  • Wastewater-environment concrete durability
  • Retaining walls, culverts, and small structures

Stormwater, floodplains, and MS4 compliance

Municipal stormwater engineering sits at the intersection of the Clean Water Act's MS4 program, FEMA floodplain regulation, and the reality that the 100-year storm keeps arriving more often. We design conveyance, detention, and green-infrastructure systems sized for current rainfall data — not the atlases of the 1970s — and we navigate the floodplain permitting: FEMA map revisions, Letters of Map Amendment, floodway encroachment analysis, and the local floodplain ordinances that overlay them.

For MS4 permittees, we engineer the program's physical side: outfall inventories, illicit-discharge detection infrastructure, post-construction BMPs, and the retrofit projects that turn a paper stormwater management plan into actual pollutant reduction. Green infrastructure — bioretention, permeable pavement, subsurface detention — gets designed as real civil infrastructure with maintenance access and verifiable performance, not as landscaping.

  • MS4 program engineering and BMP design
  • FEMA floodplain permitting and map revisions
  • Detention, conveyance, and green infrastructure
  • Rainfall-data-driven hydrologic design
  • Illicit discharge detection infrastructure
  • Flood-resilient utility siting

Funding the project: SRF, IIJA, and WIFIA

The engineering is only half the municipal project — the funding is the other half, and the best-designed project dies without it. The federal-state funding stack for municipal water infrastructure runs through the Clean Water and Drinking Water State Revolving Funds: low-interest loans (often with principal forgiveness for qualifying communities) administered by each state's designated agency, capitalized by EPA grants supercharged under the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act. We design projects to be funding-ready: the engineering reports, environmental reviews, and cost-effectiveness analyses that SRF programs require before a project makes the Intended Use Plan's priority list.

Above the SRF tier, WIFIA (the Water Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act) offers long-term, low-cost federal credit for large water projects, and IIJA's direct appropriations continue to flow through state programs. Our engineers speak funding fluently — we sequence design deliverables to match application windows, structure phasing so the first operable segment qualifies independently, and document the disadvantaged-community and green-project criteria that unlock principal forgiveness. A municipality that hires Apex Grid gets engineering and a funding strategy in one team.

  • SRF Intended Use Plan positioning
  • Principal-forgiveness and disadvantaged-community criteria
  • WIFIA credit assistance for large projects
  • IIJA/BIL funding navigation
  • Engineering reports for funding applications
  • Phasing design to match funding windows

Procurement-ready: built for qualifications-based selection

Here's what most engineering firms won't tell municipalities: in most states, public agencies are legally required to select engineers on qualifications, not low bid — the federal Brooks Act and the state 'mini-Brooks' laws that mirror it. The selection happens through RFQs and RFPs scored on experience, key personnel, past performance, and approach. Which means the firm that wins municipal work isn't the cheapest — it's the most convincingly qualified and the most responsive.

Apex Grid is built for that selection environment. Our statements of qualifications lead with licensed engineers in the project jurisdiction, relevant treatment-plant / bridge / stormwater project experience, and a project approach written around the municipality's actual constraints — funding windows, permit schedules, keeping facilities in service during construction. And once selected, we perform like the schedule matters, because public money and public health are on the line. Fast plan-review turnaround, single-point responsibility, and drawings that get built without drama: that's the RFQ promise we actually keep.

  • Licensed PEs in 49 states + DC
  • Single engineer of record, single point of responsibility
  • Fast plan-review and submittal turnaround
  • Responsible-charge sealing — never stamp-only

How does the State Revolving Fund process work for our project?
Each state's SRF program publishes an annual Intended Use Plan listing priority projects. Your project needs an engineering report, environmental review, and a priority ranking to make the list — then loan (often with principal forgiveness) financing follows. We prepare the engineering deliverables SRF programs require and sequence design to match application windows.
Can you help a small town with a lagoon upgrade or a single lift station?
Yes — small-system work is a core part of the municipal practice. Lagoons, package plants, lift stations, and collection-system extensions are exactly the projects SRF programs were built to fund, and our engineers design them efficiently without the overhead of a mega-firm.
How do municipalities select an engineering firm — low bid?
Generally no. Under the federal Brooks Act and most states' mini-Brooks laws, public agencies must select engineers through qualifications-based selection (QBS): RFQs scored on experience, personnel, and approach — not price. Being RFQ-ready with relevant experience and licensed staff in the jurisdiction is what wins municipal work.
