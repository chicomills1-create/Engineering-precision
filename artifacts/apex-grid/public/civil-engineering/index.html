Civil Engineering Services

Grading, drainage, stormwater, and utility design that bridges raw land and vertical construction — engineered to each jurisdiction's published standards so site plans clear review without redesign cycles.

From Feasibility to Approved Site Plans

The economics of a development are usually decided before the foundation is poured. Our civil work starts at feasibility: conceptual grading studies that expose cut/fill imbalances, utility availability checks with the serving districts, and preliminary stormwater sizing that reveals how much of the parcel a detention requirement will consume. Finding those constraints during due diligence costs a few thousand dollars; finding them after acquisition can cost the project.

Grading and drainage design balances earthwork, sets finished floor elevations above design storm elevations, establishes positive drainage away from structures, and maintains accessible routes at ADA-compliant slopes — running slope, cross slope, and landing requirements verified across every path of travel, not just the striped parking stalls. Drainage design is computed, not sketched: time-of-concentration, rational-method or hydrograph-based peak flows, inlet spacing, and pipe sizing all appear in a drainage report the reviewer can check.

Stormwater management is where local variation is fiercest, and where our multi-state code research pays off. We design retention basins where jurisdictions require full on-site retention, detention with controlled release where downstream capacity governs, and water-quality treatment — bioretention, permeable pavement, proprietary units — where MS4 permits demand it, each sized to the locally mandated storm events and documented in the format the reviewing agency publishes.

Utilities, Erosion Control, and Agency Coordination

Wet utility design covers domestic water and fire service sizing, sanitary sewer design with slope and manhole spacing per the district's standards, and points of connection negotiated with the serving utility. We prepare SWPPP documentation and erosion-control plans for construction-phase compliance, and we support entitlement packages with the exhibits — preliminary grading, utility, and drainage plans — that planning commissions ask for.

Most importantly, we do the unglamorous work of reading each agency's design manual before we draw. A site plan engineered to the reviewing agency's own published standards, with its own checklist answered in order, is the difference between one review cycle and four.

How Civil Design Coordinates with the Building

Civil engineering fails quietly when it is designed in a vacuum. A finished floor elevation set without checking the sewer invert forces a grinder pump that nobody budgeted. A transformer pad located for the utility's convenience lands in the fire lane. A beautifully balanced grading plan puts the accessible route at 5.2 percent and fails inspection by two-tenths of a degree. Because our civil engineers work alongside the structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing teams on the same projects, these cross-discipline collisions are caught in design — the building and the site are engineered as one system.

The workflow starts with survey and geotechnical data: we review the topographic survey for completeness before design begins, flag missing utility inverts or boundary ambiguities early, and reconcile the soils report's infiltration rates and pavement sections with what the stormwater and paving designs actually assume. Then design proceeds in the order approvals require — conceptual grading for entitlement exhibits, preliminary engineering for agency pre-application meetings, and final construction documents once the path to approval is confirmed. Sequencing it this way means you never pay for final engineering on a layout the planning commission is about to change.

Deliverables are packaged for the specific reviewing agencies involved, which on one project can include the city's engineering department, the regional flood control district, the water and sewer purveyors, the fire marshal, and the state environmental agency for construction stormwater permits. Each gets its submittal in its own required format, and we track each review cycle to closure. During construction we review contractor submittals for pipe and pavement materials, respond to field conflicts — the unmarked utility that every site eventually finds — and prepare the record drawings and engineer's certifications most jurisdictions require before they release occupancy or bonds.

Earthwork economics deserve their own mention, because dirt is often the largest hidden line item on a site budget. A grading design that exports ten thousand yards of soil in a market where haul-off runs twenty dollars a yard has quietly spent two hundred thousand dollars that a balanced design would have kept. We optimize cut and fill iteratively against the constraints that actually govern — finished floor relationships, ADA slopes, drainage patterns, and retaining wall costs — and we present the tradeoffs in dollars, not just contours. When a retaining wall is cheaper than the fill it avoids, or a slightly higher pad elevation eliminates an import, the grading plan should say so, and ours does. The same cost lens applies to pavement sections, pipe materials, and basin geometry — every one of them has a cheaper alternative that still meets the standard, and finding it is part of the engineering.

Civil Engineering FAQs

Do you work in my city or county?
With PE licensure in 49 states, almost certainly. Civil review is intensely local, so the first thing we do is pull the reviewing agency's design standards and drainage manual — then engineer to them from the first sheet.
What's the difference between retention and detention?
Retention keeps stormwater on site permanently (infiltration or evaporation); detention stores it temporarily and releases it at a controlled rate. Which one you must provide — and for what storm event — is set by the local jurisdiction, and it materially affects how much land your site design loses.
Can you fix a site that drains toward the building?
Yes. We survey existing grades, compute where the water actually goes, and design regrading, area drains, or storm piping to correct it — with a drainage analysis the jurisdiction will accept if permits are required.
Do you handle ADA site compliance?
Yes — accessible parking counts and dimensions, running and cross slopes on every accessible route, ramp and landing geometry, and detectable warnings. ADA site citations are among the most common and most expensive to fix after paving, so we verify them at design.
What does a feasibility study include?
Conceptual grading with cut/fill estimate, utility availability and probable points of connection, preliminary stormwater sizing, and a summary of the entitlements and permits the jurisdiction will require — delivered before you close on the land.
