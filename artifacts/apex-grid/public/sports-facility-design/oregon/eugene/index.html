The Eugene venue market

TrackTown USA: the University of Oregon's Hayward Field sets the national bar, and Eugene-area school bonds fund venues to match.

Oregon school districts fund athletic facilities through voter-approved bonds, with the Portland metro running large programs — new high schools arrive with stadiums, fieldhouses, and pools on fixed timelines. The University of Oregon's track-and-field heritage (Hayward Field) sets a national standard for the state's venue ambitions, and municipal recreation districts operate deep community-center portfolios.

Electrical design for sports lighting systems

Behind every well-lit field is an electrical design that most spectators never think about: service sizing for lighting loads that can exceed the rest of the building combined, voltage-drop calculations on long pole runs, surge and lightning protection (outdoor sports lighting is a lightning rod by design), and controls that let a parks department run practice lighting at 30% and game lighting at full output from a phone.

We coordinate the lighting design with the structural scope — pole foundations, catwalk and rigging loads for arena systems — and with the utility on service upgrades, because a four-pole LED stadium system can trigger a service-size conversation the owner didn't expect. For broadcast-capable venues, we add the power and dimming infrastructure television requires: flicker-free drivers, vertical-illuminance uniformity, and spare capacity for temporary broadcast compounds.

Stormwater and site work for tournament-grade complexes

Multi-field tournament complexes live or die on drainage: a weekend tournament can't survive fields that hold water, and the local stormwater authority won't approve a site that pushes its problem downstream. We design comprehensive stormwater systems — detention, water-quality treatment, and conveyance sized for the jurisdiction's design storms — integrated with the field grading so the complex drains as a system.

The civil scope extends to access and circulation: tournament traffic patterns, bus and team-vehicle staging, pedestrian separation from vehicles, and lighting and utility corridors that serve future phases. Phased complexes need a civil master plan on day one, so phase three doesn't require tearing up phase one.

Permitting assembly venues with the AHJ

Permitting a sports venue means permitting the hardest building type in the code: high occupant loads, long spans, complex MEP, and public assembly — every reviewer looks closely. We build the permit package around a clear code narrative: occupancy classifications, construction type, occupant-load and egress diagrams, structural criteria, and MEP compliance paths, organized the way plan reviewers actually work.

For school-district and municipal projects, we add the jurisdiction's own layer — state school-facility review where it applies, local amendments, and the bond-funded schedule that makes review-cycle time a project risk. A permit package engineered for the reviewer's desk clears faster than one engineered for the design team's convenience.

Arena and fieldhouse HVAC: zoning for 200 to 20,000 people

An arena's HVAC system must be comfortable for a Tuesday-morning maintenance crew of six and a sold-out Friday night of 12,000 — a 2,000-to-1 occupancy swing that no single-zone system can serve. We zone arenas by function and load profile: the bowl or court volume on high-capacity variable-air-volume or displacement systems, concourses and clubs on their own zones, locker and training rooms on 100%-exhaust-capable systems, and back-of-house on simple packaged equipment.

ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation rates for assembly occupancies set the outdoor-air baseline, but the design art is in turndown: variable-speed fans, demand-controlled ventilation keyed to CO2 and occupancy schedules, and controls that pre-condition the bowl before events and set back aggressively between them. For fieldhouses with indoor turf, ventilation must also manage heat and any off-gassing load from synthetic surfaces.

Fieldhouse and indoor-turf engineering focus

Fieldhouse engineering is about the big volume done right: clear-span structure with no columns in the drill space, destratified high-bay HVAC that keeps the playing surface comfortable without baking the ceiling, ventilation rates for tournament occupancy, and practice-tier sports lighting with controls the staff will actually use. The envelope gets vapor detailing so shoulder seasons don't become condensation seasons.

Ice-rink engineering focus

Where ice is on the program, the building is designed around the refrigeration plant: an insulated slab with embedded piping holding the sheet near 24°F, subsoil heating against frost heave, dehumidification that keeps hall dew points low enough to protect the ice, and waste-heat recovery that turns the plant's rejected heat into snow-melt, hot water, and space heating.

Frequently asked questions

Does Apex design natatoriums to ASHRAE standards?
Yes. ASHRAE 62.1 sets the ventilation baseline; we size dehumidification to the pool-water evaporation load, hold the hall at 50–60% relative humidity, and detail corrosion-resistant envelopes so the building lasts. Our natatorium dehumidification guide covers the engineering.
What civil work does a tournament sports complex need?
Laser-graded fields with underdrain systems, stormwater detention and treatment per local standards, surge-capable parking and circulation for peak crowds, and phased utility planning so future expansion doesn't tear up phase one. Multi-field complexes are civil-engineering projects with buildings on them.
Can an existing gym be converted to a fieldhouse or practice facility?
Often — with structural verification of the long-span system for new loads, HVAC upgrades for the higher occupancy and exertion levels, and code review of the changed use and occupant load. A feasibility assessment up front prevents the conversion from becoming a reconstruction.
Do you handle the permit process with the local AHJ?
Yes. We build permit packages around a clear code narrative — occupancy, egress, structural criteria, MEP compliance paths — organized the way plan reviewers actually work, plus phased submittals where the jurisdiction allows. For school-district work we add state school-facility review coordination where it applies.
