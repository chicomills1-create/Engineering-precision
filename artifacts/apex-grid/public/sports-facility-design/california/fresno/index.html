Lighting controls: the system behind the fixtures

The fixtures get the attention, but the control system determines whether the venue's lighting is an asset or a complaint line. We design zone-based controls with presets the operator actually uses — practice, game, broadcast, event, and security — plus astronomical time-clock scheduling, remote access for the parks or athletics director, and auto-shutoff that kills the "lights left on all night" utility surprise.

For broadcast-capable venues, the controls add flicker-free dimming curves and DMX integration for event production; for community venues, simplicity wins — a wall station and a phone app, not a lighting console that needs a technician. Every design includes the spill-light documentation the permit and the neighbors require.

Grading and earthwork for stadium sites

Stadium and arena sites move serious dirt: balancing cut and fill across a bowl excavation, retaining systems where the site is tight, and subgrade preparation that gives the structure a uniform bearing surface. We design the earthwork with the geotechnical report open — over-excavating and replacing problem soils, chemically stabilizing expansive clay, and dewatering plans that keep the excavation workable through the wet season.

For hillside or constrained urban sites, the civil design integrates retaining walls, terraced grading, and construction access that doesn't shut down the surrounding streets. The goal is a site that drains away from the building forever, not just on opening day.

Temporary event structures and venue overlays

Tournaments, graduations, and concerts bring temporary structures — stages, tents, bleachers, broadcast scaffolding — that need their own engineering: anchorage and ballast for wind, egress through the overlay, and coordination with the permanent venue's life-safety systems. We engineer overlays to the code's temporary-structure provisions with the same rigor as permanent work, because the crowd doesn't know the difference.

The permanent venue design can make overlays easy or miserable: structural hard points for stages, power positions for production, and egress paths that stay clear in every configuration. Designing the venue for its overlays is what separates buildings that attract events from buildings that merely survive them.

Foundations for heavy, open structures

Arenas and fieldhouses concentrate enormous loads on relatively few foundation elements — long-span columns, seating-bowl risers, and natatorium basins all punish weak soils. The geotechnical investigation comes first and the foundation system follows the ground: spread footings where bearing is good, deep foundations where it's not, and specialized attention where the site brings karst, expansive clay, or high water tables.

We coordinate foundation design with the civil scope — site grading, dewatering during construction, and long-term drainage that keeps water away from the structure — because the most common long-term venue problems (settlement, slab distress, below-grade moisture) are foundation and drainage problems, not superstructure problems. For natatoriums, the pool vessel itself is a structural and waterproofing discipline of its own.

Outdoor stadium support-buildings focus

The stadium's support buildings — fieldhouse, concessions, restrooms, ticket and operations — carry the venue's daily engineering load while the bowl sits empty most days. We design these as durable, efficient buildings in their own right: surge-capable plumbing for game-day peaks, concession MEP with proper exhaust and grease management, and HVAC zoned for event-day versus everyday use. The support buildings are where operating budgets are won or lost.

Press box and premium-seating MEP focus

Press boxes, clubs, and suites are a different building hiding inside the venue: comfort-cooled to tighter tolerances, wired for broadcast and data, with restrooms and concessions at premium ratios. The MEP design zones these spaces independently from the bowl — they operate on different schedules with different expectations — and coordinates the structure for the press-box loads cantilevered over the seating below.

Frequently asked questions

How long does sports-facility engineering take?
A fieldhouse or competition gym typically runs 6–10 months for full construction documents on a fast track; natatoriums, ice rinks, and arenas run longer. Bond-funded owners should expect early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes — overlapping phases compress the calendar without cutting scope.
What does it cost to engineer a sports facility?
Engineering fees typically run 8–14% of construction cost for full A/E services, with natatoriums, arenas, and ice rinks at the high end of the range and simpler fieldhouse shells at the low end. The honest pricing is by scope — disciplines, deliverables, and review cycles — not a flat percentage. Our sports facility engineering cost guide breaks it down.
How do you keep a bond-funded venue on its election timeline?
By compressing dead time, not engineering: early procurement packages for long-lead equipment (dehumidification units, sports lighting, scoreboard steel), phased permit submittals that keep the AHJ reviewing while design advances, and a single point of accountability across structural, MEP, and civil so decisions never wait on inter-firm coordination.
What IES lighting level does a Fresno field or court need?
It depends on the level of play: recreational ≈30 footcandles, high school ≈50–75, collegiate ≈75–100+, broadcast ≈125–200+ maintained horizontal — each verified with a photometric model, with uniformity and glare limits per IES guidance. Choosing the right tier on day one matters: upgrading later is expensive.
