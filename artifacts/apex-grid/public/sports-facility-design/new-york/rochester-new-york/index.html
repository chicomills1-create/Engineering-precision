The Rochester venue market

New York school districts fund athletic facilities through voter-approved bonds — the downstate suburbs run some of the nation's largest programs — while New York City's School Construction Authority delivers gyms and pools at unmatched municipal scale. Upstate districts build fieldhouses and natatoriums on fixed bond timelines, and the state's hockey and lacrosse cultures sustain premium rink and turf-facility investment.

Electrical design for sports lighting systems

Behind every well-lit field is an electrical design that most spectators never think about: service sizing for lighting loads that can exceed the rest of the building combined, voltage-drop calculations on long pole runs, surge and lightning protection (outdoor sports lighting is a lightning rod by design), and controls that let a parks department run practice lighting at 30% and game lighting at full output from a phone.

We coordinate the lighting design with the structural scope — pole foundations, catwalk and rigging loads for arena systems — and with the utility on service upgrades, because a four-pole LED stadium system can trigger a service-size conversation the owner didn't expect. For broadcast-capable venues, we add the power and dimming infrastructure television requires: flicker-free drivers, vertical-illuminance uniformity, and spare capacity for temporary broadcast compounds.

Stormwater and site work for tournament-grade complexes

Multi-field tournament complexes live or die on drainage: a weekend tournament can't survive fields that hold water, and the local stormwater authority won't approve a site that pushes its problem downstream. We design comprehensive stormwater systems — detention, water-quality treatment, and conveyance sized for the jurisdiction's design storms — integrated with the field grading so the complex drains as a system.

The civil scope extends to access and circulation: tournament traffic patterns, bus and team-vehicle staging, pedestrian separation from vehicles, and lighting and utility corridors that serve future phases. Phased complexes need a civil master plan on day one, so phase three doesn't require tearing up phase one.

Temporary event structures and venue overlays

Tournaments, graduations, and concerts bring temporary structures — stages, tents, bleachers, broadcast scaffolding — that need their own engineering: anchorage and ballast for wind, egress through the overlay, and coordination with the permanent venue's life-safety systems. We engineer overlays to the code's temporary-structure provisions with the same rigor as permanent work, because the crowd doesn't know the difference.

The permanent venue design can make overlays easy or miserable: structural hard points for stages, power positions for production, and egress paths that stay clear in every configuration. Designing the venue for its overlays is what separates buildings that attract events from buildings that merely survive them.

Why pool buildings fail — and how we prevent it

Failed natatoriums almost always fail the same way: chronic high humidity condenses inside the envelope, corroding steel, rotting wood, and growing mold behind finishes the public never sees until the repair bill arrives. The prevention is a three-part discipline: size dehumidification to the true evaporation load (not a rule of thumb), keep supply air washing the coldest surfaces — glazing and exterior walls — and detail an airtight, vapor-managed envelope with corrosion-resistant structure.

We design pool halls to ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation minimums with dehumidification capacity matched to water temperature, agitation level (a calm lap pool and a play-feature pool are different buildings, thermodynamically), and spectator loads. Controls sequences matter: night setback must never let humidity climb into the condensing range, and purge cycles handle super-chloramine events without dumping the building's energy budget.

Esports and multi-use arena flexibility focus

The modern venue program increasingly includes esports and convertible event space alongside traditional sports — which changes the engineering: dense power and data infrastructure at floor level, theatrical lighting and production power to complement sports lighting, and HVAC zoning flexible enough for a 500-person tournament or a 5,000-person concert. Designing the floor boxes, rigging capacity, and acoustic separation for conversion from day one costs little and future-proofs the building's revenue.

Outdoor stadium support-buildings focus

The stadium's support buildings — fieldhouse, concessions, restrooms, ticket and operations — carry the venue's daily engineering load while the bowl sits empty most days. We design these as durable, efficient buildings in their own right: surge-capable plumbing for game-day peaks, concession MEP with proper exhaust and grease management, and HVAC zoned for event-day versus everyday use. The support buildings are where operating budgets are won or lost.

Frequently asked questions

How does New York's climate shape sports venue design?
Every venue we engineer starts with the site's actual conditions — the climate, soils, and hazard profile described on this page — rather than a template from another state. Humidity, snow, wind, seismic, and flood exposure each change equipment sizing, structural systems, and envelope detailing, and designing for the real site is what keeps operating costs and maintenance where the owner budgeted them.
Can Apex stamp sports-facility drawings in New York?
Apex Grid Engineering is licensed in 49 states — every U.S. state except Alaska — and engineering stamping and licensure are confirmed for each project jurisdiction. Public owners get permit-ready, PE-stamped construction documents organized for their AHJ's review process.
How long does sports-facility engineering take?
A fieldhouse or competition gym typically runs 6–10 months for full construction documents on a fast track; natatoriums, ice rinks, and arenas run longer. Bond-funded owners should expect early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes — overlapping phases compress the calendar without cutting scope.
What does it cost to engineer a sports facility?
Engineering fees typically run 8–14% of construction cost for full A/E services, with natatoriums, arenas, and ice rinks at the high end of the range and simpler fieldhouse shells at the low end. The honest pricing is by scope — disciplines, deliverables, and review cycles — not a flat percentage. Our sports facility engineering cost guide breaks it down.
