The Missouri sports-facility market

Missouri school districts fund athletic facilities through voter-approved bonds, with the St. Louis and Kansas City suburbs running large programs — new high schools arrive with stadiums, fieldhouses, and often pools. Municipal parks departments operate deep recreation and aquatics portfolios, and the state's wrestling and basketball traditions keep competition-venue standards high.

Tornado plus seismic in the same structure, karst-aware foundations, and humidity-driven MEP — Missouri school venues carry a uniquely stacked hazard list.

Pool-hall HVAC: the evaporation load rules

Ask what sizes a natatorium air handler and the answer is almost never the sensible cooling load — it's the latent load from the water surface. Competition pools held near 80°F evaporate hundreds of pounds of water per hour into the hall; therapy and leisure pools run warmer and evaporate more. ASHRAE's pool evaporation guidance, combined with 62.1 ventilation rates, sets the dehumidification capacity, and the system must hold relative humidity in the 50–60% band to protect both swimmers and the building.

Envelope detailing matters as much as equipment: warm, moist pool air will find any cold surface, so we coordinate vapor-retarder placement, insulated deck edges, and non-corroding structural systems with the architect before equipment is ever selected. Heat recovery from the dehumidification process preheats pool water and domestic hot water — in a well-designed natatorium, the "waste" heat is a feature, not a loss.

Ventilating the big volume: arenas, gyms, and fieldhouses

Large-volume sports spaces punish lazy air distribution. Throw conditioned air from 40 feet up without a plan and it stratifies — hot at the ceiling, cold at the court — wasting energy and comfort simultaneously. We design arena air distribution around the event: high-volume, low-velocity displacement or carefully aimed jet-nozzle systems that deliver air to the occupied zone (the first 10–15 feet) without blasting athletes or drifting across competition surfaces.

Occupancy diversity drives equipment selection. A fieldhouse hosting youth tournaments all weekend needs sustained ventilation and cooling; the same building empty on a Tuesday needs to sip energy. Variable-refrigerant or modular chiller plants, heat-recovery ventilation, and CO2-based demand control let the building scale its energy use to its actual occupancy — which is the difference between a venue the owner can afford to operate and one that becomes a budget problem.

Designing the big roof: snow, wind, and seismic

Arena and fieldhouse roofs are where structural engineering earns its fee. Snow drift against parapets, rooftop units, and the high side of pitched long-span roofs creates concentrated loads that rule-of-thumb design misses; wind uplift on a 200-foot clear span with a lightweight deck demands careful pressure-zone analysis; and in seismic regions, the diaphragm action of a giant open roof must be resolved into a lateral system that doesn't compromise the column-free interior.

Our structural scope includes drift and unbalanced snow cases per ASCE 7, wind analysis for the specific exposure and topography of the site, and — where the venue hosts suspended scoreboards, lighting grids, or rigging — the concentrated and dynamic loads those systems impose. The roof structure, the MEP it carries, and the lateral system are designed as one system, not three.

Bond-funded timelines: engineered for the election calendar

School-district and municipal sports facilities are funded by elections — and elections set immovable dates. When a bond passes in November, the community expects a fieldhouse opening before the next school year, and every month of design delay is a month the contractor can't buy back. We run bond-funded venue projects on compressed, overlapping schedules: early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes, permit submittals organized for the fastest review path, and decisions sequenced so the critical path never waits on a committee.

Speed without quality is just rework on a deadline, so the fast track is built on coordination discipline: clash-resolved models before permit, equipment selections locked before structural finalization, and a single point of engineering accountability across structural, MEP, and civil. Municipal and district owners get one team, one schedule, and permit-ready documents — because the ribbon-cutting date was set at the ballot box.

Sports lighting: IES levels, glare control, and broadcast readiness

Sports lighting is designed to maintained illuminance targets that scale with the level of play — IES guidance distinguishes recreational, high-school, collegiate, and professional/broadcast tiers, with broadcast venues requiring both higher horizontal levels and strict vertical illuminance for cameras. For a municipal fieldhouse or school stadium, the design decision that matters most is choosing the right tier on day one: under-lighting a venue that later hosts televised events is an expensive retrofit.

Modern LED systems with precise optical control have replaced the old musco-style floodlight glare problem — but only when they're aimed and shielded correctly. We design to IES glare and spill-light limits, coordinate pole or catwalk structural loads with the structural engineer, and size the electrical service for the inrush and control infrastructure that LED drivers and DMX/broadcast-dimming systems require.

Frequently asked questions

Who governs school athletics in Missouri?
The Missouri State High School Activities Association (MSHSAA) sanctions interscholastic athletics statewide — the body whose tournament infrastructure and facility expectations shape what Missouri school districts build.
What engineering disciplines does a Missouri sports facility need?
A complete venue needs structural (long-span roofs, grandstands, pool vessels), mechanical (arena HVAC, natatorium dehumidification, ice-rink refrigeration), electrical (sports lighting to IES levels, event and broadcast power), plumbing (surge-capable fixtures, aquatic plants), and civil (grading, stormwater, surge parking) — coordinated under one accountable team so the seams between disciplines never become change orders.
How does Missouri's climate shape sports venue design?
Every venue we engineer starts with the site's actual conditions — the climate, soils, and hazard profile described on this page — rather than a template from another state. Humidity, snow, wind, seismic, and flood exposure each change equipment sizing, structural systems, and envelope detailing, and designing for the real site is what keeps operating costs and maintenance where the owner budgeted them.
Can Apex stamp sports-facility drawings in Missouri?
Apex Grid Engineering is licensed in 49 states — every U.S. state except Alaska — and engineering stamping and licensure are confirmed for each project jurisdiction. Public owners get permit-ready, PE-stamped construction documents organized for their AHJ's review process.
How long does sports-facility engineering take?
A fieldhouse or competition gym typically runs 6–10 months for full construction documents on a fast track; natatoriums, ice rinks, and arenas run longer. Bond-funded owners should expect early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes — overlapping phases compress the calendar without cutting scope.
What does it cost to engineer a sports facility?
Engineering fees typically run 8–14% of construction cost for full A/E services, with natatoriums, arenas, and ice rinks at the high end of the range and simpler fieldhouse shells at the low end. The honest pricing is by scope — disciplines, deliverables, and review cycles — not a flat percentage. Our sports facility engineering cost guide breaks it down.
