Why pool buildings fail — and how we prevent it

Failed natatoriums almost always fail the same way: chronic high humidity condenses inside the envelope, corroding steel, rotting wood, and growing mold behind finishes the public never sees until the repair bill arrives. The prevention is a three-part discipline: size dehumidification to the true evaporation load (not a rule of thumb), keep supply air washing the coldest surfaces — glazing and exterior walls — and detail an airtight, vapor-managed envelope with corrosion-resistant structure.

We design pool halls to ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation minimums with dehumidification capacity matched to water temperature, agitation level (a calm lap pool and a play-feature pool are different buildings, thermodynamically), and spectator loads. Controls sequences matter: night setback must never let humidity climb into the condensing range, and purge cycles handle super-chloramine events without dumping the building's energy budget.

Indoor air quality where athletes breathe hardest

Athletes at full exertion move far more air than spectators, so indoor sports venues deserve stricter air-quality thinking than the code minimums for assembly space. We design fieldhouse and arena ventilation to ASHRAE 62.1 rates as the floor, then add MERV-13+ filtration, CO2 monitoring with demand-controlled ventilation, and — for facilities near wildfire-smoke regions or urban corridors — smoke and particulate response sequences that protect a tournament weekend from a bad-air day.

For indoor turf facilities, ventilation design also addresses heat buildup under the roof and any material off-gassing: higher air-change rates during peak use, exhaust at the high points where heat stratifies, and controls that ramp with scheduled occupancy. The result is a building where the air never becomes the story — which, for a competitive venue, is exactly the point.

Foundations for heavy, open structures

Arenas and fieldhouses concentrate enormous loads on relatively few foundation elements — long-span columns, seating-bowl risers, and natatorium basins all punish weak soils. The geotechnical investigation comes first and the foundation system follows the ground: spread footings where bearing is good, deep foundations where it's not, and specialized attention where the site brings karst, expansive clay, or high water tables.

We coordinate foundation design with the civil scope — site grading, dewatering during construction, and long-term drainage that keeps water away from the structure — because the most common long-term venue problems (settlement, slab distress, below-grade moisture) are foundation and drainage problems, not superstructure problems. For natatoriums, the pool vessel itself is a structural and waterproofing discipline of its own.

One engineering team across structural, MEP, and civil

Sports venues fail at the seams between disciplines: the structural engineer who didn't know the dehumidification unit's weight, the civil engineer who graded the site before the building footprint was final, the electrical engineer who learned about the broadcast compound at the 90% review. We deliver venues with structural, MEP, and civil engineering under one roof and one project manager, so the seams are designed — not discovered.

For bond-funded and municipal owners, single-team accountability means faster decisions, fewer RFIs, and a permit package where every discipline already agrees with every other. The venue opens on the date the voters were promised, and the engineering is invisible — which is exactly how it should be.

Lighting controls: the system behind the fixtures

The fixtures get the attention, but the control system determines whether the venue's lighting is an asset or a complaint line. We design zone-based controls with presets the operator actually uses — practice, game, broadcast, event, and security — plus astronomical time-clock scheduling, remote access for the parks or athletics director, and auto-shutoff that kills the "lights left on all night" utility surprise.

For broadcast-capable venues, the controls add flicker-free dimming curves and DMX integration for event production; for community venues, simplicity wins — a wall station and a phone app, not a lighting console that needs a technician. Every design includes the spill-light documentation the permit and the neighbors require.

Frequently asked questions

Who governs school athletics in Montana?
The Montana High School Association (MHSA) sanctions interscholastic athletics statewide — the body whose tournament infrastructure and facility expectations shape what Montana school districts build.
What civil work does a tournament sports complex need?
Laser-graded fields with underdrain systems, stormwater detention and treatment per local standards, surge-capable parking and circulation for peak crowds, and phased utility planning so future expansion doesn't tear up phase one. Multi-field complexes are civil-engineering projects with buildings on them.
Can an existing gym be converted to a fieldhouse or practice facility?
Often — with structural verification of the long-span system for new loads, HVAC upgrades for the higher occupancy and exertion levels, and code review of the changed use and occupant load. A feasibility assessment up front prevents the conversion from becoming a reconstruction.
Do you handle the permit process with the local AHJ?
Yes. We build permit packages around a clear code narrative — occupancy, egress, structural criteria, MEP compliance paths — organized the way plan reviewers actually work, plus phased submittals where the jurisdiction allows. For school-district work we add state school-facility review coordination where it applies.
What makes Apex different for sports venues?
Single-team accountability across structural, MEP, and civil; real technical depth in the hard venue systems (natatorium dehumidification, long-span structures, sports lighting, ice-rink refrigeration); and delivery built for bond-funded timelines — fast-track phasing, early procurement, and permit-ready documents.
What engineering disciplines does a Montana sports facility need?
A complete venue needs structural (long-span roofs, grandstands, pool vessels), mechanical (arena HVAC, natatorium dehumidification, ice-rink refrigeration), electrical (sports lighting to IES levels, event and broadcast power), plumbing (surge-capable fixtures, aquatic plants), and civil (grading, stormwater, surge parking) — coordinated under one accountable team so the seams between disciplines never become change orders.
