Sports lighting: IES levels, glare control, and broadcast readiness

Sports lighting is designed to maintained illuminance targets that scale with the level of play — IES guidance distinguishes recreational, high-school, collegiate, and professional/broadcast tiers, with broadcast venues requiring both higher horizontal levels and strict vertical illuminance for cameras. For a municipal fieldhouse or school stadium, the design decision that matters most is choosing the right tier on day one: under-lighting a venue that later hosts televised events is an expensive retrofit.

Modern LED systems with precise optical control have replaced the old musco-style floodlight glare problem — but only when they're aimed and shielded correctly. We design to IES glare and spill-light limits, coordinate pole or catwalk structural loads with the structural engineer, and size the electrical service for the inrush and control infrastructure that LED drivers and DMX/broadcast-dimming systems require.

Civil engineering for sports complexes: grading, drainage, and fields

Sports complexes are civil-engineering projects with buildings on them: acres of graded fields, miles of stormwater management, and parking and circulation designed for surge crowds. We design the site grading so fields drain to playable conditions within hours of a storm — laser-graded surfaces, underdrain systems, and stormwater detention sized to the local jurisdiction's standards.

For synthetic-turf fields, the civil scope includes the permeable or drained base section, edge detailing that contains infill, and stormwater quality treatment for the runoff the field generates. Parking and bus circulation get the same surge thinking as the building egress: a 5,000-seat venue emptying at once is a traffic event, and the civil design must move it safely.

Fire protection coordination for venues

Sprinkler, fire alarm, and smoke-control design for assembly venues is a coordination exercise across every discipline: sprinkler zoning that respects the long-span structure and the seating-bowl geometry, fire-alarm notification that reaches every occupied space including the bowl at full crowd noise, and smoke-control sequences tied to the HVAC design for smoke-protected seating.

We coordinate the fire-protection narrative with the code strategy from schematic design — because the AHJ's fire reviewer will ask the hardest questions on the project, and the answers need to be in the documents, not in a meeting.

Indoor turf and fieldhouse ventilation

Indoor turf buildings are some of the hardest-working HVAC loads in sports design: a large clear-span volume, high occupant densities during tournaments, solar gain through any daylighting, and heat that stratifies at the ceiling while players compete at the surface. Ventilation rates follow ASHRAE 62.1 for the occupancy, but the design must also manage temperature uniformity across a 200-foot field and purge the building effectively between events.

We zone fieldhouses for their actual use patterns — full-field tournaments, half-field practices, and empty weekdays — with variable-speed air handling and demand-controlled ventilation that scales energy use to occupancy. High-bay destratification, either through the HVAC design or supplemental fans, keeps the playing surface from sitting in a cold pool while the ceiling bakes.

University-adjacent athletics focus

In college towns, school-district and municipal venues are judged against the university standard down the road. That means competition-grade gyms with proper humidity control for wood floors, natatoriums held to collegiate dehumidification practice, stadium lighting verified photometrically rather than assumed, and practice facilities engineered for the daily-use abuse that varsity programs dish out.

Private sports-performance focus

Private training, tournament, and sports-performance facilities answer to a pro forma, not a bond: the engineering prioritizes speed to revenue (fast-track permitting and construction), operating-cost efficiency (energy being the controllable expense), and flexibility (clear spans and utility capacity that let the program evolve). Broadcast-ready power and premium acoustic and lighting quality differentiate the facilities that command premium pricing.

Frequently asked questions

What IES lighting level does a Tulsa field or court need?
It depends on the level of play: recreational ≈30 footcandles, high school ≈50–75, collegiate ≈75–100+, broadcast ≈125–200+ maintained horizontal — each verified with a photometric model, with uniformity and glare limits per IES guidance. Choosing the right tier on day one matters: upgrading later is expensive.
Does Apex design natatoriums to ASHRAE standards?
Yes. ASHRAE 62.1 sets the ventilation baseline; we size dehumidification to the pool-water evaporation load, hold the hall at 50–60% relative humidity, and detail corrosion-resistant envelopes so the building lasts. Our natatorium dehumidification guide covers the engineering.
What civil work does a tournament sports complex need?
Laser-graded fields with underdrain systems, stormwater detention and treatment per local standards, surge-capable parking and circulation for peak crowds, and phased utility planning so future expansion doesn't tear up phase one. Multi-field complexes are civil-engineering projects with buildings on them.
Can an existing gym be converted to a fieldhouse or practice facility?
Often — with structural verification of the long-span system for new loads, HVAC upgrades for the higher occupancy and exertion levels, and code review of the changed use and occupant load. A feasibility assessment up front prevents the conversion from becoming a reconstruction.
