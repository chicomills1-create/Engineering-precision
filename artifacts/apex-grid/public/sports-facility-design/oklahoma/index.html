Therapy and leisure pools: the harder dehumidification case

Warm-water pools — therapy, leisure, play-feature — evaporate dramatically more moisture than a calm competition pool at 80°F, and they do it while bathers agitate the surface constantly. The dehumidification plant for a leisure-pool hall is routinely larger per square foot of water than for an Olympic pool, and the activity factor in the evaporation calculation must reflect the real use, not the calm-water default.

These halls also run warmer air temperatures for bather comfort, which raises the dew point and tightens the envelope detailing: vapor retarders, thermal breaks, and corrosion-resistant structure become even more critical. Undersize the dehumidification here and the building tells you within the first season.

Commissioning the sequences, not just the equipment

Arena HVAC fails most often in the controls sequences, not the equipment schedule: demand-controlled ventilation that never got calibrated, event pre-conditioning that nobody programmed, setback sequences that fight the dehumidification plant. We write sequences of operation around the venue's actual event calendar and commission them under simulated event loads — because a sequence verified at 2 p.m. on an empty Tuesday is a guess, not a verification.

The deliverable is a building the operator understands: graphics that show what's happening, trends that prove the sequences work, and training that survives staff turnover. The best-engineered arena still needs an operator who trusts the automation.

Seating-bowl structures and risers

Seating bowls are structures the public touches: precast or cast-in-place risers spanning between rakers, designed for the code's assembly live loads plus the dynamic reality of a crowd in motion. Vibration analysis keeps the bowl from feeling lively under rhythmic loading, and the detailing — riser connections, railing anchorage, vomitory framing — has to survive decades of crowd abuse.

The bowl structure coordinates with everything below and above: concourse framing, MEP distribution threaded through the rake, and the roof structure's support points. Tolerances matter — precast risers and long-span steel meet at connections that were detailed together or they don't meet at all.

Bond-funded timelines: engineered for the election calendar

School-district and municipal sports facilities are funded by elections — and elections set immovable dates. When a bond passes in November, the community expects a fieldhouse opening before the next school year, and every month of design delay is a month the contractor can't buy back. We run bond-funded venue projects on compressed, overlapping schedules: early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes, permit submittals organized for the fastest review path, and decisions sequenced so the critical path never waits on a committee.

Speed without quality is just rework on a deadline, so the fast track is built on coordination discipline: clash-resolved models before permit, equipment selections locked before structural finalization, and a single point of engineering accountability across structural, MEP, and civil. Municipal and district owners get one team, one schedule, and permit-ready documents — because the ribbon-cutting date was set at the ballot box.

Indoor court and fieldhouse lighting

Indoor sports lighting trades pole foundations for ceiling structure: fixtures mount to the long-span roof system or catwalks, which means the structural engineer needs fixture weights, mounting points, and maintenance access long before steel is detailed. IES targets step with the level of play — practice, high school competition, collegiate, broadcast — and uniformity matters more indoors, where the eye adapts to the room, not the sky.

Glare control is the indoor discipline: fixtures need shielding and aiming that keeps direct glare out of players' eyes on every court orientation, and the lighting must coordinate with any daylighting so the two systems don't fight. LED with zoned dimming lets one fieldhouse serve a youth practice and a championship final in the same day.

Frequently asked questions

Who governs school athletics in Oklahoma?
The Oklahoma Secondary School Activities Association (OSSAA) sanctions interscholastic athletics statewide — the body whose tournament infrastructure and facility expectations shape what Oklahoma school districts build.
What IES lighting level does a Oklahoma field or court need?
It depends on the level of play: recreational ≈30 footcandles, high school ≈50–75, collegiate ≈75–100+, broadcast ≈125–200+ maintained horizontal — each verified with a photometric model, with uniformity and glare limits per IES guidance. Choosing the right tier on day one matters: upgrading later is expensive.
Does Apex design natatoriums to ASHRAE standards?
Yes. ASHRAE 62.1 sets the ventilation baseline; we size dehumidification to the pool-water evaporation load, hold the hall at 50–60% relative humidity, and detail corrosion-resistant envelopes so the building lasts. Our natatorium dehumidification guide covers the engineering.
What civil work does a tournament sports complex need?
Laser-graded fields with underdrain systems, stormwater detention and treatment per local standards, surge-capable parking and circulation for peak crowds, and phased utility planning so future expansion doesn't tear up phase one. Multi-field complexes are civil-engineering projects with buildings on them.
Can an existing gym be converted to a fieldhouse or practice facility?
Often — with structural verification of the long-span system for new loads, HVAC upgrades for the higher occupancy and exertion levels, and code review of the changed use and occupant load. A feasibility assessment up front prevents the conversion from becoming a reconstruction.
Do you handle the permit process with the local AHJ?
Yes. We build permit packages around a clear code narrative — occupancy, egress, structural criteria, MEP compliance paths — organized the way plan reviewers actually work, plus phased submittals where the jurisdiction allows. For school-district work we add state school-facility review coordination where it applies.
