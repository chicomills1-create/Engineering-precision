Emergency and egress lighting for venues

When the house lights drop for player introductions, the egress lighting has to work — and when the power fails during a sellout, it has to work instantly. Venue emergency lighting design covers the bowl, concourses, stairs, and entries at code-required levels, with generator or battery backup that transfers fast enough that a crowd never notices the utility blink.

The design coordinates with the event operation: theatrical blackouts are planned, power failures are not, and the system must distinguish between them. Photometric verification of the egress paths — not just fixture counts — is what the AHJ's reviewer will ask for.

Utility coordination for venue sites

Arena-scale MEP loads need utility capacity to match: electrical service sized for sports lighting and event power, water service for pool fills and surge plumbing, sanitary capacity for halftime peaks, and gas or electrical infrastructure for the heating plants. We coordinate with each utility early — service upgrades and extensions carry lead times measured in months, and the transformer nobody ordered is the delay nobody budgeted.

The civil design routes it all: duct banks, utility corridors, and easements that serve the building and leave room for the future phases the master plan promises. Underground congestion is designed on paper, not discovered with a backhoe.

Occupant-load-driven plumbing and egress coordination

Occupant load is the number that drives everything in an assembly venue: exit counts and widths, plumbing fixture counts, and even the HVAC ventilation baseline. We calculate it once, rigorously, from the seating plans and standing areas — then every discipline designs to the same number. The venues that fail plan review fail it here, with three disciplines using three different occupant loads.

For multipurpose venues, the analysis covers every configuration: sports, concert, banquet, graduation. The code maximum and the operational reality both get documented, so the owner's event team knows the building's legal capacity in each setup.

Indoor air quality where athletes breathe hardest

Athletes at full exertion move far more air than spectators, so indoor sports venues deserve stricter air-quality thinking than the code minimums for assembly space. We design fieldhouse and arena ventilation to ASHRAE 62.1 rates as the floor, then add MERV-13+ filtration, CO2 monitoring with demand-controlled ventilation, and — for facilities near wildfire-smoke regions or urban corridors — smoke and particulate response sequences that protect a tournament weekend from a bad-air day.

For indoor turf facilities, ventilation design also addresses heat buildup under the roof and any material off-gassing: higher air-change rates during peak use, exhaust at the high points where heat stratifies, and controls that ramp with scheduled occupancy. The result is a building where the air never becomes the story — which, for a competitive venue, is exactly the point.

Fieldhouse and indoor-turf engineering focus

Fieldhouse engineering is about the big volume done right: clear-span structure with no columns in the drill space, destratified high-bay HVAC that keeps the playing surface comfortable without baking the ceiling, ventilation rates for tournament occupancy, and practice-tier sports lighting with controls the staff will actually use. The envelope gets vapor detailing so shoulder seasons don't become condensation seasons.

Ice-rink engineering focus

Where ice is on the program, the building is designed around the refrigeration plant: an insulated slab with embedded piping holding the sheet near 24°F, subsoil heating against frost heave, dehumidification that keeps hall dew points low enough to protect the ice, and waste-heat recovery that turns the plant's rejected heat into snow-melt, hot water, and space heating.

Frequently asked questions

What makes Apex different for sports venues?
Single-team accountability across structural, MEP, and civil; real technical depth in the hard venue systems (natatorium dehumidification, long-span structures, sports lighting, ice-rink refrigeration); and delivery built for bond-funded timelines — fast-track phasing, early procurement, and permit-ready documents.
What engineering disciplines does a Dickinson sports facility need?
A complete venue needs structural (long-span roofs, grandstands, pool vessels), mechanical (arena HVAC, natatorium dehumidification, ice-rink refrigeration), electrical (sports lighting to IES levels, event and broadcast power), plumbing (surge-capable fixtures, aquatic plants), and civil (grading, stormwater, surge parking) — coordinated under one accountable team so the seams between disciplines never become change orders.
How does North Dakota's climate shape sports venue design?
Every venue we engineer starts with the site's actual conditions — the climate, soils, and hazard profile described on this page — rather than a template from another state. Humidity, snow, wind, seismic, and flood exposure each change equipment sizing, structural systems, and envelope detailing, and designing for the real site is what keeps operating costs and maintenance where the owner budgeted them.
