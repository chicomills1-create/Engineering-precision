Natatorium dehumidification, engineered — not guessed

Indoor pool halls live or die on moisture control. ASHRAE Standard 62.1 sets the ventilation baseline, but the real design load is pool-water evaporation: for a typical competition pool the evaporation rate climbs with water temperature, air temperature, and — critically — air movement over the surface. A well-designed system holds the pool hall at 50–60% relative humidity and keeps the air temperature 2–4°F above water temperature so swimmers leaving the water don't chill.

We size dedicated outdoor-air/dehumidification units (or desiccant-assisted systems for large natatoriums) against the evaporation load plus occupant and envelope latent loads, then detail the building envelope — vapor retarders, thermal breaks, corrosion-resistant structure — so the moisture that the equipment removes doesn't condense inside walls. Spectator seating, which adds sensible load without adding moisture, gets its own zoning so a full house doesn't swing the space.

Arena and fieldhouse HVAC: zoning for 200 to 20,000 people

An arena's HVAC system must be comfortable for a Tuesday-morning maintenance crew of six and a sold-out Friday night of 12,000 — a 2,000-to-1 occupancy swing that no single-zone system can serve. We zone arenas by function and load profile: the bowl or court volume on high-capacity variable-air-volume or displacement systems, concourses and clubs on their own zones, locker and training rooms on 100%-exhaust-capable systems, and back-of-house on simple packaged equipment.

ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation rates for assembly occupancies set the outdoor-air baseline, but the design art is in turndown: variable-speed fans, demand-controlled ventilation keyed to CO2 and occupancy schedules, and controls that pre-condition the bowl before events and set back aggressively between them. For fieldhouses with indoor turf, ventilation must also manage heat and any off-gassing load from synthetic surfaces.

Long-span structural systems for arenas and fieldhouses

Clear spans of 150 to 300+ feet are the structural signature of arenas and fieldhouses — no columns in the sightlines, ever. The system choice depends on span, budget, and climate: steel trusses and space frames for the longest clear spans, long-span steel joists with arched or pitched profiles for fieldhouses, and cable or arch systems where the architecture demands it. Every option must resolve the same physics: gravity, wind uplift, snow drift against parapets and rooftop units, and — in seismic regions — the lateral system for a very large, very open box.

We design the roof structure together with the MEP scope it carries: catwalk and rigging loads for lighting and scoreboards, point loads for HVAC units and ductwork, and deflection limits that keep long-span members from telegraphing movement into glazing and finishes. Vibration matters too — a fieldhouse floor over occupied space and arena seating risers both need dynamic analysis so the building never feels lively under crowd movement.

Designing the decision schedule

Fast projects don't just need fast engineers — they need a decision schedule: every owner, architect, and vendor decision mapped to the date the critical path requires it. We build that schedule in schematic design, so the district committee choosing the scoreboard vendor in month two doesn't discover in month six that the steel was already ordered.

The decision schedule is what makes overlapping phases work: civil and structural released early, MEP finishing in parallel, equipment selections locked before they become redesign. On bond timelines, the calendar is managed as deliberately as the calculations.

Indoor court and fieldhouse lighting

Indoor sports lighting trades pole foundations for ceiling structure: fixtures mount to the long-span roof system or catwalks, which means the structural engineer needs fixture weights, mounting points, and maintenance access long before steel is detailed. IES targets step with the level of play — practice, high school competition, collegiate, broadcast — and uniformity matters more indoors, where the eye adapts to the room, not the sky.

Glare control is the indoor discipline: fixtures need shielding and aiming that keeps direct glare out of players' eyes on every court orientation, and the lighting must coordinate with any daylighting so the two systems don't fight. LED with zoned dimming lets one fieldhouse serve a youth practice and a championship final in the same day.

Frequently asked questions

Who governs school athletics in Arizona?
The Arizona Interscholastic Association (AIA) sanctions interscholastic athletics statewide — the body whose tournament infrastructure and facility expectations shape what Arizona school districts build.
How long does sports-facility engineering take?
A fieldhouse or competition gym typically runs 6–10 months for full construction documents on a fast track; natatoriums, ice rinks, and arenas run longer. Bond-funded owners should expect early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes — overlapping phases compress the calendar without cutting scope.
What does it cost to engineer a sports facility?
Engineering fees typically run 8–14% of construction cost for full A/E services, with natatoriums, arenas, and ice rinks at the high end of the range and simpler fieldhouse shells at the low end. The honest pricing is by scope — disciplines, deliverables, and review cycles — not a flat percentage. Our sports facility engineering cost guide breaks it down.
How do you keep a bond-funded venue on its election timeline?
By compressing dead time, not engineering: early procurement packages for long-lead equipment (dehumidification units, sports lighting, scoreboard steel), phased permit submittals that keep the AHJ reviewing while design advances, and a single point of accountability across structural, MEP, and civil so decisions never wait on inter-firm coordination.
What IES lighting level does a Arizona field or court need?
It depends on the level of play: recreational ≈30 footcandles, high school ≈50–75, collegiate ≈75–100+, broadcast ≈125–200+ maintained horizontal — each verified with a photometric model, with uniformity and glare limits per IES guidance. Choosing the right tier on day one matters: upgrading later is expensive.
Does Apex design natatoriums to ASHRAE standards?
Yes. ASHRAE 62.1 sets the ventilation baseline; we size dehumidification to the pool-water evaporation load, hold the hall at 50–60% relative humidity, and detail corrosion-resistant envelopes so the building lasts. Our natatorium dehumidification guide covers the engineering.
