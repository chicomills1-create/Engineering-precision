Retrofitting to LED: what the electrical engineer must verify

LED retrofits of legacy metal-halide sports lighting cut energy use dramatically — but only when the electrical and structural scope is engineered, not assumed. Existing poles must be verified for the new fixtures' weight and wind sail area; existing branch circuits must be checked against LED driver harmonics and inrush; and the control system must be designed for how the venue actually operates — practice, game, and broadcast presets with remote access.

We start every retrofit with a photometric model of the proposed system against IES targets for the venue's level of play, then engineer the electrical distribution, surge protection, and structural verification to match. The result is lighting that meets the standard on the field, controls spill light for the neighbors, and doesn't surprise the owner with a service upgrade mid-project.

Parking, circulation, and the surge crowd

A venue's civil design is tested in twenty minutes: the surge when an event lets out. We design parking capacity, internal circulation, pedestrian paths, and egress routes for the peak occupant load — not the average Tuesday — coordinated with the local traffic authority on signal timing and turn-lane improvements where the venue's traffic study requires them.

Accessible parking and paths, emergency-vehicle access that stays clear during sellouts, and bus and rideshare staging are designed into the site plan from the start. For school venues, the same circulation must handle daily drop-off and pickup without conflicting with the event plan — two traffic systems sharing one site, engineered to never meet.

Temporary event structures and venue overlays

Tournaments, graduations, and concerts bring temporary structures — stages, tents, bleachers, broadcast scaffolding — that need their own engineering: anchorage and ballast for wind, egress through the overlay, and coordination with the permanent venue's life-safety systems. We engineer overlays to the code's temporary-structure provisions with the same rigor as permanent work, because the crowd doesn't know the difference.

The permanent venue design can make overlays easy or miserable: structural hard points for stages, power positions for production, and egress paths that stay clear in every configuration. Designing the venue for its overlays is what separates buildings that attract events from buildings that merely survive them.

Year-round rink operations and energy

A community rink that operates year-round is an energy story: the refrigeration plant runs through summer, the dehumidification fights humid outdoor air, and the building still needs to be comfortable for spectators. The design answers with heat recovery at every opportunity — rink-plant waste heat to snow-melt pits, domestic hot water, and space heating — plus envelope and controls that minimize the loads the plant has to fight.

We model the annual energy profile honestly, so the municipality or operator sees the real operating budget before the bond vote — not after the first summer utility bill.

University-adjacent athletics focus

In college towns, school-district and municipal venues are judged against the university standard down the road. That means competition-grade gyms with proper humidity control for wood floors, natatoriums held to collegiate dehumidification practice, stadium lighting verified photometrically rather than assumed, and practice facilities engineered for the daily-use abuse that varsity programs dish out.

Private sports-performance focus

Private training, tournament, and sports-performance facilities answer to a pro forma, not a bond: the engineering prioritizes speed to revenue (fast-track permitting and construction), operating-cost efficiency (energy being the controllable expense), and flexibility (clear spans and utility capacity that let the program evolve). Broadcast-ready power and premium acoustic and lighting quality differentiate the facilities that command premium pricing.

Frequently asked questions

Can Apex stamp sports-facility drawings in Arkansas?
Apex Grid Engineering is licensed in 49 states — every U.S. state except Alaska — and engineering stamping and licensure are confirmed for each project jurisdiction. Public owners get permit-ready, PE-stamped construction documents organized for their AHJ's review process.
How long does sports-facility engineering take?
A fieldhouse or competition gym typically runs 6–10 months for full construction documents on a fast track; natatoriums, ice rinks, and arenas run longer. Bond-funded owners should expect early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes — overlapping phases compress the calendar without cutting scope.
What does it cost to engineer a sports facility?
Engineering fees typically run 8–14% of construction cost for full A/E services, with natatoriums, arenas, and ice rinks at the high end of the range and simpler fieldhouse shells at the low end. The honest pricing is by scope — disciplines, deliverables, and review cycles — not a flat percentage. Our sports facility engineering cost guide breaks it down.
How do you keep a bond-funded venue on its election timeline?
By compressing dead time, not engineering: early procurement packages for long-lead equipment (dehumidification units, sports lighting, scoreboard steel), phased permit submittals that keep the AHJ reviewing while design advances, and a single point of accountability across structural, MEP, and civil so decisions never wait on inter-firm coordination.
