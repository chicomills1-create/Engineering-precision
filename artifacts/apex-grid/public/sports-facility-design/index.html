Why sports venues are an engineering project

A sports facility is the most technically demanding building type most owners will ever commission. A natatorium's dehumidification plant is sized to the pool-water evaporation load, not to a rule of thumb. An arena's HVAC must serve six people on a Tuesday and 12,000 on a Friday night. A stadium's long-span roof carries scoreboards, lighting grids, and rigging on top of snow drift and wind uplift. The venues that work — and the ones owners can afford to operate — are the ones where structural, MEP, and civil engineering were designed as one system from schematic phase.

That's how Apex Grid Engineering delivers sports and recreation facilities: one accountable team across all three disciplines, real depth in the hard venue systems, and delivery built for the way these projects are actually funded — bond elections and municipal capital programs with immovable dates.

Engineered for bond-funded timelines

School-district and municipal sports facilities are funded by elections — and elections set immovable dates. We run bond-funded venue projects on compressed, overlapping schedules: early civil and structural packages released while MEP design finishes, long-lead equipment (dehumidification units, sports lighting, scoreboard steel) procured before CDs are complete, and permit submittals organized for the fastest review path. Speed without quality is rework on a deadline, so the fast track is built on coordination discipline — clash-resolved design, locked equipment selections, and one accountable team. The ribbon-cutting date was set at the ballot box; our job is to hit it.

The hard systems, done right

Natatorium dehumidification. Pool halls are sized by evaporation load, not rules of thumb: water temperature, air temperature, and surface agitation set the moisture the system must remove every hour. We hold halls at 50–60% RH with air a few degrees above water temperature, wash cold glazing with supply air, and detail vapor-managed, corrosion-resistant envelopes — because the pool buildings that fail all fail the same way, from the inside out.

Arena HVAC with real turndown. A 12,000-seat bowl and a six-person maintenance crew need the same building to behave like two different buildings. Zoned systems, displacement or aimed high-bay distribution, CO2-based demand-controlled ventilation, and event-schedule controls let the venue sip energy empty and perform full — the difference between a building the owner can afford and a budget problem with seats.

Long-span structures that carry the show. Clear spans of 150–300+ feet with no columns in the sightlines, designed per ASCE 7 for snow drift, wind uplift, and seismic — plus the concentrated, dynamic loads of scoreboards, lighting grids, and rigging that the roof was always going to carry. Foundations follow the geotechnical reality: karst, expansive clay, high water tables, or deep frost.

Sports lighting to the IES tier. Photometric models proving maintained illuminance, uniformity, and glare limits for the venue's level of play — 30 fc recreational to 150+ broadcast — with pole structural verification, surge and lightning protection, and controls the staff will actually use.

What it costs, honestly

Engineering fees for sports complexes typically run 8–14% of construction cost for full A/E services. The percentage follows technical intensity, not square footage: a pre-engineered fieldhouse shell sits near the bottom, a competition natatorium or broadcast-capable arena near the top. A $10M community recreation center usually carries $800K–$1.1M in total design fees; a $40M arena or stadium complex, $3.5M–$5M. The line item owners should watch isn't the percentage — it's scope clarity: disciplines, deliverables, review cycles, and a decision schedule tied to the bond calendar.
