Do Owner-Builders Need a PE Stamp for Their Permit?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Yes, when the AHJ requires sealed engineering — owner-builder status grants no exemption. Confirm the intake checklist's sealed-engineering requirements before applying, then engage a state-licensed PE for a genuine review-and-seal: review, verified calculations, revisions, then the seal.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Finding out what your project requires

The single most valuable hour an owner-builder spends is the pre-submittal conversation with the building department. Bring the site address, a description of the project, and whatever plans exist, and ask directly: what sealed engineering does this project require? The intake staff will walk through the checklist — structural, MEP, civil, soils report, energy compliance — and you will leave knowing the engineering scope instead of guessing. I also recommend asking about the jurisdiction's owner-builder requirements themselves (the permit application, the owner-builder disclosure forms, inspection scheduling), because acting as your own GC carries administrative obligations beyond the engineering. With the checklist in hand, the PE engagement can be scoped precisely. I ask owner-builders for three things at intake: the AHJ's requirements list, everything drawn so far (however rough), and the geotechnical report if one exists. From that I produce a written scope: what the review covers, what design development is needed to make the set reviewable, the deliverables (sealed drawings, calculations, energy forms), and the fee. No surprises, no "stamp fees" — a professional engagement with defined work. Owner-builders who do this step report the same thing: the engineering stopped feeling like a mysterious tax and started feeling like the part of the project that prevents disasters.

What the review looks like from the owner's side

The review process is collaborative, not adversarial — though it can feel otherwise the first time a PE marks up your plans. I review the structural design first: foundations against the soils report, framing against the loads, the lateral system for a complete load path, and every connection that the prescriptive code does not cover. The calculations get verified independently — I run the numbers rather than trusting them — and the revisions get marked specifically enough that whoever implements them (you, your designer, or me) knows exactly what changed and why. Then the MEP and civil scopes get the same treatment where they apply. Expect revision rounds; they are the review working, not the review failing. A clean owner-builder set typically goes through one to two revision cycles before sealing — more if the starting drawings were rough, fewer if they were professional. I review the corrections each round and seal when the design is one I will stand behind professionally. The sealed set that emerges is genuinely more buildable than what went in: coordinated, calculated, and detailed to a standard the inspectors recognize. Owner-builders consistently tell me the review paid for itself in the first month of construction, when the framing crew built straight from the plans without a single structural RFI.

Owner-builder PE checklist

Do these in order and the engineering phase stays predictable. • Pre-submittal visit to the building department: confirm exactly which sealed engineering your project requires • Assemble everything drawn so far plus the geotechnical report (or commission one — foundation design needs it) • Engage the state-licensed PE with a written scope: review coverage, design development needed, deliverables, fee • Expect and plan for revision rounds — they are the review working, not failing • Build from the sealed set: the review's value compounds when the field follows the engineered drawings

What else do project teams ask?

I am building my own home. Does that change the PE requirement?
It does not change whether sealed engineering is required — but single-family homes often qualify for prescriptive (non-engineered) construction under the residential code, which is why many owner-built homes permit without a PE seal. The PE requirement triggers when the design leaves the prescriptive path: engineered foundations on problem soils, long spans or unusual framing, hillside construction, high-wind or high-seismic design, or MEP systems beyond the residential prescriptive scope. I advise owner-builders to check the prescriptive limits against their actual design early — the most common surprise is discovering at plan check that the "simple" custom home exceeded prescriptive framing tables three pages ago.
What does the PE review cover on an owner-builder project?
Whatever engineered scope the permit requires: the structural design (foundations, framing, lateral system) with verified calculations, the MEP design if sealed MEP is required, and the civil/grading design where applicable. The review starts with completeness — I confirm the design actually exists in reviewable form, because owner-builder drawing sets are often the thinnest input I see — then verifies code compliance, checks the calculations independently, coordinates the disciplines, and marks required revisions. The deliverable is a sealed, coordinated permit set plus the calculation package. I also flag constructability issues generously on owner-builder reviews, since the builder reading the plans is often the owner reading them for the first time.
How much should an owner-builder budget for PE review-and-seal?
It scales with the engineered scope, not with the romance of the project: a straightforward structural review-and-seal on a prescriptive-plus home costs far less than full structural plus MEP engineering on a custom home with complex site conditions. I price owner-builder work from the actual drawings and the AHJ's requirements — never from a flat "stamp fee," because there is no such legitimate thing. As a planning range, expect the engineering to run a small percentage of construction cost, and expect it to be the best-spent money on the project: the review catches the foundation error, the undersized beam, the missed lateral detailing before they become the most expensive change orders of the build.
Can I do the design myself and just have a PE seal it?
A PE can review and seal your design, but "just seal it" misunderstands the engagement: the PE must review it genuinely — verify the calculations (or perform them), check code compliance, require revisions — and the review frequently finds that owner-produced designs need substantial engineering development before they are sealable. That is normal and not a judgment; residential structural design is a professional discipline. I work with owner-designers regularly: bring what you have, I review it honestly, we develop what is missing under my responsible charge, and the seal goes on a design the PE truly stands behind. What no legitimate PE does is seal your drawings unreviewed — the board holds the sealing engineer responsible, and responsible charge cannot be faked.
