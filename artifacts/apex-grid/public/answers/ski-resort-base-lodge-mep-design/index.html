How Is MEP Design Engineered for a Ski Resort Base Lodge?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Ski resort base lodge MEP design derates heating equipment for altitude, recovers heat from locker-room and kitchen exhaust, sizes hot water for the rental and après-ski peaks, tempers the large makeup-air load for commercial kitchen hoods, and builds freeze protection and snowmelt into every exposed surface.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Altitude Derating and Cold-Climate Ventilation

Every fuel-burning appliance in the lodge must be evaluated at elevation. The engineer applies the manufacturer's altitude derating to boilers, furnaces, unit heaters, and kitchen equipment — a rooftop unit rated at sea level is a smaller machine at 10,000 feet — and verifies that gas-fired kitchen equipment will actually ignite and hold temperature with the thinner air, since some commercial ranges and fryers need high-altitude orificing. Electric heat avoids the derating question but carries the resort's demand charges, so the engineer typically models both and lets the lifecycle cost decide. Ventilation is designed around heat recovery because the lodge exhausts enormous volumes: locker rooms full of wet gear, restrooms, and kitchens. Energy-recovery wheels or heat pipes on those exhaust streams preheat the incoming ventilation air, and the engineer sequences the recovery so the coldest incoming air sees the warmest exhaust first. Vestibules and air curtains are the envelope's front line. The main entries see hundreds of door cycles per hour on a powder day, and the engineer designs double vestibules with heaters sized for the door-open condition, plus overhead air curtains at service and rental doors that stay open for equipment movement. Boot rooms and rental shops get dedicated exhaust — wet boots and drying gloves generate humidity that will condense on any cold surface — and that exhaust is routed to the heat-recovery stream rather than dumped to the outside.

Base Lodge MEP Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the permit set is developed: • Combustion equipment derated for the site's actual elevation and verified against the jurisdiction's design temperature • Domestic hot water plant sized for the rental, lesson, and après-ski peaks with recovery, not just storage • Commercial kitchen hood exhaust balanced with tempered makeup air; demand-controlled kitchen ventilation evaluated • Heat-recovery ventilation on locker-room, restroom, and kitchen exhaust streams • Snowmelt zoned for entries, accessible ramps, and service areas with slab-sensor control and pre-storm override • Heat trace or in-envelope routing for every water line exposed to freezing conditions • Septic or sewer capacity confirmed with the county health department before seating and fixture counts are locked • Fire-district water-supply review completed early — hydrant flow, access, and any required on-site storage • Jurisdiction confirmed: incorporated ski-town design review and energy code vs unincorporated-county fire-district path

What else do project teams ask?

Why does heating equipment need to be derated at ski resort elevations?
At 8,000 to 11,000 feet the air is much thinner, so a gas burner gets less oxygen per cubic foot of combustion air and produces less heat than its sea-level rating. The engineer applies the manufacturer's altitude correction to every boiler, furnace, and rooftop unit, then checks the corrected capacity against the design heating load. Skipping this step is one of the classic base lodge failures: the building is fine at 30 degrees and cannot hold temperature on a minus-10 powder day.
What is the biggest energy cost in a base lodge?
Usually the makeup air for the commercial kitchen exhaust. A Type I hood can exhaust thousands of cubic feet per minute, and every cubic foot must be replaced with outdoor air that has to be heated from single digits to room temperature. The engineer attacks this with a right-sized hood, demand-controlled kitchen ventilation that ramps the exhaust with actual cooking activity, and heat recovery on the exhaust stream where the grease load allows it.
Do base lodges need snowmelt systems?
At the entries, accessible routes, and anywhere guests transition from snow to hard floor — yes, effectively. Skier traffic tracks snow and meltwater inside, and an unmelted entry slab ices over within an hour on a cold day. The engineer zones the snowmelt separately from space heating, controls it with slab sensors, and gives operations a pre-storm override so the slab is warm before the snow falls rather than after it freezes.
Does the permit path differ between a ski town and county land?
Meaningfully. Inside an incorporated resort town, the lodge goes through the town building department and often a design review board with strict architectural standards and sometimes a tougher local energy code. On unincorporated county land, the county issues the building permit but the mountain fire district conducts its own review — focused on water supply, access, and defensible space — and the county health department governs septic design, which can cap the building's seating. The engineer confirms the authority having jurisdiction before schematic design locks the program.
