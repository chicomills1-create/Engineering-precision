How Are Municipal Wastewater Treatment Plants Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

Municipal wastewater treatment plant design starts with the NPDES permit — the effluent limits for BOD, suspended solids, ammonia, nitrogen, and phosphorus dictate the entire process train. Engineers then select treatment processes (preliminary, primary, biological, tertiary), size basins and equipment for current and 20-year projected flows, design the solids handling and disinfection systems, and produce permit-ready civil, structural, mechanical, electrical, and controls drawings. The design must keep the existing plant running if it's an upgrade — which most projects are.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Start with the permit, not the equipment

Every treatment plant design I've been part of begins the same way: with the discharge permit. The NPDES permit — issued by the state environmental agency under EPA delegation — sets numeric limits on what can leave the plant: biochemical oxygen demand, total suspended solids, ammonia, and increasingly total nitrogen and total phosphorus. Those numbers are the design basis. A plant discharging to a nutrient-sensitive watershed needs biological nutrient removal or better; a plant with generous limits can run a simpler conventional activated-sludge train. Designing the process before reading the permit is how projects end up with the wrong plant.

The second input is flow: current influent flows and loadings, plus the 20-year projection the state usually requires for funded projects. Inflow and infiltration studies, population projections, and industrial pretreatment surveys all feed the design flows — average day, peak day, peak hour. Undersize the hydraulics and the plant floods its own processes during the first big storm; oversize everything and the municipality pays for concrete it will never fill. Getting the flow basis right is unglamorous and decisive.

The treatment train, stage by stage

Preliminary treatment protects everything downstream: screening (coarse and fine) removes rags and debris, grit removal takes out sand and gravel that would otherwise wear out pumps and fill basins. Skip or undersize this stage and maintenance costs eat the plant alive — I've seen fine screens pay for themselves in avoided pump rebuilds within two years. Headworks also include influent pumping sized for peak-hour flow with standby capacity, because the plant cannot go offline when the influent doesn't stop.

Primary treatment settles out the easy solids. Then the biological stage does the real work: activated sludge, with its aeration basins, blowers, and secondary clarifiers, remains the workhorse of American municipal treatment. Where nutrient limits apply, the design adds anoxic and anaerobic zones for biological nitrogen and phosphorus removal — the BNR configurations (A2O, Bardenpho, oxidation ditches with anoxic zones) that meet limits without chemical addition. Where footprint is constrained or effluent quality must be exceptional, membrane bioreactors (MBR) replace secondary clarifiers with membrane filtration — higher capital cost, dramatically smaller footprint, and effluent clean enough for reuse.

Tertiary treatment polishes: filtration (granular media or cloth-disk) and disinfection. UV disinfection has largely displaced chlorine gas for safety reasons, though chlorination/dechlorination remains common. Then solids handling — the half of the plant the public never sees: thickening, anaerobic digestion (which can produce biogas worth capturing), dewatering, and disposal or beneficial reuse of biosolids. Odor control — biofilters, chemical scrubbers, or carbon adsorption — is designed for the neighbors, because a plant that smells is a plant the community fights.

  • Headworks: screening, grit removal, influent pumping
  • Biological treatment: activated sludge, BNR, or MBR
  • Tertiary filtration and UV or chlorine disinfection
  • Solids: thickening, digestion, dewatering, biosolids
  • Odor control designed for the fence line

Upgrades: designing while the plant runs

Most municipal treatment work isn't greenfield — it's upgrading a plant built in the 1970s or 80s that's hitting structural, mechanical, and regulatory limits simultaneously. The defining constraint of upgrade design is that the plant must stay in compliance and in service throughout construction. That means phased sequencing: temporary treatment, bypass pumping plans, tie-in windows scheduled around low-flow periods, and a construction sequence the operations staff can actually live with.

Our structural engineers assess the existing basins first — concrete in wastewater service suffers sulfide attack, and a basin that looks fine can be structurally compromised. The honest assessment sometimes says demolish and rebuild; sometimes it says rehabilitate with coatings and targeted repair. Either way, the decision is engineered, not guessed. Then process, MEP, and structural design proceed as one package: new blowers need new electrical service and MCCs, new processes need new SCADA integration, and every tie-in needs a shutdown plan. The firms that treat upgrades as 'just add equipment' are the ones whose projects end up in claims.

What drives cost and schedule

Treatment plant capital costs scale with flow, effluent quality, and site constraints — a small lagoon-to-mechanical conversion is a fundamentally different project than a 20-MGD BNR upgrade, and anyone quoting without the permit limits and flow basis is guessing. What I tell municipalities: the engineering fee is the smallest line item and the highest-leverage one. A well-engineered project with complete documents bids competitively and builds without change orders; an under-engineered project saves 2% on design and loses 15% in construction claims.

Schedule is driven by three things: the funding calendar (SRF Intended Use Plan cycles reward projects with complete engineering reports), the permit timeline (state environmental review and NPDES modification if limits change), and equipment lead times (large blowers, generators, and custom gates are long-lead items that must be ordered during design, not after bidding). We sequence design deliverables to all three — because a treatment plant that misses its funding window waits a year, and a year of noncompliance is expensive.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the first step in designing a wastewater treatment plant?
Reading the NPDES permit and establishing the design flow basis. The permit's effluent limits dictate the process train, and the 20-year flow and loading projections size every basin and pump. Equipment selection comes after — never before — those two inputs are fixed.
How long does it take to design a municipal treatment plant?
Preliminary engineering and the funding-required engineering report typically run 3-6 months; final design runs 6-12 months depending on plant size and complexity. Permitting and funding cycles run in parallel. The honest schedule-killer is starting design before the flow basis and permit strategy are settled.
Can a treatment plant stay in operation during an upgrade?
It must — municipalities can't stop treating sewage. Upgrade design is phased around continuous operation: temporary treatment provisions, bypass pumping, tie-ins scheduled for low-flow windows, and sequencing the operations staff approves. This is why upgrade engineering costs more per dollar of construction than greenfield design.
What is BNR and when is it required?
Biological nutrient removal — treatment configurations that remove nitrogen and phosphorus biologically rather than chemically. It's required when the NPDES permit sets numeric nutrient limits, which is increasingly common in watersheds with TMDLs for nutrients (Chesapeake Bay, Lake Erie, the Gulf hypoxia zone, and many state-impaired waters).
