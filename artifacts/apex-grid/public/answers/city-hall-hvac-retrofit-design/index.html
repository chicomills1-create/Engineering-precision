How Are City Hall HVAC Retrofits Engineered for Efficiency?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers reassess actual loads, phase controls upgrades and equipment replacement around continuous government operations, and commission every phase so savings persist.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Assessment, Load Recalculation, and Retrofit Strategy

The retrofit begins with measurement, not assumptions. The engineer trends existing equipment runtime, reviews utility data, interviews facilities staff about chronic issues, and tests the systems that are suspected of underperforming. This assessment separates the equipment that is genuinely at end of life from systems that are simply miscontrolled — a surprising share of city hall energy waste lives in controls, not iron. Loads are recalculated for the building as it stands today: LED lighting has slashed internal gains, envelope improvements changed the shell load, and occupancy patterns shifted. The retrofit strategy follows a hierarchy — fix controls and recommission first, replace the worst equipment second, improve distribution third — so each dollar chases the best return. The engineer documents the baseline rigorously, because the project's success will be judged against it.

Phased Construction and Decarbonization Options

Phasing design is construction sequencing as an engineering discipline. The engineer divides the building into work zones, designs temporary conditioning and isolation for each phase, and schedules disruptive work around the council calendar and public-facing operations. Equipment replacements are timed to the seasons — chillers in heating season, boilers in cooling season — so the building is never without its critical system when it needs it most. Decarbonization gets an honest engineering analysis rather than a slogan. Air- and water-source heat pumps, heat recovery chillers, and full or partial electrification are modeled against the existing gas infrastructure, electrical service capacity, and utility rate structures. The engineer presents lifecycle costs with real numbers so the city council can decide with eyes open — some buildings electrify beautifully, others need a phased path that starts with heat recovery and high-efficiency gas.

City Hall HVAC Retrofit Checklist

A complete retrofit design delivers verified savings without disrupting government: • Measured baseline: trended performance, utility analysis, and staff interviews • Load recalculation for the building as it exists today, not as originally built • Controls upgrade and recommissioning as the first retrofit phase • Right-sized high-efficiency equipment selected for actual, not historical, loads • Phased construction sequencing around council sessions and public hours • Decarbonization analysis with lifecycle costs for heat pumps and electrification • Commissioning and measurement-and-verification for every phase

What else do project teams ask?

Why redo the load calculation for an HVAC retrofit?
Because the building changed. LED retrofits cut lighting heat dramatically, envelope improvements altered the shell load, occupancy patterns shifted, and equipment was added or removed over the decades. Sizing new equipment to the original 1980s loads almost guarantees oversized machines that short-cycle, waste energy, and control poorly. A fresh load calculation takes a few weeks and prevents twenty years of inefficiency — it is the cheapest engineering in the project.
Can city hall stay open during an HVAC retrofit?
Yes — continuous occupancy is a design constraint, not a wish. The engineer phases the work by zone and season, provides temporary conditioning where needed, isolates construction areas from public spaces, and schedules the noisiest, dustiest work outside public hours. Council chambers and public counters get special protection in the phasing plan. It costs more than an empty-building retrofit, but closing city hall is not an option.
What is recommissioning and why does it come first?
Recommissioning is tuning the existing systems back to their design intent — fixing stuck dampers, correcting schedules, recalibrating sensors, and rewriting sequences that drifted over the years. It comes first because it is fast, cheap, and often captures a large share of the total savings, which improves the economics of everything that follows. It also reveals which problems are controls-deep versus equipment-deep, so the capital plan targets the right things.
Should a city hall retrofit electrify heating?
It depends on the building, the climate, and the utility math — which is exactly what the engineer analyzes. Heat pumps excel in mild climates and in buildings with simultaneous heating and cooling needs where heat recovery shines. In cold climates or buildings with limited electrical service, a phased approach — high-efficiency equipment now, electrification-ready infrastructure for later — may be the responsible path. The analysis presents lifecycle costs honestly so the decision is engineering, not ideology.
