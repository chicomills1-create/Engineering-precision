How Are Cleveland Historic Theaters Structurally Restored?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Cleveland historic theaters are structurally restored by documenting the existing steel, masonry, and long-span roof systems, repairing deterioration with compatible methods, upgrading the structure for modern loads and seismic demands, and coordinating every intervention with historic preservation review and the authority having jurisdiction.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Theater's Structural Systems

Understanding a historic theater starts with its three structural personalities. The auditorium is a long-span box: steel roof trusses clear-spanning the seating, masonry side walls carrying the truss reactions, and a balcony structure - often a combination of cantilevered steel and hung rods - projecting into the volume. The stage house is a tall, narrow tower with its own structural logic: high masonry walls, the gridiron floor of steel beams near the top where rigging loads concentrate, and a roof designed for the stage's unique demands. Connecting them are lobbies, stairs, and secondary spaces in more conventional framing, but the whole assembly must work as one building under wind and seismic loads. The original materials tell the engineer what can be relied upon. Early-1900s structural steel has lower and more variable strength than modern steel, and riveted connections behave differently from bolted or welded ones - the evaluation establishes actual material properties through testing or conservative historical defaults rather than assuming modern values. The masonry is typically multi-wythe brick with lime mortar, strong in compression but weak against the lateral loads the restoration must now resist. Ornamental plaster, terra cotta, and decorative stone are nonstructural but heavy, and their anchorage - often original wire and light clips - gets evaluated as falling hazards, the same concern that drives facade ordinances in other cities.

Restoration with Modern Demands

The restoration engineer designs for the theater the building is becoming, not the one it was. Modern rigging loads are calculated from the actual equipment inventory - line sets, lighting battens, speaker clusters - and the gridiron and roof steel are verified and strengthened for those loads with new members that read as backstage infrastructure, not as alterations to the historic fabric. HVAC modernization threads large ducts and heavy air handlers through a building never designed for them; the engineer designs the supports and the openings, verifying that cutting a duct chase through a historic wall does not compromise the wall's structural role. Seismic and wind upgrades add the lateral capacity the original design lacked, detailed as new steel braced frames or reinforced masonry cores tucked into non-public spaces. Preservation coordination runs through every structural decision. The engineer works with the preservation architect and the reviewing authorities - Cleveland's Landmarks Commission for designated buildings, and state historic preservation review where tax credits are involved - to detail interventions that meet the Secretary of the Interior's standards: repairs in kind where possible, new work differentiated but compatible, and no removal of character-defining features for structural convenience. Structural observation during construction is essential, because the true condition of century-old steel and masonry is only fully revealed as finishes come off, and the engineer must adapt details to what the opened building actually shows.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the biggest structural risk in a historic theater?
Corroded long-span roof trusses over the auditorium. Roof leaks attack the steel that holds up the entire ceiling, and the ornate plaster below hides the damage from view. The structural assessment opens ceilings and inspects the trusses hands-on, because a compromised truss chord over a thousand seats is the highest-consequence element in the building.
Can modern stage rigging hang from century-old steel?
Only after verification and usually with strengthening. Modern rigging imposes concentrated loads far beyond what the original gridiron and roof steel were designed for. The engineer calculates the actual rigging loads, evaluates the existing steel's capacity, and designs supplemental members or reinforcement where demand exceeds capacity.
How does landmark status affect theater restoration in Cleveland?
Designated landmarks require review by the Cleveland Landmarks Commission, and projects using historic tax credits must also satisfy state and federal preservation standards. Structural interventions - new bracing, openings, reinforcement - are detailed to preserve character-defining features while meeting code. The engineer coordinates with preservation reviewers from schematic design onward.
Does a theater restoration use current building codes?
Yes, with the existing-building provisions of the code, which recognize that a 1920s theater cannot be rebuilt as new construction. The engineer documents compliance through those provisions: the structure must support the new loads and meet defined levels of safety for wind, seismic, and fire, while the code allows reasonable approaches to elements that cannot practically meet new-construction rules. The authority having jurisdiction approves the compliance path.
