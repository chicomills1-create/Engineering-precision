How Are Refrigerant Leak Detection Systems Designed for Stores?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers place sensors where refrigerant collects, set two-stage alarms tied to code concentration limits, and interlock detection with ventilation, equipment shutdown, and building management alerts.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Sensor Placement and Refrigerant Behavior

Refrigerants do not mix evenly with air, so sensor placement follows physics. Heavier-than-air refrigerants sink and pool at floor level, in pits, and under case lineups; lighter ones rise toward ceilings. The engineer maps every potential leak point — compressor shaft seals, valve packings, brazed and mechanical joints, case connections — and places sensors in the breathing zone and at collection points, not just one detector in the middle of a room. Coverage rules come from ASHRAE 15 and the equipment listing: machinery rooms need detection as a matter of course, and occupied spaces get sensors where the refrigerant charge and room volume trigger code requirements. The engineer also considers airflow — a sensor downstream of a supply diffuser may never see a leak that a sensor in a stagnant corner catches immediately. Placement drawings show each sensor's location, height, and the leak sources it covers.

Alarm Levels, Ventilation, and Shutdown Interlocks

A two-stage alarm strategy gives the store a measured response instead of panic. The first stage, typically set around 25 percent of the lower flammability limit or the applicable concentration limit, triggers increased ventilation and notifies maintenance through the building management system. The second stage, higher up the scale, shuts down the refrigeration equipment feeding the leak, starts emergency exhaust, and sounds the evacuation alarm. The engineer sizes machine room ventilation per ASHRAE 15 for both normal and emergency operation, designs makeup air paths so exhaust fans can actually move air, and wires shutdown relays to the right equipment — killing the rack compressors but keeping exhaust running. Every interlock is proven at commissioning with simulated alarms, because an interlock that was never tested is just a hope.

Leak Detection Design Checklist

A code-compliant detection design documents the refrigerant, the room, and the response: • Refrigerant safety classification and charge quantity for every system • Sensor locations at collection points, breathing zone, and per ASHRAE 15 coverage • Two-stage alarm setpoints tied to concentration limits with listed detectors • Machine room ventilation sized for normal and emergency exhaust rates • Interlocks to equipment shutdown, exhaust fans, and the building management system • Bump-test and calibration schedule with sensor replacement life documented • Commissioning tests proving every alarm level and interlock actually operates

What else do project teams ask?

Why does leak detection matter more with newer A2L refrigerants?
Newer low-GWP refrigerants like R-454B and R-454C are classified A2L — mildly flammable — unlike the non-flammable A1 refrigerants they replace. That changes the code requirements: lower alarm thresholds, more ventilation, attention to ignition sources, and stricter limits on charge in occupied spaces. Detection becomes a life-safety system rather than just an environmental or product-loss measure, so the engineering, placement, and commissioning all get more rigorous.
Where should refrigerant sensors be placed in a grocery store?
Sensors go where refrigerant will collect and where leaks originate: low in the compressor rack machine room, near case lineup piping with mechanical joints, at low points in enclosed spaces, and in any occupied area where the charge-to-volume ratio triggers ASHRAE 15 requirements. Height follows the refrigerant — heavier-than-air near the floor, lighter near the ceiling — and placement accounts for room airflow so sensors sit where leaking refrigerant actually travels, not in dead corners or direct supply airstreams.
What happens when a leak detector alarms?
A properly designed system responds in stages. The first alarm level starts additional ventilation and alerts maintenance through the building management system while the store keeps operating. If concentrations keep rising to the second level, the system shuts down the leaking refrigeration equipment, ramps exhaust to emergency rates, and triggers evacuation alarming. The exact sequence is documented in the design and proven during commissioning so staff know what each alarm means.
How often do refrigerant sensors need calibration?
Most manufacturers require bump-testing or calibration every six to twelve months, and the design documents the schedule, the calibration gas required, and the expected sensor life — typically several years for infrared sensors, shorter for some electrochemical types. Sensors drift, get contaminated by cleaning chemicals, or simply age out, and an uncalibrated detector is a false sense of security. The maintenance plan treats calibration as a scheduled task, not an afterthought.
