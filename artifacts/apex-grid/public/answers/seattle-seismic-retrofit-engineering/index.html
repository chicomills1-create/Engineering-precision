How Is a Seattle Building Retrofitted for Earthquakes?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A Seattle building is retrofitted by evaluating it under ASCE 41, anchoring unreinforced masonry walls to floors and roofs for out-of-plane forces, bracing or removing parapets, strengthening diaphragms, and correcting soft stories — permitted through Seattle's building department or King County for unincorporated sites.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The URM Retrofit Sequence

A typical Seattle URM retrofit follows a fixed engineering sequence. First, the ASCE 41 evaluation: the engineer documents the lateral system, tests or assumes material properties, and runs the linear or nonlinear analysis the standard prescribes for the building's size and configuration. The evaluation identifies each deficiency — missing wall anchors, inadequate diaphragm shear capacity, weak piers between windows — and assigns it a priority. Second, the retrofit design addresses the deficiencies in an order that matches the city's compliance tiers, which generally start with wall anchorage and parapet bracing before progressing to full diaphragm and in-plane wall upgrades. Third, the permit set goes through structural plan review, and fourth, the engineer provides structural observation during construction, because anchor installation quality in old brick cannot be verified after the fact. Material reality shapes every detail. Old lime-mortar brickwork can be soft enough that anchors must bear on the full wall thickness with large plates, and the engineer specifies proof testing of installed anchors to confirm capacity in the actual wall rather than in a catalog. Where walls are too deteriorated to anchor reliably, the design may add new interior shear walls or steel braced frames that carry the seismic load instead, leaving the brick as a veneer. The engineer coordinates all of this with the architect, because new steel inside a historic storefront changes the interior the tenants see.

Parapets, Diaphragms, and Soft Stories

Parapets are the most visible hazard and often the first retrofit element. The engineer calculates the out-of-plane demand on the parapet, then either designs steel braces from the parapet back to the roof framing at regular intervals or specifies partial demolition to a safe height with a new cap. Bracing must connect to roof structure capable of taking the load, so the engineer checks the roof diaphragm and its supports rather than assuming they suffice. On buildings where the parapet is a character-defining historic feature, the bracing is detailed to preserve the exterior appearance while the steel does its work behind the face. Diaphragm strengthening turns flexible wood floors into reliable load paths. The engineer typically specifies a structural plywood overlay nailed or screwed to the existing floor, new blocking at panel edges, and steel collectors that drag the diaphragm forces into the shear walls or new braced frames. At soft stories, new steel moment frames or cantilevered columns are threaded into the ground floor with minimal disruption to the retail frontage, and their foundations are checked or underpinned for the overturning the frames deliver. Each of these elements is designed so its failure mode is ductile — steel yielding before masonry crushing — which is the core philosophy behind the entire retrofit.

What else do project teams ask?

What is a URM building and why does Seattle require retrofits?
URM stands for unreinforced masonry — typically early-1900s brick buildings whose walls contain no steel reinforcement. In earthquakes these walls can separate from floors and collapse outward, which is why Seattle established a mandatory retrofit program requiring owners to anchor walls, brace parapets, and strengthen diaphragms on a compliance timeline. The engineering goal is collapse prevention and safe egress, not a damage-free building.
What is an ASCE 41 evaluation?
ASCE 41 is the national standard for seismic evaluation and retrofit of existing buildings. The engineer surveys the structure, establishes material properties through testing or conservative defaults, and analyzes the building's expected performance in design-level earthquakes. The evaluation produces a deficiency list — missing anchors, weak diaphragms, soft stories — that becomes the scope of the retrofit design.
How are brick walls anchored to floors in a retrofit?
Through-bolts or adhesive anchors are installed through the masonry wall into the floor or roof framing, with steel plates, channels, or rods on the exterior face distributing the load across the brick. Each anchor is sized for the out-of-plane seismic force on its tributary wall area, and installations are typically proof-tested because century-old mortar varies widely in strength.
Does the permit path differ inside Seattle versus unincorporated King County?
Yes. Within Seattle, URM retrofits go through the Seattle Department of Construction and Inspections under the city's published retrofit standards and compliance tiers. In unincorporated King County, the county's permitting division reviews the project under the state building code without the city's URM-specific program. The structural engineering is equivalent, but submittal portals, review timelines, and inspection scheduling differ.
