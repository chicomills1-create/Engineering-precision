How Should an Equestrian Facility Be Engineered for Horses and Riders?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

An equestrian facility is engineered around clear-span, column-free riding arenas, continuous barn ventilation that controls dust and ammonia for horse respiratory health, and drainage plus manure management that prevents mud, odor, and runoff problems.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Clear-span arena structures

I start the arena design from the discipline and the program: what events it hosts, how many horses work at once, and what clear dimensions that demands. Then I select the structural system — rigid-frame steel for the widest spans, engineered timber or steel trusses where the owner wants a warmer look — and design the frame for the full clear width with no intermediate support. Eave height gets set for air quality and lighting as well as clearance: I want the roof high enough that dust and heat stratify above the work zone and that arena lighting can be aimed for uniformity without glare. The footing is a structural system in its own right even though it sits on the ground. I specify a compacted, drained base with a cushion layer of washed sand or a sand-fiber blend, with the drainage base sloped so the arena sheds water rather than ponding. Kick boards and arena walls are designed to take horse impact without splintering, and any doors or gates get latches and hardware that a horse cannot catch a hoof or halter on. Every detail in an arena gets judged by one question: what happens when a thousand-pound animal hits it.

Ventilation and dust control

Horse barns run on the stack effect: warm, moist, ammonia-bearing air rises and leaves through ridge vents or cupolas while cooler fresh air enters at the eaves. I size the ridge and eave openings for the barn's volume and the local climate, and I orient the building so prevailing winds assist rather than fight the natural flow. In hot climates or deep barns where natural ventilation falls short, I add mechanical exhaust or circulation fans sequenced to barn temperature and humidity. Ammonia control is really a management-and-design partnership: good ventilation removes it, but the design also has to make cleaning easy — washable wall surfaces in stalls, drains that actually drain, and aisle floors that can be hosed without ponding. I keep the ventilation design honest about dust too. A barn full of horses on dry bedding generates enormous particulate load, so the air-exchange rate I design for accounts for the real use, not an empty-barn ideal. Respiratory health is the whole reason the system exists.

Drainage, washdown, and manure management

Every wet area in the facility gets its own drainage strategy. Wash racks get sloped, sealed floors with trench drains, hair and sand screening, and a connection to the site sanitary or approved disposal system — never to a storm drain. Stable aisles and feed rooms are graded so washdown water reaches a drain instead of soaking into bedding. Outdoor paddocks and turnouts are graded and surfaced so they drain without turning to mud, because mud is a hoof-health problem as much as a nuisance. Manure management is a site-planning discipline: storage on an impervious pad, covered or sited to shed rain, located downwind and downgradient of the barn and away from wells, with equipment access for loading and hauling. The jurisdiction question matters here more than most owners expect. An equestrian facility in an unincorporated county area typically answers to county environmental and agricultural rules — manure-storage setbacks, runoff controls, sometimes nutrient-management expectations — while the same facility inside city limits may face different nuisance and zoning constraints. I identify the AHJ and its rules before the site plan is drawn, and the civil design carries the stormwater and runoff controls through permitting.

What else do project teams ask?

Why must a riding arena be clear-span with no interior columns?
Because horses and riders move fast in every direction, and a column inside the working area is a collision hazard. A horse at a canter or gallop cannot reliably avoid an obstruction in the middle of the ring, and a collision can kill the horse and seriously injure the rider. Clear-span structures — rigid frames or trusses carrying the full width — keep the entire arena open. The structural cost of the longer span is small compared to the liability and safety cost of columns in the ring.
How wide can a clear-span riding arena be?
Common private arenas span 60 to 80 feet; training and small show arenas run 80 to 100 feet; large show arenas can push past 120 feet with engineered steel frames. The right width depends on the discipline — dressage needs a full 20-by-60-meter court, while a cutting or reining arena can be narrower but wants more length. I size the span from the program first, then design the frame, because the structure is cheap compared to discovering the arena is too small for its intended events.
How is dust controlled in an equestrian arena?
Dust control is a combination of footing, water, and air movement. I specify a riding surface with a proper base and cushion layer — typically washed sand, sometimes blended with fiber or textile additives that hold moisture — plus a watering system and schedule that keeps the top layer damp enough to suppress dust without turning slick. Overhead, the arena gets ventilation that moves dusty air out through ridge vents rather than letting it hang at rider level. Skipping the watering system to save money is the most common mistake I see; the dust comes back within weeks.
What does wash-rack and manure drainage need to handle?
Wash racks need floors sloped to trench drains that capture washdown water, horsehair, and sand — with screening or settling so solids do not plug the system. Manure storage needs an impervious pad with contained leachate, sited away from wells and watercourses and downwind of the barn. Both connect to the site's broader stormwater plan so wash water and manure runoff never reach a stream or a neighbor's property. In county jurisdictions, agricultural runoff and manure-storage rules often govern the details, so I verify the local requirements early.
