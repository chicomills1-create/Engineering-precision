How Are Dealership Detail Bays Designed for Water and Drainage?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design detail bays with tempered wash water, sloped floors to trench drains feeding an oil-water separator, chemical-resistant finishes, and ventilation and high-CRI lighting suited to wet detailing work.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Wash Water Supply and Backflow Protection

Detail bays need reliable tempered water at every wash position. The engineer designs hot and cold supplies — often with a dedicated water heater for the detail operation — piped to hose stations or pressure-washer drops with quick connects, sized so simultaneous use does not collapse pressure. Water chemistry matters too: in hard-water regions, spot-free rinse systems or deionized final rinse prevent the mineral spotting that ruins a detail. Backflow prevention is a code requirement wherever hoses connect to the potable system, especially with chemical injectors in the mix. The design specifies the appropriate backflow assembly for the hazard level, locates it for testing access, and keeps chemical mixing stations separated from potable outlets so there is no cross-connection path.

Trench Drains, Separators, and Floor Grading

The floor is graded as a drainage system, not just a slab. Slopes direct wash water to trench drains running the length of each bay, and the trench drains feed a treatment train: sediment and sand traps first, then the oil-water separator, then the sanitary connection. The engineer sizes each element for peak wash flow and documents the maintenance access — separators that cannot be pumped out will fail. Discharge permitting shapes the design. Most jurisdictions prohibit wash water in storm drains outright and require separator-treated discharge to sanitary sewer under an industrial discharge permit, sometimes with sampling ports. The engineer confirms the local requirements early, because a detail bay designed without the permit path becomes a very expensive retrofit.

Detail Bay Design Checklist

A complete detail bay package manages water from supply to sewer: • Tempered wash water with backflow prevention at every hose station • Floor grading to trench drains sized for peak simultaneous wash flow • Sediment traps and oil-water separator ahead of the sanitary connection • Discharge permit path confirmed with the local authority • Chemical-resistant sealed floors with coved bases and washable wall finishes • Ventilation for chemical fumes and elevated humidity • High-CRI lighting positioned for paint defect visibility

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't detail bay wash water go to the storm drain?
Wash water carries petroleum residues, detergents, heavy metals from brake dust, and detailing chemicals — all pollutants that storm drains carry untreated to waterways. Environmental regulations prohibit the discharge, and violations bring fines. The engineered solution routes wash water through trench drains and an oil-water separator to the sanitary sewer, where it receives treatment. The design also includes the local discharge permit, which many jurisdictions require before the bay opens.
How is an oil-water separator sized for a detail bay?
By the peak wash water flow it must treat — the number of bays washing simultaneously, hose flow rates, and any pressure-washer demand — plus the expected oil loading. Gravity separators need enough residence time for oil droplets to float and separate, so undersizing means oil passes straight through. The engineer sizes from the plumbing fixture count and flow rates, adds sediment pretreatment, and specifies a unit with accessible chambers for the regular pump-outs it will need.
What flooring works in a dealership detail bay?
Sealed, chemical-resistant flooring — typically an epoxy or polyaspartic system rated for petroleum and detergent exposure — with integral coved bases that carry the waterproofing up the wall. The surface needs enough texture for wet slip resistance without being so rough it traps grime. Control joints are sealed, and the whole assembly is graded to drains. Bare concrete absorbs oils and chemicals, stains permanently, and eventually breaks down under constant wet chemical exposure.
Do detail bays need special lighting?
Yes — defect visibility is the point of detailing. The design uses bright, high-CRI lighting positioned at angles that reveal swirls, holograms, and paint defects rather than washing them out with flat overhead light. Many shops add color-tunable or inspection lighting at the final quality station so the finish reads correctly before delivery. Standard shop high-bays alone leave technicians chasing defects they cannot see.
