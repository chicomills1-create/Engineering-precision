How Does the Tribal Cultural Resource Review Process Work?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Tribal cultural review runs through the THPO, who assumes SHPO functions on tribal lands under the 1992 NHPA amendments. For federally involved projects, Section 106 requires identifying historic properties in the area of potential effects, assessing effects, and resolving adverse effects through consultation. Engineers start review during conceptual design, design around identified resources, and carry inadvertent-discovery plans in the construction documents.

The Section 106 Sequence Engineers Actually Follow

The process begins when a federal nexus appears: federal funding, a federal permit, or federal land. The agency official identifies consulting parties, which always include the THPO for undertakings on tribal lands and any tribe attaching religious and cultural significance to properties that could be affected, wherever they are located. Together they define the area of potential effects, the geographic area where the project could change the character or use of historic properties, directly or indirectly. The APE is bigger than the construction footprint: visual, auditory, and atmospheric effects count. Identification effort scales with the undertaking. A records search reviews known sites, previous surveys, and historic maps. Field survey follows where the records suggest potential, using pedestrian inventory and, where appropriate, subsurface testing. Tribal consultation runs throughout, because some properties, traditional cultural properties, sacred sites, gathering areas, are known through tribal knowledge rather than the archaeological record. Eligibility evaluation applies the National Register criteria with tribal expertise formally recognized. The agency then makes its finding of effect in consultation with the THPO or SHPO, and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation enters the process for adverse effects and certain program alternatives.

What Happens When Resources Are Found

Discovery during survey is the good outcome: the resource is known before design is final, and the site plan can avoid it. Avoidance is the preferred treatment under the regulations, and engineers achieve it by shifting alignments, reducing footprints, or placing improvements outside site boundaries plus a protective buffer. The redesign is documented as part of the consultation record, showing the agency considered the resource and acted on the findings. Discovery during construction is the expensive outcome the process is designed to prevent. That is why construction documents on tribal-land projects carry inadvertent-discovery protocols: stop work in the immediate area, secure the location, notify the project archaeologist, the agency, and the THPO, and resume only with authorization. Some projects carry tribal monitors during earthwork as a standard condition. Where adverse effects cannot be avoided, the memorandum of agreement spells out mitigation, data recovery, documentation to archival standards, public interpretation, or other measures the consulting parties accept, and construction proceeds under those terms. The consulting parties decide what is adequate, never the contractor alone.

What else do project teams ask?

When is Section 106 review triggered?
Section 106 is triggered by a federal undertaking: any project a federal agency carries out, funds, permits, licenses, or approves. A purely private project on private land with no federal money or permits does not trigger it, but most tribal-land development involves a federal nexus through BIA approvals, federal funding, or trust-land transactions. Tribal law can independently require cultural review even without a federal trigger, as the Navajo Nation's cultural resources protection law does.
What is the difference between a SHPO and a THPO?
A State Historic Preservation Officer administers the federal preservation program for a state. A Tribal Historic Preservation Officer is designated by a federally recognized tribe that has assumed SHPO functions on its tribal lands under the National Historic Preservation Act. On tribal lands, the federal agency consults with the THPO in lieu of the SHPO. Off tribal lands, tribes are still consulting parties for properties of religious and cultural significance to them, alongside the SHPO.
What is an area of potential effects?
The area of potential effects, or APE, is the geographic area within which a project may directly or indirectly cause changes to historic properties. It includes the construction footprint plus areas affected by visual intrusions, noise, vibration, traffic, and changes in land use or access. The agency defines it in consultation with the THPO or SHPO, and it sets the boundary for identification and survey effort.
Does discovering artifacts stop construction?
It pauses work in the immediate discovery area while the find is evaluated, under the project's inadvertent-discovery plan. Work does not necessarily stop project-wide. The project archaeologist, the federal agency, and the THPO assess the discovery, determine whether it is a historic property, and decide on treatment, which can range from avoidance by minor redesign to data recovery. The process resumes once the consulting parties agree on the path forward.
