How Are Feedlot Windbreaks Designed for Cattle Protection?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Windbreaks are engineered for porosity and placement: 40 to 60 percent porous barriers set perpendicular to the prevailing winter wind, protecting a downwind zone roughly 10 to 15 times their height, with structures and snow-drift geometry designed for the loads they will carry.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Porosity, Height, and Protected-Zone Geometry

The engineer designs the barrier's porosity first, because it sets everything downstream. Forty to 60 percent density — measured as the solid fraction of the barrier face — gives the longest, calmest shelter zone; the engineer hits it with board-and-gap fencing patterns, windbreak mesh of the right openness, or tree rows spaced for the mature canopy. Solid barriers are avoided for the main break, though solid sections have their place shielding specific spots like waterers from direct blast. Height and placement are drawn together on the site plan. The engineer measures the barrier height, multiplies by 10 to 15 for the protected depth, and overlays that zone on the pen layout to confirm which pens are actually sheltered. Where the yard runs deeper than one zone, the engineer adds interior breaks — often the pen fences themselves are upgraded to windbreak porosity on the windward rows. The engineer also checks summer: a windbreak that blocks the cooling summer breeze can add heat stress, so orientation and seasonal wind patterns are weighed together.

Structural Design and Snow Drift Control

A fence windbreak is a sail, and the engineer designs it like one. Posts are sized and embedded for the wind pressure on the barrier area, with bracing at corners and ends where loads concentrate; footings go below frost depth and are sized for the overturning moment, not just the post. The engineer specifies the board or mesh attachment so the barrier stays at its design porosity — loose boards that gap wider in a storm change the aerodynamics and then tear off. Snow is the second load case and the operational headache. The engineer places the windbreak so drifts form in a designated zone — not across the feed alley, not against the bunks, not burying the waterers. A bottom gap of a foot or two under a fence break lets the lowest wind through and pushes the main drift farther downwind, a classic detail the engineer uses where pen access must stay open. Living windbreaks get species that keep low branches and a maintenance plan for the years before they reach effective height, during which a temporary fence break carries the load.

Feedlot Windbreak Design Checklist

Use this checklist before windbreaks are built: • Prevailing winter wind direction established from the local wind rose • Barrier porosity designed at 40 to 60 percent, detailed to hold as materials weather • Windbreak placed perpendicular to the winter wind, upwind of pens and working areas • Protected zone (10 to 15 times barrier height) overlaid on the pen layout and verified • Alley and road gaps detailed against wind funneling • Posts, bracing, and footings engineered for wind load plus snow drift loading • Drift zone placed clear of feed alleys, bunks, and waterers • Summer breeze impact checked so winter protection does not create summer heat stress • Living windbreaks: low-branching species selected, with temporary breaks until maturity

What else do project teams ask?

How far downwind does a windbreak protect?
Roughly 10 to 15 times the windbreak's height for a properly porous barrier — a 20-foot break shelters 200 to 300 feet of pens. The engineer overlays that zone on the site plan to confirm which pens are covered, and adds interior breaks where the yard runs deeper than one protected zone.
Should a windbreak be solid or have gaps?
Gapped — about 40 to 60 percent solid. A solid wall creates violent turbulence just downwind and a short shelter zone, while a porous barrier filters the wind into a long, calm pocket. The engineer designs the porosity deliberately; a solid fence is only used to shield specific spots like waterers.
Do windbreaks pay for themselves?
In cold, windy country they typically do, through feed efficiency: cattle sheltered from winter wind burn less feed energy staying warm and keep gaining. The engineer weighs the structure's cost against the yard's winter feed bill and the health cost of wind-chill events — the math favors windbreaks where winters are long and windy.
Where should snow drift relative to a windbreak?
In a designated zone clear of operations. The engineer places the break so drifts pile where they do no harm — not across feed alleys, against bunks, or over waterers — and uses details like a bottom gap to push drifts downwind of pen access. Snow placement is drawn on the plan, not left to the first blizzard.
