What MEP Systems Does a Modern Fire Station Need?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Fire station MEP design covers the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems for apparatus bays, crew living quarters, and decontamination. The package typically includes vehicle exhaust capture systems for the apparatus bay, station alerting with integrated lighting and PA, HVAC zoned separately for bays and living areas, decontamination laundry and gear cleaning plumbing, commercial kitchen and dormitory systems for 24-hour staffing, and emergency power for alerting, doors, and critical loads.

The concise answer

Fire station MEP design covers the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems for apparatus bays, crew living quarters, and decontamination. The package typically includes vehicle exhaust capture systems for the apparatus bay, station alerting with integrated lighting and PA, HVAC zoned separately for bays and living areas, decontamination laundry and gear cleaning plumbing, commercial kitchen and dormitory systems for 24-hour staffing, and emergency power for alerting, doors, and critical loads. The zoning principle is everything: the dirty side (bays, decon) and the clean side (dorms, kitchen, day room) are different worlds, and the mechanical design keeps them that way — separate air systems, pressure relationships that push air from clean to dirty, never the reverse. I design the station like the health of the crew depends on it, because it does.

Systems for readiness and recovery

The apparatus bay is the working heart: exhaust capture at every truck position, high-volume ventilation, radiant or unit heating for the big doors that open in winter, compressed air, and power for tools and charging. Bay doors get emergency power — a station that can't open its doors in an outage isn't a station. The alerting system ties dispatch to the building: tones, lighting that ramps up instead of blinding sleeping crews, PA zones, and integration with the bay doors and traffic signals outside. Living quarters get residential-grade comfort engineered for shift work: quiet HVAC, individual dorm controls, blackout-capable lighting, and a commercial kitchen that feeds a crew at 3 a.m. Decon sits between the worlds — extractors, showers, and gear storage with the plumbing and ventilation to handle contaminated equipment. Emergency power covers alerting, doors, communications, and life safety, because the station is the building that has to work when nothing else does.

What can change the scope and cost

Apparatus count sets the bay size and exhaust scope. Combined police-fire facilities add security layers. And station location — urban infill vs. suburban greenfield — changes the site and community interface. Program the apparatus, the crew size, and the decon standard first; the building follows.

  • Apparatus fleet: more and larger trucks mean bigger bays, more exhaust drops, larger doors
  • Crew size: staffing levels drive dorm, kitchen, and plumbing fixture counts
  • Decon standard: basic gear laundry vs. full hot-warm-cold separation changes plumbing and HVAC
  • Combined facilities: shared stations with police or EMS add secure separation requirements
  • Community rooms: public meeting space adds assembly occupancy and after-hours access design

What else do project teams ask?

What is a vehicle exhaust capture system?
It's a system of hoses or rails that connects directly to each apparatus tailpipe and captures diesel exhaust at the source, venting it outside the building. When the truck leaves, the hose disconnects automatically. Without capture, every engine start fills the bay with diesel particulate — which then migrates into the living quarters through every door opening and duct. Given what we now know about firefighter cancer risk, exhaust capture isn't a luxury; our engineers treat it as a health-critical system and design the bay ventilation around it.
Why do fire stations need decontamination areas?
Because turnout gear comes back from fires carrying carcinogens, and the old practice of tossing it in a corner of the bay contaminates the station. Modern stations have dedicated decon: extractors (specialized washers) for turnout gear, showers so crews can clean up before entering living areas, and a layout that enforces the hot-warm-cold zone separation — contaminated gear never crosses into clean living space. The plumbing design serves the extractors and showers, and the HVAC keeps the zones separated so contaminants don't ride the air into the dorms.
Does this guarantee permit approval?
No. Engineering documents support a defined project scope, while the authority having jurisdiction controls its interpretation, completeness decision, review queue, and approval.
What should I send for an initial review?
Send the project address, plain-language scope, current drawings, existing-condition records, relevant calculations or comments, schedule, and the authority or code information already available. The responsible engineer will identify gaps.
