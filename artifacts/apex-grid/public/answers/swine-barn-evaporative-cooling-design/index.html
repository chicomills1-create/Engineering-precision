How Is Swine Barn Evaporative Cooling Designed for Summer?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Swine barn evaporative cooling is engineered around the tunnel airflow: cooling pads or high-pressure fogging sized to the design air volume, water supply and drainage designed for continuous summer duty and the farm's water quality, and controls that stage cooling in before heat stress begins.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Cooling Pad Sizing and Water Systems

Pad area is a function of airflow. The engineer takes the barn's summer design airflow and divides by the target pad face velocity to get the required pad square footage, then fits that area into the inlet wall with distribution and framing. Pad depth — typically four to six inches of cellulose — sets the cooling effectiveness and the static pressure the fans must overcome, and the engineer adds that pressure to the fan selection so tunnel performance does not sag when pads are wet. The water system is a small process plant. A sump collects recirculated water, pumps lift it to the distribution header, and gutters return it — the engineer sizes pumps for the distribution flow plus head, specifies sump capacity for the recirculation volume, and designs makeup and bleed-off to control mineral concentration. In freezing climates the engineer details drain-down: pads, sumps, and piping that empty completely so nothing bursts in winter. Algae and scale management — shading, treatment, cleanable sumps — is in the design because a neglected pad system grows biology that cuts airflow and cooling together.

Fogging Systems and Humidity Control

High-pressure fogging puts cooling where pads cannot go. The engineer lays out nozzle lines to cover the air path or the animal zone, selects nozzle flow and droplet size for rapid evaporation, and sizes the pump station for the total nozzle count at the required pressure with filtration ahead of the pump. Water quality is non-negotiable: the design includes filtration and, where the well water warrants it, softening or treatment, because a plugged nozzle is a dead zone in the cooling pattern. Humidity sensing keeps fogging honest. Evaporative cooling only works when the air can accept more moisture, so the engineer adds humidity sensors that inhibit or stage back fogging as relative humidity climbs. The control sequence runs fogging in stages tied to temperature with humidity override — cooling the barn on a humid heat wave by adding more water is how floors get slick and pigs stay hot. The engineer also details the drainage for any carryover and keeps electrical devices clear of the fogging zones, since fine mist and electricity are a poor combination.

Swine Barn Evaporative Cooling Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the evaporative cooling design is finalized: • Pad area sized to summer design airflow at effective face velocity • Pad static pressure included in tunnel fan selection • Sump, pump, distribution, and gutter system sized for continuous recirculation • Makeup and bleed-off designed for the farm's water mineral content • Drain-down detailing for freezing climates • Fogging nozzle layout, pump station, and filtration sized for total nozzle count • Water treatment specified where well quality threatens nozzles or pads • Humidity sensors with override inhibiting fogging at high relative humidity • Cooling staged ahead of heat stress in the controller sequence • Cooling interlocked with ventilation staging — no water without airflow • Pumps and controls on circuits that ride through with the ventilation system

What else do project teams ask?

How much can evaporative cooling drop barn temperature?
In hot, dry weather, 10 to 20 degrees off the incoming air is realistic with well-designed pads. The drier the air, the bigger the drop — evaporative cooling follows the wet-bulb depression. In humid heat waves the benefit shrinks, which is why the engineer designs the system for the local climate's design conditions and adds humidity override on fogging systems.
Cooling pads or fogging — which is better?
Pads are the standard for tunnel-ventilated barns: simple, robust, and effective across the whole inlet airstream. Fogging suits barns where pads are impractical or as a second stage for extreme heat, but it demands excellent water quality and humidity control. The engineer selects based on the barn's ventilation type, water quality, and climate, and sometimes designs both in stages.
Does hard well water ruin evaporative cooling?
It will if the design ignores it. Recirculating pad systems concentrate minerals, scaling pads and plugging distribution; fogging nozzles plug even faster. The engineer designs bleed-off and makeup rates for the water analysis, specifies treatment where needed, and makes sumps and distribution cleanable. Water quality is a design input, not an afterthought.
Should cooling run before the barn gets hot?
Yes — staging cooling in ahead of heat stress is the whole point. The control sequence wets pads and starts fogging stages as temperature climbs toward the stress threshold, not after pigs are already suffering. The engineer writes these setpoints into the controller program with the farm's genetics and production targets in mind, since heat-stress thresholds vary.
