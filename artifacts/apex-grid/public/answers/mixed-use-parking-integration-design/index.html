How Is Parking Integrated Into Mixed-Use Development Design?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Mixed-use parking is engineered as one shared garage sized by combined-peak demand analysis, structured to carry the building above, zoned by user group with access control, and equipped with ventilation, drainage, and managed EV charging for all uses at once.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Shared-Parking Analysis and Stall Allocation

The engineer builds the demand model from the ground up: gross floor area or unit count per use, the jurisdiction's base parking ratios, adjustments for transit proximity and walkability, and hourly demand curves for each use across a full week. The curves are overlaid to find the true combined peak — often a weekday midday when office and retail overlap, or a Friday evening when residential and restaurant demand coincide. The garage stall count is set at that peak plus a small operational cushion, and the engineer presents the analysis in the format the planning department expects, since shared-parking reductions are approved on methodology, not assertion. Allocation inside the garage is a separate design. Residents get secured, assigned zones — usually the levels closest to the residential elevators, gated and camera-covered. Office tenants get reserved zones with their own access credentials. Retail and restaurant visitors get the most accessible public zones near the retail elevators and stairs. The engineer lays out the zones so each user group reaches its elevators without crossing another group's secured area, designs the signage and wayfinding for first-time visitors, and sizes the access-control and revenue-control equipment — gates, ticket dispensers, license-plate readers — for the peak entry and exit rates the analysis predicts.

Garage Ventilation, Drainage, and EV Infrastructure

Ventilation design starts with the code's requirement — typically a set number of air changes per hour or a carbon-monoxide-sensor-driven system — and the engineer usually designs the sensor-driven approach for energy efficiency, with fans staging up as CO levels rise. Exhaust is collected at the low points where fumes accumulate and discharged at the roof or at louvers positioned away from residential windows, balconies, and air intakes. Makeup air is introduced to sweep the space, and the engineer verifies the garage stays negative relative to the occupied floors above so exhaust never migrates into the building through elevator shafts and stair enclosures. EV charging is sized as infrastructure, not as an afterthought. The engineer provides conduit and panel capacity for the full EV-ready stall count the code requires, plus empty conduit for future expansion, and designs a load-management system that rotates available capacity among plugged-in vehicles so the electrical service stays reasonable. Each charging zone is metered separately where the cost allocation requires it — residential EV charging billed to the association, public charging on a revenue meter. Drainage is sloped to trench drains and oil-water separators, and the structural engineer details the slab joints and sealants so the drainage system does not become a maintenance failure in five years.

Mixed-Use Parking Integration Checklist

Use this checklist before the garage design is finalized: • Shared-parking demand model completed by hour and day, with the combined peak documented • Stall count justified to the jurisdiction with published ratios and transit adjustments • Garage structured for transfer loads from the building above, with waterproofing at the podium top • Resident, office, and public zones separated with access control and dedicated elevator cores • CO-sensor-driven ventilation with exhaust routed clear of residential intakes and windows • EV-ready conduit and panel capacity for the code-required count plus growth, with load management • Drainage sloped to trench drains and oil-water separators; slab joints sealed • Fire access, headroom, and turning radii verified for the design vehicle on every level

What else do project teams ask?

How many fewer stalls does shared parking actually save?
Typically 20 to 30 percent versus the sum of the individual use requirements, though it varies with the mix. An office-residential project saves more than a retail-restaurant project, because office and residential peaks barely overlap while restaurant and residential evening peaks coincide. The engineer proves the specific project's savings with the hourly demand model — the jurisdiction approves the number the model supports, not a rule of thumb.
Can residents be guaranteed a parking spot in a shared garage?
Yes, through design and legal structure. Residents get assigned stalls in a secured, gated zone with their own elevator core, and the condominium documents or leases guarantee the assignment. The shared-parking analysis accounts for the assigned residential stalls as a fixed demand, and the public and office zones flex around them. The engineer lays out the gating, cameras, and access credentials so the residential zone is genuinely secured, not just signed.
Where does EV charging load show up in the electrical design?
As one of the largest new loads in the building. A garage with a hundred EV-ready stalls can represent hundreds of kilowatts if every stall drew full power at once — which is why the engineer designs load management that staggers charging across the plugged-in vehicles. The service is sized for the managed peak, not the arithmetic total, and the distribution includes the conduit, panels, and metering the jurisdiction's EV-ready percentages require.
Does county review treat mixed-use parking differently?
The ratios may be similar but the reviewer and the constraints differ. An unincorporated county project often faces a fire district that sets its own fire-lane widths and turning radii through the garage and site, and where the site is on septic, the county may limit the total development intensity — which caps the parking demand the project can generate. The engineer confirms the county's parking ratios, the fire district's access requirements, and any intensity caps before the garage size is set.
