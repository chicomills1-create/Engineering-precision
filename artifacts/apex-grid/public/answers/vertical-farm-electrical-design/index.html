How Is Electrical Design Done for Vertical Farm Operations?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Vertical farm electrical design sizes the service to the true connected lighting and HVAC load, distributes power in independently controlled grow zones, isolates controls from power interference, and backs up the pumps, controls, and critical climate equipment that keep crops alive through an outage.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Service Sizing for High-Density Lighting Loads

The load calculation is the foundation everything else stands on. The engineer builds the panel schedules from the lighting layout — fixture input watts per tier, times tiers, times zones — then layers in HVAC at design-day cooling, dehumidification, pumps, and miscellaneous loads. Because lighting is the dominant load and it is essentially continuous during photoperiods, the engineer treats it as continuous load per code, which affects conductor and breaker sizing. The resulting service size often lands in a range that requires utility coordination well beyond a standard commercial tenant improvement, including transformer upgrades and new switchgear. Power quality gets attention because LED drivers are nonlinear loads. Large quantities of drivers can introduce harmonics that overheat neutrals and transformers, so the engineer evaluates harmonic content, specifies appropriately rated transformers and oversized neutrals where needed, and may require power-factor-corrected drivers in the fixture specification. Metering is designed per zone or per major load category so the operator can see exactly what the lighting, HVAC, and pumps each cost — that visibility is what lets the farm tune photoperiods and climate strategies against the actual electric bill.

Distribution, Zoning, and Backup Power

The one-line diagram reads like the farm's operating plan. Lighting contactor panels sit near the zones they serve to keep homeruns short, each zone independently scheduled so a vegetative room and a flowering room can run different photoperiods without compromise. The engineer provides local disconnects at each rack row or room for maintenance — relamping or servicing a tier should never require de-energizing the whole farm — and emergency lighting and exit signage follow the life-safety code through grow areas, corridors, and exits just like any commercial occupancy. Backup power design starts with a failure-mode walkthrough: what dies first, and what does the crop lose? Irrigation and fertigation pumps, environmental controllers, sensors, and alarms are the non-negotiable backup loads; the engineer adds critical dehumidification or cooling for high-value rooms as the budget allows. An automatic transfer switch moves these loads to generator without human intervention, and the controls are programmed to shed non-critical loads and hold a survival climate rather than trying to run full production on backup power. The generator is exercised on a schedule and its alarms report to the same monitoring system as the grow controls, so a failed exercise test is caught before the outage that matters.

Vertical Farm Electrical Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the electrical permit set is finalized: • Complete load inventory built from actual fixture input watts, HVAC, pumps, and controls, with lighting treated as continuous load • Service size coordinated with the utility, including transformer and switchgear upgrades with realistic lead times • Harmonic evaluation for LED driver loads, with transformer, neutral, and power-factor requirements specified • Lighting distribution zoned per tier level or grow room with independent contactor panels and photoperiod scheduling • Local disconnects at each rack row or room so maintenance never de-energizes the whole farm • Backup power scoped to crop-survival loads: pumps, controls, sensors, alarms, and critical climate equipment • Automatic transfer with load-shedding sequence that holds a survival climate on backup power • Zone-level metering on lighting, HVAC, and pumps so operating cost is visible per system

What else do project teams ask?

How large an electrical service does a vertical farm need?
Much larger than the building's previous use suggests — the lighting load alone often exceeds the entire prior electrical demand of a warehouse shell several times over. The engineer sizes the service from the fixture input watts across all tiers plus HVAC, dehumidification, and pumps, with lighting treated as continuous load. Utility coordination for the transformer and service upgrade should start during design, not after permits, because the lead time can drive the schedule.
Why zone the lighting circuits instead of running one big system?
Because the farm operates in zones. Different crops and growth stages need different photoperiods and dimming recipes, maintenance requires shutting down one area without darkening the rest, and troubleshooting is far faster when a fault is isolated to a zone. Zoned contactor panels also let the controls stage startup so the whole farm's lights never hit the service at the same instant.
What absolutely must stay powered during an outage?
The systems whose failure kills crops fastest: irrigation and fertigation pumps, environmental controllers and sensors, alarm and monitoring systems, and at least partial climate control for the highest-value rooms. Full production lighting is almost never on backup power — the generator could not sensibly carry it. The engineer designs a survival mode that keeps plants alive until utility power returns.
Do LED grow lights create power quality problems?
They can at farm scale. LED drivers are nonlinear loads that generate harmonics, and hundreds of fixtures on one service can overheat shared neutrals and transformers if the design ignores it. The engineer evaluates the harmonic profile, specifies K-rated transformers and adequately sized neutrals where warranted, and may require power-factor-corrected drivers. This is standard industrial power design applied to a horticultural load.
