How Are EV Charging Hubs Engineered in Arizona's Extreme Heat?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Arizona EV charging hubs are engineered as high-power electrical installations: service sized for coincident fast-charging load with utility-coordinated transformers, shade canopies and thermal design so chargers deliver full power at 115 degrees, battery storage for demand management, and accessible, well-lit, well-drained stall layouts.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Electrical Service and Utility Coordination

The engineer builds the load model dispenser by dispenser, then applies coincidence factors grounded in charging-behavior data rather than generic commercial demand factors — fast chargers at a highway hub coincide far more than office receptacles do. The one-line diagram shows the full distribution: utility transformer, main switchgear, distribution to charger cabinets and dispensers, metering, and the battery storage interconnection. Fault-current and arc-flash studies protect the design, and the grounding system is engineered for the large fault currents a multi-megawatt service can produce. Utility engagement starts at concept. The engineer submits the load letter and preliminary one-line to the serving utility, tracks the system impact study, and designs around the utility's answers: service voltage, transformer location and pad requirements, metering configuration, and any upstream upgrades. Long-lead equipment — switchgear, transformers — is ordered against the utility's timeline, not the construction schedule's wishes. The engineer also evaluates rate structures with the owner, because demand charges on a multi-megawatt peak can exceed the energy charges, and battery storage plus managed charging can reshape that peak profitably.

Heat, Shade, and Site Design

Thermal design starts with the equipment's ratings. The engineer collects the ambient-temperature derating curves for the selected chargers, compares them against the site's design temperature, and proves the hub delivers its advertised power on the hottest design day — upsizing equipment or adding cooling where the curves demand it. Shade canopies are structural as well as thermal: the engineer designs the canopy structure for wind loads, sizes footings for the overturning a big sail produces in monsoon gusts, and lays out the rooftop solar for the structural capacity the canopy provides. The site layout serves drivers first. The engineer separates charging circulation from through traffic, provides pull-through stalls for towing vehicles, and designs accessible stalls with the correct clearances, reach ranges, and accessible routes to amenities. Lighting photometrics prove safe nighttime levels without light trespass onto neighbors, and security cameras and emergency call stations are placed with the owner's operations plan. Drainage grading keeps every equipment pad above ponding, conduit runs are detailed for the corrosive desert soil conditions, and bollards protect every dispenser and cabinet from the vehicles they serve.

Arizona EV Charging Hub Checklist

Use this checklist before the permit set is issued: • Coincident load modeled from the dispenser mix; service, switchgear, and distribution sized • Utility engaged at concept: load letter, impact study, transformer lead time tracked • Charger derating curves verified against the site's design temperature • Shade canopies designed structurally with solar capacity; equipment sited for airflow • Battery storage evaluated for demand-charge management and outage ride-through • Accessible stalls, pull-through stalls, lighting, and security designed for drivers • Drainage grading keeping equipment pads clear of monsoon ponding • Permitting tracked on both paths: building jurisdiction (city or county) and utility interconnection

What else do project teams ask?

How much power does an EV charging hub need?
Far more than the building it sits next to. A hub with ten 350-kilowatt fast chargers can approach 3 megawatts of coincident demand — the equivalent of a mid-size office building — before adding Level 2 stalls, lighting, and battery storage. The engineer sizes the service from a coincidence analysis of the actual dispenser mix, because fast chargers coincide much more than typical commercial loads. This is why utility coordination starts at concept: a service this large needs the utility's study, transformer, and timeline.
Do EV chargers work in 115-degree heat?
They work, but they derate — delivering less than nameplate power — unless the thermal design accounts for it. The engineer checks each charger's ambient-temperature derating curve against the site's design temperature and designs shade canopies, equipment siting for airflow, and enclosure cooling so the hub delivers its advertised charging speeds through August. A hub designed for a mild climate and dropped into Phoenix will disappoint drivers on exactly the days they need it most.
Can solar canopies offset a charging hub's load?
Partially, and the economics are attractive. A canopy over a dozen stalls can carry a meaningful solar array — often hundreds of kilowatts — that shaves the site's peak demand and cuts energy purchases during the sunny hours when charging demand is high. The engineer sizes the array to the canopy structure, models the production against the charging load profile, and pairs it with battery storage where demand-charge savings justify it. Solar does not eliminate the utility service, but it materially shrinks the bill.
Does the electric utility territory change the project?
Yes. The Phoenix metro is split between APS and SRP territories, and the two utilities have different service requirements, metering standards, interconnection processes, and rate structures. The engineer identifies the serving utility from the parcel location, designs the service entrance and metering to that utility's rulebook, and engages its new-service process at concept stage — because the utility's study and transformer procurement set the project's critical path.
