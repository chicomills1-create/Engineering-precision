How Are Courtroom AV and Evidence Systems Engineered Today?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design integrated evidence presentation with displays for judge, jury, and witness, speech reinforcement with assisted listening and recording feeds, plus video arraignment capability — all run from simple presets.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Evidence Presentation and Display Design

The evidence presentation workflow drives the video design. Counsel tables get connection points — HDMI and wireless presentation — plus a document camera for physical exhibits. From there, a matrix switcher routes any source to any display: the judge's bench monitor, the witness monitor, individual or shared jury displays, and a large flat panel or projection screen for the gallery. The engineer sizes displays so the back row of the jury box can read document text, which sets minimum screen sizes and viewing distances. Signal infrastructure is planned like building infrastructure, not an afterthought: conduit and floor boxes at counsel tables, rack space in a secured AV closet, and network drops for wireless presentation gateways. Annotation capability lets attorneys mark up exhibits live, and a confidence monitor at the clerk's station shows exactly what the jury sees. Everything is specified for reliability over novelty — a failed switcher mid-trial is a mistrial risk, so the design favors proven components with local service support.

Audio, Assisted Listening, and the Official Record

Courtroom audio serves three masters at once: reinforcement so everyone hears, assistive listening for accessibility, and the recording that becomes the official record. The DSP-based design assigns microphones to zones — bench, witness, counsel, jury — with automixing that opens only the active microphone, which keeps background noise and feedback under control. Separate mix buses feed the room speakers, the hearing loop or IR assisted listening system, and the court reporter's recorder, so each gets the right balance. Microphone placement is coordinated with the millwork: recessed or low-profile mics that do not block sightlines or the camera's view of the witness. The engineer verifies speech intelligibility targets during commissioning with the room fully furnished, because empty-room measurements lie. Backup recording — independent of the primary — is standard practice, since the transcript is the product the whole system exists to protect.

Courtroom AV Design Checklist

A complete courtroom AV package covers presentation, audio, and remote capability: • Evidence presentation routing from counsel tables to bench, witness, jury, and gallery displays • Display sizing verified for document legibility from the back of the jury box • DSP-based speech reinforcement with zoned automixing and feedback suppression • Assisted listening system (hearing loop or infrared) covering all seating • Independent recording feeds for the court reporter plus a backup recorder • Video arraignment cameras, lighting, and network reliability for remote proceedings • Touch-panel presets so court staff can operate the room without a technician

What else do project teams ask?

What is an evidence presentation system?
It is the AV backbone that lets attorneys show exhibits to everyone who needs to see them at the same time. Sources at the counsel tables — laptops, document cameras, media players — route through a switcher to displays at the judge's bench, the witness stand, the jury box, and a large courtroom display. The clerk gets a confidence monitor showing exactly what the jury sees. The design goal is simple: no participant should have to guess what an exhibit shows.
Why do courtrooms need assisted listening systems?
Accessibility law and basic fairness require that participants with hearing loss can follow proceedings fully. Hearing loops or infrared systems deliver the courtroom's audio mix directly to hearing aids or headsets, bypassing room reverberation and distance. The engineer designs coverage for every seat — including the jury box and witness stand — and the system is tested with the room in its normal operating condition, not empty.
How is courtroom audio recorded for the official record?
The DSP provides a dedicated recording mix, separate from what the room speakers play, optimized for speech clarity rather than room fill. That feed goes to the court reporter's equipment and to a backup recorder — redundancy matters because the transcript is the legal product. Microphone discipline is part of the design: automixing opens only active mics, and placement keeps every speaker on-mic without blocking sightlines.
What does video arraignment require from the building?
Video arraignment needs courtroom cameras with proper framing and lighting, far-end audio that is intelligible over the connection, and — critically — a network path with the bandwidth and reliability the proceeding depends on. The engineer designs redundant network connectivity where the jurisdiction requires it, acoustic treatment so the far end hears a clean signal, and a simple control preset so staff can switch between in-person and video modes without reconfiguring the room.
