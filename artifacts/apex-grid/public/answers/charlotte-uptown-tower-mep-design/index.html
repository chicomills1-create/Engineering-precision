How Is MEP Designed for a Charlotte Uptown Tower?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

MEP design for a Charlotte Uptown tower starts with an electrical load study and utility coordination with Duke Energy, sizes chilled-water or VRF cooling for the tower's stack effect and high internal loads, zones plumbing and ventilation by floor use, and routes everything through a congested vertical core, permitted through Charlotte's development center, with county review for unincorporated sites.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Electrical Service and Emergency Power

Sizing electrical service for a tower is an exercise in realistic estimation. The engineer lists every load, from tenant lighting and receptacles to elevators, mechanical equipment, and commercial kitchens, then applies code demand factors that recognize not everything runs at once. The resulting service size drives the utility coordination: available fault current, transformer vault dimensions, and service entrance routing through a downtown site with limited street frontage. Getting Duke Energy's requirements locked early prevents the late discovery that the vault does not fit or the service lateral cannot reach it. Emergency and standby power are layered by criticality. Life-safety loads, egress lighting, fire alarm, fire pump, and at least one elevator, get emergency power with fast automatic transfer. Legally required standby covers smoke control and other systems the code demands but with more tolerant timing. Optional standby serves the owner's chosen loads, often including data rooms, sump pumps, and select tenant panels. The engineer sizes generators for the largest starting motor plus running load, plans fuel storage and exhaust routing, and specifies the full start-and-transfer test sequence that commissioning will later verify.

Cooling, Ventilation, and the Stack Effect

Charlotte's hot, humid summers make cooling the dominant HVAC load, and a tower concentrates that load vertically. The engineer typically chooses between a central chilled-water plant with cooling towers and floor-by-floor VRF or water-source heat pumps, weighing first cost, tenant metering, and redundancy. Either way, ventilation air is usually handled by dedicated outdoor-air units that dehumidify Charlotte's muggy intake air before it reaches the floors, because humidity control matters as much as temperature for comfort and for keeping the envelope dry. Stack effect is the tall-building phenomenon the design must tame. In winter, warm indoor air rises through shafts and stairs, pressurizing the top of the building and pulling cold air in at the bottom; in summer the pattern weakens but never fully disappears. The engineer models these pressures, compartmentalizes shafts, specifies revolving doors or vestibules at the lobby, and balances the ventilation system so upper floors are not over-pressurized while lower floors starve. Envelope commissioning, testing the curtain wall and air barriers, closes the loop, because no mechanical system can overcome a leaky tower.

What else do project teams ask?

How is electrical service sized for a high-rise in Charlotte?
The engineer performs a load study totaling every connected load, then applies National Electrical Code demand factors to find the realistic peak. That number sizes the service entrance, main switchgear, and the utility transformer, all coordinated with Duke Energy for capacity, vault space, and fault current. Generators are sized separately for emergency, legally required standby, and optional standby loads, with the largest motor start setting the generator's required capacity.
What is stack effect and why does it matter in tower HVAC design?
Stack effect is air movement driven by temperature differences over the building's height: warm air rises through shafts and stairs, pressurizing upper floors and depressurizing lower ones. In a tower it can slam lobby doors, whistle through elevator shafts, and unbalance ventilation so some floors get too much air and others too little. The engineer counters with shaft compartmentalization, vestibules, pressure zoning, and a ventilation balance proven during commissioning.
How are plumbing systems zoned in a tall building?
Municipal water pressure cannot reach the top of a tower, so the engineer splits domestic water into pressure zones, each served by booster pumps lifting water to its floors, with pressure-reducing valves protecting lower fixtures in each zone from excessive pressure. Sanitary drainage stacks run the full height through the core with proper venting to prevent trap seal loss, and the design separates grease waste, lab waste, and storm drainage into their own systems where the occupancy requires it.
Where do Uptown tower MEP permits get reviewed?
Towers inside the Charlotte city limits go through the City of Charlotte's development center, which coordinates building, electrical, mechanical, plumbing, and fire reviews in one submittal. Projects on unincorporated Mecklenburg County land follow the county's permitting path instead. Either way, reviewers expect complete load calculations, riser diagrams, and coordinated drawings, and the AHJ makes the final decision on every permit.
