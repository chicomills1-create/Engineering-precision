How Is a Desert Solar Farm Substation Engineered?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A desert solar farm substation is engineered around a medium-voltage collection network, power transformers sized for hot-day derated capacity with oil containment, grounding designed for high-resistivity desert soils, monsoon-grade lightning protection, dust-hardened equipment, and utility interconnection that typically sets the project schedule.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Transformers, Switchgear, and Protection

The engineer selects the main power transformer for the plant's full AC output at the hot-day derated rating, then adds the margin the interconnection agreement and the owner's availability target require. Impedance is chosen to limit fault current to what the downstream switchgear can interrupt while keeping voltage regulation within the utility's limits. The protection scheme — differential protection on the transformer, overcurrent and ground-fault on the feeders, breaker-failure backup — is coordinated with time-current curves so a fault is cleared by the nearest device, and the relay settings are calculated, not defaulted. Medium-voltage switchgear lives in a controlled environment in the desert: a prefabricated control building with filtered air conditioning, because dust and 120-degree heat inside a metal enclosure destroy electronic relays and shorten breaker life. The engineer sizes the building HVAC for the internal heat load of the gear plus the solar gain, specifies the filtration for dust season, and lays out the building so technicians can rack breakers and pull cable with safe clearances. DC arc-flash and AC arc-flash studies are completed for the whole substation so the operations team has the labels and the personal-protective-equipment requirements before energization.

Grounding, Lightning, and Site Civil

The ground grid design starts with Wenner four-pin soil-resistivity testing across the substation footprint, ideally in the dry season when resistivity is worst. The engineer models the grid for the available fault current, checks mesh and step voltages against the safety criteria, and extends the grid under the control building, the transformer containment, and the fence — with the fence bonded so a fault cannot energize it. Ground wells or chemical electrodes go where the native soil will not deliver the needed resistance, and the design includes test points so the owner can verify the grid over the plant's life. Site civil protects the electrical investment. The substation pad is graded above the local flood elevation with positive drainage away from equipment; access roads are designed for transformer delivery loads and all-weather maintenance access, with culverts or dips where the road crosses desert washes. Dust control during construction and operations — stabilized surfaces, wind fencing where needed — keeps the switchgear filters from loading prematurely. The engineer coordinates the grading, drainage, and erosion control with the county permit, since desert jurisdictions review stormwater management closely even on remote sites.

Desert Solar Substation Checklist

Use this checklist before the substation design is issued for construction: • Collection system routed and sized for full-load current, voltage drop, and protection coordination • Power transformer sized for hot-day derated capacity with oil containment and fire protection • Protection scheme coordinated with time-current curves; relay settings calculated • Ground grid designed from seasonal soil-resistivity testing; step-and-touch potentials verified • Lightning and surge protection designed for monsoon-season strike exposure • Switchgear and controls housed in filtered, air-conditioned buildings for dust and heat • Arc-flash studies completed with labels and PPE requirements before energization • Substation pad above flood elevation; access designed for transformer delivery and wash crossings • Utility interconnection requirements and study queue confirmed at project inception

What else do project teams ask?

Why does desert heat reduce a transformer's capacity?
Transformers are rated at standard ambient conditions, and their insulation life depends on staying below temperature limits. When the ambient air is 115 degrees instead of the standard rating condition, there is less thermal headroom — so the safe continuous loading drops. The engineer sizes the transformer for its derated capacity at the site's design temperature, which often means specifying a larger nameplate unit than the plant's output would suggest at first glance.
What makes desert soil difficult for substation grounding?
Dry, rocky, sandy soils have high electrical resistivity, which makes it hard to achieve the low ground-grid resistance that protective relaying and personnel safety require. The engineer tests resistivity at multiple depths and in dry-season conditions, then designs around it with larger grids, deep-driven rods, ground wells, or engineered backfill — and verifies the design with step-and-touch potential calculations for the actual fault current.
How long does utility interconnection take?
Often longer than construction. The interconnection study process — feasibility, system impact, and facilities studies — runs through the utility's queue and can take many months to over a year depending on the utility and the queue depth. The engineer initiates utility coordination at project inception with preliminary one-lines and site data, because the energization date is usually set by the interconnection process, not by how fast the substation can be built.
Who permits a solar farm substation in the desert?
Typically the county, since most utility-scale desert sites are in unincorporated county land. The county reviews grading, drainage, and electrical plans together, and the substation's stormwater management gets close scrutiny even on remote sites. Separately, the interconnecting utility — APS, SRP, Tucson Electric Power, or an out-of-state utility — enforces its own interconnection standards for relaying, metering, and testing, which the engineer designs to from the start.
