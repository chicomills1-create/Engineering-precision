How Is Utility Metering Designed for Mixed-Use Buildings?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Utility metering for mixed-use is engineered as a complete measurement architecture: utility meters per occupancy at each service entrance, submeters on every shared system, and meter locations and types matched to the building's legal cost-allocation structure.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Service Entrances and Utility Coordination

The engineer coordinates with each utility company before the electrical room is drawn. Electric utilities publish service requirements — meter socket types, CT cabinet dimensions, working clearances, acceptable service voltages — and the engineer lays out the service entrance to match, because a service that does not meet the utility's requirements does not get energized. Where occupancies get separate services, the engineer verifies the utility will actually provide multiple services to one building; some utilities insist on a single service point and leave the splitting to submeters. Gas and water utilities have their own service rules. Gas meter assemblies need ventilation, protection from vehicle impact, and access for reading and maintenance — the engineer sites them accordingly, often on the building's exterior or in a dedicated meter room. Water services need backflow preventers sized to the hazard, meter pits or rooms the water department can access, and separate fire-service connections where the fire flow exceeds what the domestic meter can pass. The engineer schedules a pre-design meeting with each utility to lock the service arrangement, since utility lead times for new services can run months and sit on the project's critical path.

Submetering and Cost Allocation

The submetering design mirrors the legal documents. The engineer reads the condo declaration or the lease exhibits to learn exactly which costs are shared and in what proportions, then places meters to measure those splits: kilowatt-hour submeters on each occupancy's feeders, BTU meters on the chilled- and heating-water branches serving each use, water submeters on the retail and residential domestic branches, gas submeters on restaurant branches. Every shared load — garage exhaust fans, site lighting, common corridors, the central plant's auxiliaries — gets its own meter or a defined allocation formula the meters support. The building automation system ties it together where the owner wants it. Submeters with communication outputs feed a central dashboard showing each occupancy's consumption in real time, which serves both billing and energy management — the engineer can see the retail's after-hours load or the residential common-area waste and act on it. The engineer specifies the meter communication protocol, the network infrastructure, and the data the owner needs for the allocation calculations, and commissions the metering so the readings are accurate from day one. A meter that reads wrong is worse than no meter, because it bills wrong with authority.

Mixed-Use Utility Metering Checklist

Use this checklist before the metering design is finalized: • Service arrangement per utility confirmed: separate services vs single service with submetering • Utility meter sockets, CT cabinets, and clearances laid out per each utility's service requirements • Gas meter assemblies sited for ventilation, protection, and utility access • Water services with backflow preventers, accessible metering, and separate fire connections • Submeters on every occupancy feeder and every shared system, matching the legal cost splits • BTU meters on hydronic branches serving each occupancy from shared central plants • Meter communication network specified and tied to the building automation system • Metering one-line on the drawings showing every meter, its type, and what it measures

What else do project teams ask?

Can each apartment have its own electric meter?
Usually yes for the dwelling units — utilities commonly meter apartments individually, either with utility-owned meters in a meter bank or with owner-installed submeters billed through the association. The engineer designs the meter bank or submeter locations with utility access, and separates the house loads — corridors, elevators, garage — onto their own meter so common-area consumption never mixes with unit consumption.
How is a restaurant's gas usage separated from the building?
With a dedicated gas meter on the restaurant's branch, sized for the commercial cooking load. The engineer routes a separate metered branch from the gas service to the restaurant space, with the meter located for utility access and the piping sized for the full connected appliance load. The restaurant's gas bill then reflects its actual burners, not an allocation formula — which matters because a busy kitchen's gas use dwarfs a residence's.
What if the utility only allows one electric service?
Then the building is master-metered and the engineer designs a complete submetering system: a main utility meter at the single service plus submeters on each occupancy's distribution. The owner becomes the billing entity, reading the submeters and invoicing tenants per the lease terms. The engineering is actually more involved than multiple services, because every split the leases promise has to be measurable in the field.
Do submeters need to be utility-grade accurate?
They need to be accurate enough for billing, which usually means revenue-grade metering with published accuracy classes. Many jurisdictions regulate submetering for tenant billing — requiring approved meter types, transparent billing formulas, and sometimes caps on the rates charged. The engineer specifies revenue-grade meters and confirms the local submetering rules before the owner starts billing from them.
