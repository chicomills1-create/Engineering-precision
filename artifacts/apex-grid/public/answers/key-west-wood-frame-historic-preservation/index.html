How Are Key West Wood-Frame Homes Preserved?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Key West's historic wood-frame homes are preserved through structural assessment, termite and rot repair with in-kind lumber, hurricane-tie retrofits that keep the original appearance, and climate-appropriate coatings, all reviewed by the Historic Architectural Review Commission, which must approve exterior changes before a building permit can issue.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What Fails First in a Key West Wood-Frame House

The sill plate and floor framing fail first. These sit closest to the ground in a humid, termite-rich environment, and in many Old Town houses they have endured a century of moisture cycles. The assessment probes sills, rim joists, and porch framing with an awl or resistance drill, opens walls selectively to inspect studs and sheathing, and checks the masonry or wood piers that carry the raised floor. Termite damage often hides behind paint, so the engineer looks for the hollow sound and frass that signal active infestation and coordinates treatment before structural repair, since new lumber installed into an active termite zone will not last. Roof framing and connections are the second concern. Original cut nails and toe-nailed connections have limited uplift capacity, which is why older Key West houses lost roofs in hurricanes. The assessment documents the existing connections, measures member sizes and spans against current wind load demands, and identifies where hurricane ties, straps, and clips can be added. Foundation piers are checked for settlement, undermining, and deterioration, because a house that has settled differentially will keep cracking finishes until the foundation is stabilized.

Retrofits That Respect Historic Character

The guiding rule is that structural work should be invisible from the street. Hurricane ties and straps go inside attics and wall cavities, connecting rafters to wall plates and wall plates to floor framing to create the continuous load path the original builders never had. Sistered joists and replaced sills match the original dimensions so floor levels and trim profiles stay consistent. Where the commission allows, impact-rated windows can replace deteriorated originals, but the profiles, muntins, and shutters must match the historic appearance, which is why the engineer details the structural attachment behind historically accurate exteriors. Material choices matter as much as structural ones. Replacement lumber matches the original species and grade where available, with pressure-treated or naturally durable wood used at ground contact. Stainless steel connectors resist the salt air that would corrode ordinary galvanized hardware within years. Coatings are breathable systems that let the wood dry, because trapping moisture behind impermeable finishes accelerates rot. The result is a house that meets modern wind and safety expectations while reading, from the sidewalk, exactly as it did a century ago.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the Historic Architectural Review Commission?
The Historic Architectural Review Commission, known as HARC, is the City of Key West board that reviews exterior changes to properties in the historic district for compatibility with the area's historic character. It issues a certificate of appropriateness that must be in hand before the building department will issue a permit for exterior work. Its guidelines cover materials, proportions, details, and colors, so the engineer and architect design to those guidelines from the start.
Can I elevate a historic Key West home for flood protection?
Sometimes, but it is one of the hardest approvals to get. Elevation changes the home's relationship to the street and its historic proportions, which HARC scrutinizes closely, and FEMA floodplain rules may still require the elevation for substantial improvements. Successful projects typically lift the minimum needed, preserve the porch and stair configuration, and use lattice or historically compatible skirting below. The design has to satisfy the building code, the floodplain administrator, and HARC simultaneously.
How do you repair termite-damaged framing without replacing everything?
Sound wood stays; only the damaged portions are removed. Engineers specify sistering, where new lumber is bolted alongside the remaining sound portion of a joist or stud to restore capacity, or partial replacement with scarfed or lapped joints where the damage is localized. Epoxy consolidation can stabilize partially deteriorated members that still have structural value. Active termites are treated first, and the repair details use borate-treated or naturally resistant lumber so the fix lasts.
Do interior renovations need HARC approval?
Generally no. HARC's jurisdiction covers exterior appearance, what is visible from the public right-of-way. Interior structural work like sistering joists, adding hurricane ties inside attics, or upgrading electrical and plumbing typically needs only the building permit. But the line blurs at windows, doors, porches, and anything that changes the exterior, so the safe practice is to confirm with HARC staff before assuming interior-only work stays interior-only.
