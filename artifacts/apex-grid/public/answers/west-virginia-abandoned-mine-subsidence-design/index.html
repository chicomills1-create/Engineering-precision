How Do Engineers Design for Abandoned Mine Subsidence in West Virginia?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design for abandoned mine subsidence in West Virginia by locating old mine voids through records and drilling, then spanning the risk with deep foundations such as drilled piers to rock, articulated structural slabs, and grout-injected stabilization where voids threaten the building footprint.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Finding the Voids: The Mine Subsidence Investigation

The investigation starts with records: state mine map archives, company maps, and production records that show where seams were worked and at what depth. The engineer overlays the building footprint on the best available mapping and identifies the risk zones. Because old maps can be off by hundreds of feet or missing entirely, the next step is field verification - typically a grid of exploratory borings advanced to below the mined seam. A boring that drops suddenly or loses circulation has found a void; one that grinds through solid coal has found unmined ground. Geophysics can supplement drilling where access is limited. Electrical resistivity and microgravity surveys detect the density contrasts that voids create, helping place borings where they count instead of drilling blind. The engineer synthesizes maps, borings, and geophysics into a risk model: where voids are confirmed, where they are possible, and where the seam was never mined. That model sets the foundation strategy for each part of the building, because a site can be safe at one corner and undermined at the other.

Foundations That Survive a Collapse

The gold standard is a deep foundation to rock below the mined seam. Drilled piers are installed through the overburden and the mined horizon and socketed into competent rock beneath, with each pier designed for the full building load plus the downdrag of collapsing soils around the shaft. The structural slab spans between piers so the ground can move without taking the floor with it. Articulation joints in the structure let one bay settle without dragging the rest of the building down - a controlled hinge is far better than a rigid frame that cracks everywhere. Grouting is the alternative where piers are impractical or where an existing building sits over voids. Pressure grouting pumps a cementitious mix into the mine openings until refusal, filling rooms and supporting the roof rock; the engineer monitors pressures and volumes to confirm the voids are actually taking grout rather than channeling it away. Compaction grouting can densify caved rubble above collapsed workings. Both methods are followed by verification borings. The engineer also designs monitoring - settlement points surveyed over time - so any movement is caught while it is still a maintenance issue rather than an emergency.

What else do project teams ask?

What is mine subsidence and why is it a design issue in West Virginia?
Mine subsidence is the sinking or sudden collapse of the ground above abandoned underground coal mines. As old mine roofs deteriorate, voids can cave in, dropping the surface by inches or opening sinkholes. In West Virginia's coalfield counties, many homes and commercial buildings sit above workings mined a century ago, so engineers must investigate the voids and design foundations that do not depend on the undermined ground.
Can you build safely over an abandoned mine?
Yes, with the right investigation and foundation. Once the engineer knows where the voids are and how deep, deep foundations to solid rock below the mined seam carry the building independently of the undermined ground. Grouting can stabilize voids where foundations cannot practically bypass them. What is not safe is ignoring the mines and building on a conventional shallow foundation over unmapped workings.
How does an engineer tell whether a void has already collapsed?
Exploratory borings reveal the difference: an open void shows as a sudden drop of the drill string, while a collapsed and compacted void shows as broken rubble with some resistance. Geophysics can map the extent of low-density zones. The distinction matters because a collapsed void may only need settlement-tolerant design, while an open void demands foundations to rock or grout stabilization.
Does the building department require a mine subsidence study?
It depends on the authority having jurisdiction. Some coalfield counties and cities require subsidence investigations for permits in mapped mining areas; others do not ask, and the requirement then comes from the lender or insurer instead. Either way, the prudent course is the same: the engineer performs the investigation and designs the foundation for the voids, because the AHJ's permit decision does not change the geology under the building.
