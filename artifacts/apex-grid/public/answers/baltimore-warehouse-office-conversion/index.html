What Structural Work Converts a Baltimore Warehouse to Offices?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Baltimore warehouses convert to offices through structural assessment of existing floors for office loads, new lateral bracing or shear walls, diaphragm upgrades, added window openings with lintels, and stair-elevator cores - permitted through Baltimore City's permitting office with code-compliance review by the authority having jurisdiction.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Floors, Columns, and Concentrated Loads

The floor assessment starts with documentation: slab thickness, beam sizes, column grids, and the condition of every element. In early concrete warehouses the engineer checks for the era's typical details - plain round bars, light stirrups, and flat-slab construction with drop panels - and analyzes punching shear at each column under the new office loading. Timber warehouses get the same probing and grading treatment as mill buildings, with bearing pockets inspected for rot. The new program's heavy spots - electrical rooms, break rooms with stone counters, rooftop units - are mapped onto the framing plan so each concentrated load lands on a verified member. Where capacity falls short, strengthening is designed to the element. Concrete beams can take externally bonded fiber-reinforced polymer or added steel plates; slabs can receive a bonded concrete overlay that acts compositely with the original. Steel beams can be reinforced with cover plates, and timber members sistered or flitched. Each strengthening method is detailed with its anchorage and load-transfer mechanism, because a plate that cannot deliver its force into the member is decoration. The engineer also verifies the foundations for any net increase in building weight, including new cores and rooftop equipment.

Lateral Upgrades and New Openings

Most pre-war warehouses were designed for gravity alone, so the lateral upgrade is often the largest structural scope. The engineer establishes the design wind and seismic demands, evaluates what the existing building offers - usually very little - and designs a new lateral system: steel braced frames tucked into party walls, concrete shear walls at new cores, or moment frames where openness matters. Diaphragms are the critical link: wood floors get structural overlays, concrete slabs get new collectors and drag struts, and every connection from diaphragm to wall is detailed for the actual force. New openings serve daylight and egress, and each one is a structural event. Window openings in bearing masonry get steel lintels with bearing verified on the remaining wall; large storefront openings at the ground floor may need a new steel frame carrying the wall above. Where openings align vertically across floors, the engineer checks the piers between them as a perforated shear wall, since punching a full height of windows can convert a bearing wall into a row of slender piers. Openings are sequenced with shoring and temporary support so the wall above is never left hanging during construction.

What else do project teams ask?

Do warehouse floors need strengthening for office use?
Sometimes. Warehouse floors were often designed for heavy uniform loads, but offices bring concentrated loads - server rooms, file storage, and rooftop equipment - that can exceed the original design locally. The engineer analyzes each bay and each column for the new loading, including punching shear in concrete flat slabs. Strengthening with steel plates, fiber-reinforced overlays, or added beams is designed only where the analysis shows it is needed.
How are new windows added to old warehouse walls?
Each opening is designed before it is cut. The engineer sizes a steel lintel for the masonry and floor loads above, verifies bearing on the wall each side of the opening, and sequences the work with shoring so the wall is supported throughout. Where windows stack across floors, the remaining piers are checked as a perforated shear wall to confirm they still carry gravity and lateral loads.
What lateral upgrades do old warehouses need?
Most need an entirely new lateral system. Pre-war warehouses were typically designed for gravity loads only, with masonry infill or timber framing that was never detailed for wind or seismic forces. The engineer designs steel braced frames or concrete shear walls, strengthens the floor diaphragms to deliver forces to them, and details collectors and connections so the load path is complete from roof to foundation.
How does permitting differ between Baltimore City and Baltimore County?
They are separate authorities having jurisdiction. Baltimore City reviews conversions through its own permitting office with city-specific submittal requirements, while Baltimore County runs an independent permitting process with different portals, fees, and review timelines. The structural engineering standard is the same - the building code applies in both - but the paperwork, the reviewers, and the schedule differ. The engineer confirms the AHJ before the permit set is assembled.
