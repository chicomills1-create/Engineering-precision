Who Handles Plan-Check Corrections That Need a PE Re-Seal?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineering corrections go to the licensed PE in responsible charge, who revises the design, re-verifies the calculations, and re-seals the corrected sheets — a fresh professional statement covering the revised design, never a signature over unreviewed changes. GCs who have the sealing PE under contract with a corrections scope turn corrections around in days.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Triage: sorting corrections on day one

When the correction list lands, I read it end to end before anyone starts working — because the list has a structure the GC needs to see. First pass: mark every comment that touches engineering analysis, sizing, system design, or code compliance calculations. Those go to the sealing PE that day, with the comment text, the affected sheet numbers, and the resubmittal deadline. Second pass: mark the clarifications and administrative items — missing references, inconsistent labels, forms — which the architect or GC answers directly. Third pass: flag the comments that are actually questions about design intent, which need a conversation with the reviewer before anyone revises anything; answering the wrong interpretation of a vague comment burns a full cycle. The triage meeting should happen within 48 hours of receiving corrections. I have watched GCs sit on a correction list for two weeks "reviewing it internally" while the permit clock ran — the review cycle does not pause because you are thinking. Same-day triage, same-week engineering revision, complete resubmittal: that is the cadence that keeps permits moving.

The re-seal: what the PE actually does

A re-seal is a new engineering act, not a signature on old work. When corrections require design changes, the PE revises the affected design — resizing, re-detailing, re-selecting equipment — then re-verifies the calculations behind the revision, because a changed design with unverified math is exactly what the next correction round will catch. The revised sheets get clouded so the reviewer can find every change without hunting, the response letter ties each comment to its clouded revision, and the PE applies a fresh seal to the revised sheets. That fresh seal carries the same professional weight as the original: it states the revised design was reviewed under responsible charge. I also use the correction round as a coordination check. Corrections have a way of exposing clashes the original review missed — the resized beam now conflicts with the duct the MEP reviewer asked to reroute — so the PE re-checks the revised sheets against the other disciplines before the re-seal. One thorough correction round beats two fast sloppy ones; the AHJ keeps score on resubmittal quality, and reviewers remember the projects that waste their time.

Correction-round checklist for GCs

Run every correction list through this process and the permit stays on schedule. • Triage within 48 hours: engineering corrections to the sealing PE, clarifications to the architect/GC, vague comments flagged for reviewer discussion • Send the PE the complete comment text plus affected sheets and a hard resubmittal deadline — never a forwarded PDF with "please advise" • Require clouded revisions and a comment-by-comment response letter on every resubmittal • Re-seal only the revised sheets, with the PE's fresh review behind the fresh seal — never re-date an old seal over changed design • Resubmit complete: every comment answered, every sheet included — piecemeal resubmittals restart review cycles

What else do project teams ask?

Does every plan-check correction require a PE re-seal?
No — only corrections that change the engineering. Clarifications, added references, corrected labels, and non-technical missing items can be answered without touching the seal. But any correction that revises the design — resized members, changed equipment, modified details, new analysis — requires the PE to review the revision and re-seal the affected sheets, because the seal covers the design as a professional statement and the design just changed. I sort corrections into engineering and non-engineering on day one so the PE's time goes to the sheets that actually need re-seal and the rest move in parallel.
What if the original sealing engineer will not respond to corrections?
This is the scenario that turns a two-week correction round into a two-month delay. A new PE cannot simply re-seal the old sheets — taking responsible charge means the new engineer must review the design, verify the calculations, and require revisions before sealing, which is a full review-and-seal engagement on a compressed timeline. I have taken over stalled projects this way and the honest timeline is weeks, because the review cannot be skipped. The prevention is contractual: engage the sealing PE with a written corrections scope and a response-time commitment before the first submittal, and keep the native design files accessible so a successor engineer is not reverse-engineering PDFs.
How do MEP corrections differ from structural corrections?
MEP corrections tend to be more numerous but individually smaller — equipment schedule mismatches, missing controls sequences, lighting power density issues, ventilation calculations the reviewer wants to see — while structural corrections tend to be fewer but heavier, often requiring re-analysis of a lateral system or a foundation detail. Both need the same discipline: the PE revises, re-verifies, clouds, and re-seals. On MEP-heavy projects like restaurants or medical buildouts I budget more correction cycles, because energy and mechanical reviewers in jurisdictions like California (Title 24) and Florida generate the longest comment lists. The GC's job is the same either way: get the comments to the right PE immediately and completely.
Can corrections be resubmitted in phases to save time?
Only if the AHJ allows it, and most do not want phased correction responses — they want one complete resubmittal that answers every comment, because partial responses create version-control chaos on their side. A few jurisdictions accept phased resubmittals on large projects by prior agreement, but I never assume it. The faster play is a complete, well-organized resubmittal: every comment answered in a response letter keyed to clouded revisions on the re-sealed sheets. Reviewers reward clarity — a resubmittal they can verify in one pass goes back out faster than one they have to decode.
