How Is Service Bay Ventilation Designed for Dealerships?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers pair tailpipe source-capture exhaust with general bay exhaust sized to repair-garage code rates, hold the shop negative to the showroom, and provide tempered makeup air interlocked with the exhaust fans.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Tailpipe Source Capture and Hose Reel Layout

Source capture removes exhaust at the tailpipe before it enters the breathing zone, and it is far more effective per cfm than general exhaust alone. The engineer lays out overhead hose reels — or underfloor systems in high-end shops — so every service position has a reel within practical reach, with hose lengths and reel spacing matched to the bay layout and lift positions. Fan sizing accounts for the number of reels likely in simultaneous use plus system static pressure through hose, duct, and fittings. The design includes blast gates or automatic dampers so unused reels do not waste capacity, and discharge stacks are located to avoid re-entrainment into building intakes or neighboring properties. For diesel service areas, capture design gets extra attention because diesel particulate demands more complete collection.

General Exhaust Rates and Pressure Relationships

General exhaust covers what source capture cannot: evaporative vapors, heat, and the exhaust that escapes during vehicle movement. Mechanical codes set minimum ventilation rates for repair garages, and the engineer designs to those rates as a floor, increasing them where the service mix — heavy line work, transmission shops, high bay counts — justifies more air. Pressure zoning protects the customer experience. The service department runs negative to the showroom, parts department, and customer lounge so fumes and noise stay in the shop. Vestibules or air curtains at the service drive help, but the engineered pressure cascade is the reliable mechanism. Commissioning verifies the cascade with the overhead doors both open and closed, because door position changes everything.

Service Bay Ventilation Design Checklist

A complete service bay ventilation package captures contaminants at the source and the room: • Tailpipe exhaust hose reels at every service position with simultaneous-use fan sizing • General exhaust meeting repair-garage code ventilation rates as a minimum • Tempered makeup air units interlocked with exhaust, heated for winter operation • Negative pressure cascade from shop to showroom, lounge, and parts areas • Discharge stacks located clear of intakes, operable windows, and neighbors • Continuous exhaust operation during business hours with after-hours setback • Commissioning verification of airflow, capture, and pressure relationships

What else do project teams ask?

Why do service bays need both exhaust hose reels and general exhaust?
Hose reels capture tailpipe exhaust at the source, which is the most concentrated contaminant, but they cannot capture evaporative fuel vapors, solvent fumes from parts washers, or heat. General exhaust handles the background contaminants and thermal load across the whole bay. Together they keep technician breathing zones clean; either one alone leaves gaps — source capture misses ambient fumes, and general exhaust alone needs enormous airflows to dilute a running engine's tailpipe.
How much makeup air does a dealership service bay need?
Roughly as much as the exhaust removes — every cubic foot exhausted must be replaced or the building goes negative. The engineer totals the general exhaust, paint-adjacent exhaust, and any process exhaust, then designs makeup air units to match, tempered for winter heating. Undersized makeup air shows up as hard-to-open doors, backdrafting water heaters, and exhaust fans that cannot move their rated airflow. It is sized as a system with the exhaust, never as an afterthought.
Should the service shop be positive or negative to the showroom?
Negative. The shop contains exhaust fumes, chemical odors, and noise that must not migrate into customer areas. The engineer designs a deliberate pressure cascade — showroom and lounge most positive, stepping down through parts and offices to the shop most negative — and verifies it at commissioning. Service drive doors and vestibules are detailed to preserve the cascade even during busy drop-off periods.
Can service bay exhaust run on a schedule instead of continuously?
General exhaust typically runs continuously during business hours because contaminants and heat are generated all day, with setback or shutdown after hours. Source-capture reels can run on demand per bay. The control sequence ties exhaust to occupancy schedules and may add contaminant or temperature sensors for variable operation. What the design avoids is a dead shop with engines running and no ventilation — the sequence makes that operating mode impossible, not just discouraged.
