How Are Foundations Designed for Navajo Expansive Soils?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Navajo expansive soils, bentonitic Chinle Formation clays, swell when wet and shrink when dry, damaging foundations. Engineers start with a geotechnical investigation measuring swell potential and the active-zone depth, then design drilled piers below the active zone or stiffened slabs for the predicted movement, with strict drainage and moisture control around the building.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Why Chinle Clays Move

The Chinle Formation's clay-rich beds contain bentonite derived from altered volcanic ash, along with montmorillonite clays, minerals whose layered structure absorbs water and expands. USGS geological work on the Colorado Plateau has long documented bentonite as a common constituent of the Chinle, and federal geologic hazard inventories for the region list shrink-swell soils as a known hazard associated with these clays, citing foundation, wall, driveway, and road cracking. On the Navajo Nation, where the Chinle and related formations underlie broad areas, geotechnical engineers encounter these deposits routinely and have documented the structural damage they cause. Movement follows moisture, and moisture follows the site. Monsoon-season downpours, snowmelt, landscape irrigation, and leaking water or sewer lines each wet the active zone and trigger heave; dry seasons and vegetation drawing moisture from the soil trigger shrinkage. The result is differential movement: one corner of a building heaves while another stays put, which is what cracks slabs and walls. Flatwork fails first and most visibly, but the same forces work on shallow footings. Sites with a history of distress nearby deserve extra investigation, because expansive-soil behavior is consistent within a geologic setting.

Foundation Strategies That Work

Drilled piers are the definitive answer where expansive deposits are thick. The piers extend through the active zone into stable soil or rock below the reach of seasonal moisture change, and the building bears on the piers, not on the moving soil. Grade beams tie the pier tops together, and the design isolates the beams from the soil with void forms or clearance so heaving ground cannot jack them upward. Pier shafts in the active zone are designed for the uplift the swelling soil applies to the shaft, which the geotechnical engineer quantifies from swell-pressure testing. Stiffened slabs serve where the movement is moderate and the geotechnical engineer endorses them. Post-tensioned slabs and heavily reinforced stiffened slabs are designed as rigid plates that bridge localized heave and settlement, with the design movement taken directly from the geotechnical recommendations. Over-excavation and replacement with engineered, non-expansive fill works where the expansive layer is shallow enough to remove economically. Every strategy pairs with moisture control: the site is graded to shed water, the building perimeter is surfaced to block infiltration, downspouts discharge well away from the foundation, and irrigation is kept clear of the structure. The foundation system is only as good as the drainage that protects it.

What else do project teams ask?

What makes a soil expansive?
Expansive soils contain clay minerals, such as bentonite and montmorillonite, that absorb water between their crystal layers and swell, then shrink as they dry. The Chinle Formation clays underlying much of the Navajo Nation are classic examples. The swell generates uplift pressures strong enough to heave foundations and crack slabs, and the seasonal wet-dry cycling produces the differential movement that damages structures.
How is swell potential tested?
The geotechnical investigation combines field and laboratory work: borings and test pits to map the expansive strata, Atterberg limits and clay mineralogy to characterize the soil, and one-dimensional swell and swell-pressure tests that measure how much a sample expands when wetted and how much pressure it exerts. These results define the active-zone depth and the design movement the structural engineer must accommodate.
Can good drainage alone fix an expansive-soil site?
No. Drainage and moisture control are essential companions to the foundation design, but they cannot substitute for it. Surface grading, perimeter surfacing, and leak-free plumbing slow the moisture cycling that drives movement, yet seasonal climate alone wets and dries the active zone. The foundation itself must be designed for the movement, with piers, stiffened slabs, or soil replacement, and the drainage must protect that foundation for the building's life.
Does every Navajo Nation site need drilled piers?
No. Expansive deposits vary in thickness and severity across the Nation, and some sites have competent near-surface soils where conventional foundations on engineered fill perform well. The geotechnical investigation for the specific site decides: where borings find thick expansive clays, deep foundations are the standard recommendation; where they find stable ground, the report says so. Designing without the investigation is what produces the failures.
