How Is a Brewery Expansion in Eugene Engineered?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A Eugene brewery expansion is engineered around process loads: expanded steam, glycol, air, and electrical capacity for new tanks, CO2 detection and ventilation for life safety, sloped chemical-resistant floors with trench drainage, wastewater pretreatment for high-strength effluent, and seismic bracing of every vessel.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Process Utilities and Electrical Growth

The engineer builds the expansion's utility budget tank by tank. Glycol chilling is usually the binding constraint: crash-cooling multiple fermenters simultaneously defines the chiller tonnage, and the engineer sizes the glycol piping mains and pumps for the expanded loop with the pressure drops the longer runs create. Steam or high-temperature hot water for the brewhouse and CIP is evaluated against the existing plant's spare capacity; where the plant is maxed, the engineer designs the new boiler or heater with flue, gas service, combustion air, and blowdown drainage. Compressed air gets a similar treatment — instrument air must be dry and oil-free, so the engineer specifies the dryer and filtration the process controls require. Electrical design follows the equipment list. The engineer calculates the new connected and demand loads, checks the existing service entrance and utility transformer against the growth, and where an upgrade is needed, coordinates the new service with the utility while keeping the brewery running — often by building the new switchboard and feeders first, then cutting over during a planned shutdown. Panel schedules, motor controls, and process instrumentation are designed for washdown-rated enclosures in the wet areas, and the engineer separates the process power from the tasting-room and office loads so a production fault never darkens the hospitality side.

CO2 Safety, Drainage, and Wastewater Pretreatment

CO2 protection is engineered in layers. The engineer designs mechanical ventilation for the cellar and tank areas sized to dilute the maximum credible CO2 release, places fixed gas detection at low level — CO2 is heavier than air — with audible and visible alarms, and interlocks detection with increased ventilation and personnel alerting. Any new pits, trenches, or below-grade spaces are evaluated as permit-required confined spaces, and the engineer designs the access, ventilation, and rescue provisions the classification demands. Operating procedures and signage are coordinated with the owner's safety plan, but the engineering provides the systems the procedures rely on. The drainage and pretreatment design handles the brewery's hardest effluent. Trench drains with chemical-resistant grating collect washdown across the sloped production floor; the engineer sizes the drainage for hose flows and details the waterproofing at every penetration, because brewery floors leak at the details. The wastewater stream goes through the pretreatment the city requires — typically screening for solids, equalization to smooth the batch discharges, and pH adjustment for the caustic and acid cleaning cycles — and the engineer designs the pretreatment equipment, its controls, and its own drainage and ventilation. Sampling and metering points the city requires for compliance are built into the design, not added after the inspector asks.

What else do project teams ask?

Why does adding fermenters require utility upgrades?
Because each tank is a bundle of utility demands: glycol cooling for fermentation control and crash-cooling, hot water or steam for brewing and cleaning, compressed air for controls, and electrical load for pumps and instrumentation. The engineer totals these across the new tanks and usually finds the existing boiler, chiller, air, or electrical service cannot absorb them — the expansion's real cost is often in the utilities, not the tanks.
What makes CO2 dangerous in a brewery?
Fermentation releases large volumes of carbon dioxide, which is odorless, heavier than air, and displaces oxygen in low areas — cellars, pits, and around open tank manways. The engineer designs continuous ventilation, low-level gas detection with alarms interlocked to ventilation, and confined-space provisions for pits, because CO2 asphyxiation is a leading brewery fatality hazard.
Why does brewery wastewater need pretreatment?
It is high in organic load and solids with extreme pH swings from cleaning chemicals — far stronger than domestic sewage. Municipal systems require pretreatment so the brewery does not upset the treatment plant. The engineer designs screening, equalization, and pH adjustment (at minimum) with the sampling and metering the city requires for compliance monitoring.
How are fermentation tanks braced for earthquakes?
Each tank gets engineered anchorage to its foundation designed for the site's seismic design category, accounting for the full liquid weight and the sloshing dynamics of the contents. The engineer also designs flexible connections on all piping to the tanks so the piping does not tear free as the tank moves, and verifies the slab or foundation for the concentrated seismic overturning.
