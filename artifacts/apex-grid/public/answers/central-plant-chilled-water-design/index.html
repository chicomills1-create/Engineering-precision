How Is a Central Chilled Water Plant Properly Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A central chilled water plant is designed by sizing chillers to the block load with N+1 redundancy, staging unequal machines to follow the load curve, choosing primary/secondary or variable-primary distribution, and rejecting heat through properly treated cooling towers with waterside economizer potential.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Chiller selection and staging

Chiller selection starts with the load profile, not the peak number. I build an annual cooling load profile from the building simulation or campus metering data, then test chiller lineups against it — counting run hours in each staging combination and computing annual energy, not just design-day efficiency. This is where unequal machine sizes earn their keep: a plant with a small, a medium, and a large chiller spends most of its hours running one or two machines near full load instead of short-cycling a single giant. Staging controls decide which machines run and when. I sequence on measured load and chiller efficiency curves rather than simple timers, with rotation to equalize wear, automatic standby start on failure, and condenser water temperature reset that lets chillers unload as the towers produce colder water. Magnetic-bearing centrifugal and high-efficiency screw machines have made part-load performance so good that the old rule of thumb about avoiding part load is obsolete — but only if the staging sequence actually uses that capability instead of fighting it.

Distribution piping strategies

The distribution system carries the plant's efficiency to the building, or squanders it. In a primary/secondary plant I size the decoupler for low velocity and near-zero pressure drop so the primary and secondary loops truly decouple, and I instrument it — flow direction and temperature in the decoupler tells the operator instantly whether the plant is keeping up or the building is misbehaving. Secondary pumps get VFDs and differential-pressure control reset by valve position, so pump speed follows the most demanding coil rather than a fixed setpoint. In a variable primary plant, the design centers on chiller protection: minimum flow bypass with fast-acting control, flow meters on each chiller, and sequencing that never strands a running chiller below its minimum. Both architectures live or die on coil delta-T, so I specify pressure-independent control valves at air handlers, design coils for realistic entering conditions, and put system delta-T on the operator's dashboard with an alarm. The piping itself gets reverse-return or carefully balanced direct-return layouts, air elimination, and expansion handling sized for the full system volume — details that decide whether the plant is maintainable or miserable.

Condenser water and heat rejection

Cooling towers are selected for the design wet-bulb with an approach that balances tower cost against chiller efficiency — colder condenser water makes chillers dramatically more efficient, up to the manufacturer's limits. I arrange multiple cells so any cell can be isolated for maintenance or winter operation without losing the plant, and I place towers considering plume, drift, noise, and distance from intakes and occupied outdoor spaces. Makeup water, blowdown, and basin heating for freeze protection are designed as systems, not accessories. Water treatment protects the entire investment. Scale on a condenser tube costs more in a year of wasted energy than treatment costs in several, so I design filtration, chemical or non-chemical treatment, and automated blowdown control from day one — and I meter makeup and blowdown so the operator can see the treatment working. Legionella risk management follows the industry guidance for tower operation and maintenance, with the design providing the drains, access, and monitoring points the water management plan needs. • Size chillers to the block load with N+1 redundancy and unequal machines for load-following • Choose variable primary flow for pump savings or primary/secondary for simplicity — deliberately • Fight low delta-T syndrome with pressure-independent valves and BAS delta-T alarming • Select towers for design wet-bulb with cell redundancy and proper placement • Design water treatment, blowdown, and legionella management as core systems • Model waterside economizer hours with local weather data on every plant

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between primary/secondary and variable primary flow?
Primary/secondary uses constant-flow primary pumps through the chillers and variable-flow secondary pumps to the building, with a decoupler pipe between them — simple, forgiving controls, but vulnerable to low delta-T syndrome that wastes energy. Variable primary flow eliminates the decoupler and varies flow through the chillers themselves with VFD pumps, saving significant pump energy but requiring careful minimum-flow protection, bypass control, and tighter sequencing. I recommend variable primary for most new plants with a capable operations team, and primary/secondary where simplicity and resilience matter more than peak pump efficiency.
How many chillers should a central plant have?
Enough to meet the peak block load with the largest unit out of service — that's N+1 — and enough granularity to follow the load curve efficiently. For most campuses that means two to four machines of unequal size: the smallest carries winter and night loads near its sweet spot while larger machines stage on for summer peaks. A single chiller is never acceptable for a critical facility, and beyond four the maintenance and controls complexity usually outweighs the part-load benefit. I model the annual load profile against candidate lineups to prove the selection.
What is low delta-T syndrome and how do you prevent it?
Low delta-T syndrome is when the building returns chilled water only a few degrees warmer than it was supplied — the coils aren't extracting the designed temperature difference — forcing the plant to pump far more water and run more chillers than the actual load requires. Causes include oversized coils, low airflows, leaking control valves, and setpoints fighting each other. I prevent it by designing for honest coil performance, specifying pressure-independent control valves, monitoring system delta-T in the BAS with alarms, and giving the operators a way to find and fix the offending coils. In variable primary plants it's critical; in primary/secondary it's merely expensive.
Should a chilled water plant include waterside economizing?
In most climates, yes — evaluate it on every plant. When the outdoor wet-bulb is low enough, cooling towers can produce chilled-water temperatures directly through a plate heat exchanger, and the chillers shut down entirely. That free-cooling window can cover hundreds to thousands of hours a year depending on climate and chilled water temperature reset strategy. The added cost is a heat exchanger, extra tower capacity, and controls; the payback in chiller energy savings is usually compelling. I model it with local weather data rather than guessing.
