How Is a Birmingham Steel Mill Adaptively Reused?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A Birmingham steel mill is adaptively reused by assessing the existing steel frame and foundations, repairing corrosion and deteriorated masonry, upgrading floors for new loads, and threading modern MEP systems through the heavy-timber or steel structure -- permitted through the City of Birmingham or Jefferson County, with historic review where applicable.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Assessing the Existing Steel Frame

The structural assessment is part detective work, part materials science. The engineer surveys the frame bay by bay, recording member sizes, connection types -- riveted, bolted, or early welded -- and any modifications made over decades of industrial use. Steel coupons or hardness testing establish the actual yield strength, because steel from the early 1900s does not match modern grades. Masonry walls get their own investigation: mortar composition, wythe thickness, and the condition of the bond between the brick and the steel frame it often bears against. Capacity analysis follows the survey. Each member is checked for the new occupancy's gravity loads, and the frame is analyzed for lateral loads it may never have been designed for. Connections get special attention: riveted connections can be remarkably robust, but pack rust -- corrosion that swells between plies -- can jack connections apart and must be remediated. The assessment produces a clear map of what stays as-is, what gets repaired, and what gets supplemented, so the architect knows exactly where the design has freedom and where the structure sets the rules.

New Loads, New Systems, Old Bones

Changing occupancy changes everything the structure must do. A mill floor that carried raw material on rails might become offices with a fraction of the load -- or a brewery with fermentation tanks heavier than anything the floor has seen. The engineer verifies each floor's capacity for the new use, designs strengthening where needed (steel plates bolted to beams, new composite action with the slab, or supplemental framing), and checks foundations for any net increase in load, since century-old footings were sized for the original building, not the new one. New vertical circulation and MEP systems create the most coordination work. Stair and elevator openings cut through old floors need new headers and trimmers tied into the existing frame; rooftop mechanical units add concentrated loads that the old roof structure must be verified for; and fire suppression, plumbing, and electrical distribution route through the frame's web openings and along columns rather than through members. The structural and MEP engineers coordinate early so a duct run never discovers a historic truss in the field, which is where adaptive reuse budgets go to die.

Corrosion and Masonry Repair

A century of Birmingham humidity, industrial fumes, and deferred maintenance leaves its mark. The corrosion survey distinguishes surface rust -- which can be cleaned and recoated -- from section loss that reduces a member's capacity. Where steel has thinned, the engineer designs repairs: bolted or welded cover plates that restore the section, partial member replacement where deterioration is advanced, or new supplemental members that pick up the load. All repairs are detailed to shed water going forward, because the repair is only as good as the drainage around it. Masonry gets parallel treatment. Repointing with compatible mortar -- not hard modern cement that traps moisture and spalls historic brick -- restores the wall's weather resistance, while cracked or bulging wythes are tied back to the frame with helical anchors. Parapets, the most exposed masonry on the building, are rebuilt or braced. The engineer writes the repair specification with the same care as the new structural design, because on an adaptive reuse project the repairs ARE the structural design for half the building.

What else do project teams ask?

Why are old steel mills good candidates for adaptive reuse?
They were overbuilt by modern standards: heavy steel frames designed for crane loads and industrial abuse usually have large reserve capacity for lighter uses like offices or housing. The long clear spans, high ceilings, and big floor plates suit almost any program, and the industrial character -- exposed steel, brick, crane rails -- is exactly what tenants and buyers pay a premium for. Reuse also preserves enormous embodied carbon compared to demolition and new construction.
How is century-old steel evaluated?
Through field survey and testing. The engineer measures actual member sizes, documents connection types, maps corrosion and section loss, and tests the steel's strength with coupons or hardness testing since historic grades differ from modern steel. That data feeds a structural analysis for the new occupancy's loads, producing a repair and strengthening scope targeted at the real deficiencies rather than assumed ones.
How do modern MEP systems fit into an old mill?
By routing through the structure's natural voids -- truss web openings, along columns, beneath raised floors -- rather than cutting historic members. The structural engineer verifies every new penetration, rooftop unit, and mezzanine against the frame's verified capacity, and designs new steel to carry stairs, elevators, and equipment. Early coordination between the structural and MEP teams keeps field conflicts, and the change orders they cause, to a minimum.
What is the permit path for a mill reuse in Birmingham?
Inside Birmingham city limits, the project goes through the city's permitting and plan review, and designated historic structures add design review by the historic preservation authority, which governs exterior changes and demolition. In unincorporated Jefferson County, the county's permitting office handles review without the city's historic overlay. Either way, a change of occupancy triggers full code review for the new use -- structural, fire, accessibility, and energy -- and the authority having jurisdiction decides what is approved.
