Does a Generator Installation Need a PE Stamp?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Commercial generator installations need PE review and sealing for the equipment pad and vibration design, placement with fire-code setbacks and noise compliance, the electrical integration with transfer switching, and the fuel storage system.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Pad, placement, and the site

Site review comes first because placement constrains everything. I verify the generator location against the fire code's setbacks from buildings, property lines, and openings; the clearances the manufacturer requires for airflow, exhaust, and service access; and the local noise ordinance at the nearest sensitive receptor. Exhaust routing gets designed away from air intakes, operable windows, and pedestrian areas — the termination clearances are life-safety dimensions, not suggestions. Where the site is tight, the design works the constraints explicitly: acoustic enclosures for noise, extended exhaust for clearances, barriers for separation. The pad design follows the equipment. I size the concrete pad for the generator's operating weight with full fuel, design the reinforcement for the concentrated loads, and specify the vibration isolation the manufacturer requires — because a generator hard-mounted to a pad transmits vibration into the ground and the building, fatiguing connections and annoying occupants. The subgrade gets prepared and documented; a pad on unprepared fill settles differentially and takes the generator's alignment with it. For rooftop installations, the review expands to the building structure: the roof's capacity for the weight and vibration, plus seismic anchorage designed for the site's criteria.

Electrical integration and fuel systems

The electrical review proves the emergency system works as a system. I build the load analysis for the emergency and standby loads, verify the generator's rated capacity carries them with margin, and check the transfer equipment: ratings, transition type, and the code-required separation of emergency, legally required, and optional standby branches. The distribution downstream gets the coordination study's attention — selective coordination so faults isolate to the faulted branch — and the grounding design treats the generator as the separately derived source it is. Control and monitoring wiring, including the remote annunciation the code requires for emergency systems, completes the electrical scope. Fuel system review runs in parallel with the fire marshal's expectations in mind. I verify tank listings, secondary containment sizing, vent routing and termination, fuel piping materials and routing, and the separation distances for the fuel quantity stored. Day tanks, fuel polishing where long storage demands it, and the runtime calculation proving the stored fuel serves the required emergency duration all get checked. The sealed package shows the AHJ and the fire department a fuel system designed to the fire code, not just a tank the contractor preferred.

Generator installation PE checklist

A generator submittal is ready for the PE's seal when the site, the structure, the electrical, and the fuel scopes are each designed and coordinated. Noise and exhaust clearances are the most commonly missed items. • Placement verified: fire-code setbacks, manufacturer clearances, exhaust away from intakes and occupants, noise ordinance compliance • Equipment pad designed for operating weight with full fuel, vibration isolation specified, subgrade prepared • Load analysis proving generator capacity for the emergency and standby loads with code demand factors • Transfer switch and emergency distribution verified: ratings, load separation, selective coordination, grounding • Fuel storage designed to the fire code: listed tanks, secondary containment, venting, separation distances • Runtime calculation proving the stored fuel serves the required emergency duration; fire department coordination complete

What else do project teams ask?

What structural design does a generator pad need?
The pad is designed for the generator's operating weight including full fuel, the dynamic forces from vibration, and the soil's bearing capacity beneath it. I verify the pad thickness and reinforcement for the concentrated equipment loads, detail vibration isolation — spring isolators or pads per the manufacturer's requirements — so the vibration doesn't transmit into the building or fatigue the connections, and confirm the subgrade preparation. Rooftop generators add the building structure check: the roof framing must carry the unit's weight plus vibration, with seismic anchorage for the site's zone. The pad looks like a simple slab until the vibration and seismic detailing enter; then it's engineered work.
How do noise requirements affect generator placement?
Many AHJs enforce noise limits at the property line — often stricter at night — and generators are loud enough that placement and mitigation are design decisions, not afterthoughts. I review the generator's sound rating against the local ordinance, verify the setback distances, and where the numbers don't comply, the design adds mitigation: sound-attenuated enclosures, acoustic barriers, or exhaust silencers. The placement review also keeps the exhaust away from building air intakes and operable windows — carbon monoxide entrainment is the life-safety item behind the clearance rules. A generator package that ignores acoustics earns corrections from planning or the building department before it ever runs.
What does the PE verify in the transfer switch and electrical design?
The transfer switch is the life-safety heart of the installation, and I verify it thoroughly: the switch rating against the loads served, the transition type — open, closed, or delayed — suited to the loads, and the separation of emergency, legally required standby, and optional standby loads per the code's requirements. The load analysis proves the generator's capacity carries the connected emergency loads with the code's demand factors. Feeder sizing, overcurrent protection, and selective coordination of the emergency distribution get checked so a fault on one branch doesn't darken the whole emergency system. Grounding and bonding of the separately derived system complete the review.
What fuel storage rules apply to generator installations?
The fire code governs: tank type and listing, secondary containment for the fuel quantity, venting, separation distances from buildings and property lines, and spill control. Belly tanks and sub-base tanks integral to the generator have their own listing and containment provisions; remote above-ground or underground tanks trigger the fuller tank regulations. I verify the fuel system design against the fire code's requirements for the installation's fuel quantity and location, coordinate with the fire marshal's expectations — fuel storage is a fire department permit item in most jurisdictions — and confirm the fuel capacity supports the required runtime for the occupancy's emergency systems.
