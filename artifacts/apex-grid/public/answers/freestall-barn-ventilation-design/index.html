How Is Freestall Barn Ventilation Engineered for Dairy Herds?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Freestall barn ventilation is engineered around airspeed over the cow: tunnel or cross-ventilated designs deliver 5 to 8 mph across stalls in summer with baffles keeping air low, while winter minimum ventilation clears moisture without chilling the herd.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Tunnel, Cross-Ventilated, and Natural Strategies

The strategy decision comes before the fan schedule. Tunnel barns are long and narrow — air enters through the inlet end wall, travels the full length past the cows, and exits through the fan bank on the far end wall. The engineer sizes the end-wall fans for the barn's full summer airflow, checks the temperature rise from inlet to exhaust end, and details the inlet end with evaporative pads or baffled openings. Tunnel works best where the barn can be built long, and the design must reckon with the exhaust-end penalty: the last cows breathe the warmest, most ammonia-laden air in the building. Cross-ventilated barns turn the airflow 90 degrees: inlets run the length of one sidewall and exhaust fans the length of the other, so air crosses the barn's width. This fits wide barns and gives every cow nearly identical air quality, which is why the biggest new dairies favor it. The engineering cost is more fans, a larger electrical service, and careful inlet design — motorized baffle boards or curtain inlets that modulate with the seasons. Natural ventilation uses adjustable sidewall curtains and an open or baffled ridge, cheapest to build and run, but at the mercy of wind; most natural barns get circulation fans over the stalls or over the feed line as mechanical assist. The engineer compares the strategies against the site's wind data, summer design temperatures, and the owner's staffing and energy preferences before committing the barn's geometry.

Airspeed, Baffles, and Inlet Design

Airspeed is the deliverable, not fan count. The engineer lays out the barn cross-section and asks whether the air stream actually reaches the cow's nose at stall level. Baffles — solid panels hung from the ceiling — force the incoming air down out of the roof peak and across the stalls instead of letting it stratify along the ceiling. In tunnel barns, baffles are spaced down the length; in cross-ventilated barns, the inlet baffle sets the throw across the width. Fan spacing, diameter, and static pressure are selected so the design airspeed lands in the 5 to 8 mph band at cow level during summer design conditions. Inlets are engineered openings, not holes in the wall. Summer inlets are large and low-resistance; winter minimum-ventilation inlets are small, distributed, and aimed to mix incoming cold air with warm barn air above the cows before it drops. The engineer details inlet actuators and linkages so one control signal moves the whole sidewall together — a curtain that sags or an inlet that sticks open in January creates a cold draft lane that the cows will avoid, leaving stalls empty on one side of the barn. Evaporative cooling pads at the inlet get their own water supply, drainage, and pump design, and the engineer confirms the local water chemistry won't scale the pads shut in a single season.

Freestall Barn Ventilation Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the barn ventilation design is finalized: • Ventilation strategy (tunnel, cross-ventilated, or natural with assist) selected from climate data and barn geometry • Summer design airspeed of 5 to 8 mph verified at stall level, not just at the fan • Baffles detailed to keep the air stream low over the stalls instead of stratifying at the ceiling • Winter minimum ventilation rates set to clear moisture without drafting resting cows • Inlet actuators specified for coordinated, staged operation across seasons • Evaporative pad water supply, drainage, and water-chemistry review where pads are used • Control sequence staging fans, curtains, and cooling through winter, transitional, and summer modes • Ventilation-failure alarms and emergency power coordinated — a power loss on a hot day is a herd emergency

What else do project teams ask?

How much airspeed do dairy cows need in summer?
Design practice targets roughly 5 to 8 miles per hour of air movement over resting and feeding cows during heat-stress conditions. That airspeed drives evaporative cooling off the cow's skin and is the main lever against heat stress in the barn. The engineer verifies it at stall level in the design — fan nameplate airflow means little if baffles, inlets, or obstructions keep the air stream up at the ceiling.
What is the difference between tunnel and cross-ventilated barns?
Tunnel barns move air along the barn's length, from an inlet end wall to exhaust fans on the far end wall — simple and effective for long, narrow barns, but conditions degrade toward the exhaust end. Cross-ventilated barns move air across the barn's width, with inlets on one sidewall and fans on the other, giving every cow nearly the same air quality and suiting wide barns. The engineer selects based on barn proportions, climate, and how uniform the owner needs conditions to be.
Do freestall barns need ventilation in winter?
Yes — minimum winter ventilation removes the moisture that cows exhale and that evaporates from manure and bedding. Without it, humidity condenses on cold steel and the air grows heavy with ammonia, hurting respiratory health. The design provides small, controlled winter air exchange with inlets aimed to mix incoming air above the cows, so the barn stays dry without chilling the animals. Curtains and inlet panels stage down as temperatures fall.
Can evaporative cooling pads work in humid climates?
Poorly. Evaporative pads cool by evaporating water into the incoming air, which works best when the air is hot and dry — arid regions see large temperature drops. In humid climates the air is already near saturation, so pads add little cooling and can push barn humidity higher. The engineer checks local design wet-bulb data before specifying pads; in humid regions, high airspeed alone is usually the better investment.
