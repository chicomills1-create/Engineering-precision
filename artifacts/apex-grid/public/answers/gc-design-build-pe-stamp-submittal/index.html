How Does PE Stamping Work on GC-Led Design-Build Projects?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

On GC-led design-build, the EOR must be engaged from the start and review design as it develops — staged review-and-seal (foundations, then structure, then MEP) lets early work permit while later design is still under review. The seal follows responsible charge; it cannot be manufactured at the end.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

EOR coordination across the design-build timeline

The coordination that matters most on GC-led design-build is between the EOR's review milestones and the GC's procurement milestones. Steel procurement, equipment buyout, and long-lead MEP orders all want to happen before the design is truly final — that is the nature of fast-track — and the EOR's job is to review the systems driving those buyouts first. I structure the review so the structural system and the major MEP concepts get verified at design development, before the GC commits to the steel package or the rooftop units. That way the PE's revisions land in the pricing set, not in already-ordered material. The second coordination point is with the design-build architect and the MEP design-assist subcontractors. On paper the EOR reviews their work; in practice the EOR needs their backgrounds, their equipment selections, and their routing concepts in reviewable form at each milestone — not as a data dump the week before permit. I set submittal expectations at kickoff: what the PE needs, in what format, by when, for each review stage. GCs who run this as a managed process get clean seals on schedule; GCs who treat the EOR as a mailbox get surprises.

Responsible charge inside a design-build team

Responsible charge does not bend for delivery method. Whether the engineer works for the GC, the architect, or a sub, the PE who seals must have performed or directly supervised the engineering work — reviewed the design, verified the calculations, required the revisions. On design-build this gets tested because so much engineering is distributed: the structural engineer designs the frame, the steel fabricator's engineer designs the connections, the MEP subs design their systems, and each piece needs a PE in responsible charge of that piece. The EOR's seal covers the EOR's scope; delegated components carry their own seals from their own engineers. I make the seal map explicit at the start of every design-build engagement: a simple matrix of every engineered component, the PE responsible for it, and the license state. In Texas that matrix also tracks the F-number for each firm. When plan check asks "who sealed the truss package" or "who is responsible for the fire sprinkler design," the GC answers in one email instead of a week of archaeology. The matrix is also the GC's proof that no component is floating without an engineer — which is exactly what an AHJ worries about on design-build.

GC design-build sealing checklist

Set these up before design development starts, and the permit phase takes care of itself. • Name the EOR in the design-build agreement with review scope across all design phases — not just a sealing line item • Build the seal map: every engineered component, its responsible PE, license state, and firm registration • Tie EOR review milestones to procurement milestones — structure and major MEP verified before steel and equipment buyout • Plan phased submittals with the AHJ early and match the PE's review sequence to the phased intake checklist • Confirm every sealing PE is licensed in the project state (and the firm registered, in Texas) before design starts, not at intake

What else do project teams ask?

Who is the engineer of record on a GC-led design-build project?
The EOR is the licensed PE who takes responsible charge of the engineering design and ultimately seals the permit set — on GC-led design-build, that engineer is typically contracted through the design-build team, but professional responsibility for the engineering still rests with the PE personally, not with the GC. The GC cannot delegate responsible charge to itself or to an unlicensed designer; the seal must come from the licensed engineer who actually reviewed the work. I advise GCs to name the EOR in the design-build agreement itself, with a defined review scope across design phases, so there is never a question later about who owns the engineering decisions.
Can the GC's in-house designer seal the design-build drawings?
Only if that person is a licensed PE in the project state who performed or directly supervised the engineering work and takes responsible charge of it. A talented unlicensed designer, a drafter, or an out-of-state PE cannot produce a lawful seal — and in states like Texas the firm itself needs registration too. GCs with in-house design staff sometimes assume the license of a PE elsewhere in the company covers the work; it does not unless that PE was in responsible charge of the actual design. When in doubt, the clean path is an outside EOR engaged for review-and-seal with a real review scope.
How does staged permitting work with the PE seal on design-build?
Many AHJs accept phased submittals — foundations and site work first, then the full building — and each phase gets its own reviewed and sealed package from the EOR. The key is that the PE reviews each phase on its real schedule: the foundation package is reviewed and sealed while the superstructure is still in design development, which requires the EOR to be under contract from the beginning with review milestones tied to the fast-track plan. I coordinate the phased review scope in writing at engagement, so the AHJ's phased intake checklist and the PE's review sequence match. Done right, the project is digging while the upper floors are still being engineered.
What happens when the EOR's review requires redesign late in design-build?
It happens, and it is cheaper than the alternative — which is the AHJ finding the problem, or the field finding it. When my review uncovers a real problem late — a lateral system that does not work for the final geometry, MEP systems that cannot meet code with the selected equipment — I mark the required revisions and the design team corrects them before the seal goes on. The GC's protection against this is early EOR involvement: reviews at schematic and DD stages catch the expensive problems when they are still cheap to fix. Late redesign is almost always a symptom of late EOR engagement, not of overzealous review.
