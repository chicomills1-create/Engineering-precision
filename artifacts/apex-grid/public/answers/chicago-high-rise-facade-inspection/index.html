How Do Chicago High-Rise Facade Inspections Work?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Chicago high-rise facade inspections work through the city's facade ordinance, which requires owners of tall buildings to have a licensed professional examine exterior walls on a set cycle, document unsafe conditions, file a critical examination report with the city, and repair deficiencies on the schedule the building official requires.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What the Facade Ordinance Requires

Chicago's facade ordinance applies to buildings above a defined height threshold and runs on a repeating inspection cycle measured in years. Each cycle, the owner retains a licensed architect or structural engineer - not a contractor, not a property manager - to perform the critical examination and file the report. The ordinance classifies findings so the response matches the risk: safe conditions need no action, conditions requiring repair get scheduled within the cycle, and unsafe conditions trigger immediate public protection plus repair within a short mandated window. The Department of Buildings tracks filings and pursues owners who miss deadlines, which makes the calendar as important as the engineering. Compliance is a program, not a single inspection. Smart owners use each examination as the scoping document for a multi-year facade maintenance plan: the engineer prioritizes the elevations and conditions, the owner budgets the repair phases, and each cycle's report measures progress against the last. This turns a regulatory burden into an asset management tool - the building with a documented, current facade program leases better, insures better, and never faces the emergency sidewalk-canopy scenario that follows a neglected unsafe condition. The examining professional's independence matters here: the report must describe conditions honestly, because the city relies on it to protect the public.

How Engineers Inspect a Facade

The examination method matches the building. Hands-on inspection from a swing stage or lift lets the engineer touch the masonry, sound it for delamination, probe sealant joints, and photograph anchorage details at arm's length - the gold standard for masonry, stone, and terra cotta buildings where deterioration hides behind a sound-looking face. For glass and metal curtain wall buildings, the focus shifts to gaskets, pressure plates, anchor bolts, and the weep and drainage paths that keep water out of the system. Drones increasingly supplement the work for initial surveys and hard-to-reach areas, but the ordinance's critical examination still demands the close-up evaluation that only direct access provides. What the engineer is hunting for follows a known pattern of facade failure. Water is the universal enemy: it enters through failed sealant and mortar, freezes, expands, and progressively breaks the bond between the face material and its backup. Corroding steel shelf angles and ties expand as they rust, jacking masonry apart from the inside. And original anchorage from the early and mid-1900s was often minimal by modern standards - wire ties and spot anchors that have simply given up after a century of cycles. The report connects each observed condition to its mechanism, because the repair design must fix the cause: re-anchoring where anchorage failed, rebuilding where the masonry is spent, and managing water everywhere.

What else do project teams ask?

Which Chicago buildings fall under the facade ordinance?
The ordinance applies to buildings above a specified height threshold, which captures essentially all high-rises. Shorter buildings are still subject to the building code's general maintenance requirements but not the recurring critical examination cycle. An architect or engineer can confirm whether a specific building is covered and when its next filing is due.
Who is qualified to perform a facade inspection?
Chicago requires the critical examination to be performed by a licensed architect or structural engineer. The qualification matters because the examiner must evaluate structural anchorage, material deterioration mechanisms, and public-safety risk - judgments that go well beyond a visual condition survey - and must certify the report filed with the Department of Buildings.
What happens if an unsafe facade condition is found?
The owner must immediately protect the public, usually with a sidewalk canopy or enclosure, and then complete the permanent repair within the short timeline the ordinance mandates. The finding is documented in the filed report, and the Department of Buildings enforces the repair schedule with violations and fines for noncompliance.
Do suburbs follow Chicago's facade ordinance?
No. The facade ordinance is a City of Chicago law. Suburban municipalities and unincorporated Cook County enforce their own building and property maintenance codes, which require facades to be maintained but do not impose Chicago's specific recurring examination cycle. The engineer confirms the authority having jurisdiction for the site and scopes the inspection program to that jurisdiction's requirements.
