How Is Security System Design Integrated Across Mixed-Use?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Security across mixed-use is engineered as zoned access-control domains per occupancy on one credential platform, CCTV layered at every domain boundary, intrusion detection per commercial need, and full integration with fire alarm and elevators so security never blocks evacuation.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Access-Control Domains and Credentials

The engineer architects the access-control system as domains that mirror the occupancies. Each domain — residential, office, retail common, parking, service — gets its own zone programming, its own schedules, and its own administrator, all running on one head-end platform so the building has a single system to maintain. Credentials are issued per domain: a resident's fob works at the residential lobby, the residential elevators, the residential parking gate, and the residential amenities — and is denied everywhere else by default. Visitor management is designed per domain too: the residential intercom calls the unit, the office lobby has its own reception procedure, and the retail manages its own after-hours vendor access. The hardware is specified for the duty. Exterior residential doors get heavy-duty electrified mortise locks with request-to-exit and door-position monitoring; office entries get the schedule-driven locks their hours demand; retail tenant entries get storefront-rated hardware the tenants manage. The engineer details the power supplies — centralized with battery backup sized for the code-required standby duration — and the wiring topology so a single power-supply failure does not take down a whole domain. Every controlled door is shown on the security plans with its hardware set, its zone assignment, and its fire-alarm interface.

CCTV, Intrusion, and Network Segmentation

The CCTV design starts from the question each camera answers: who entered the residential lobby, which car used the parking gate, what happened at the loading dock. The engineer places cameras to answer those questions with identifiable images — the right focal length, the right lighting, the right angle — rather than blanketing the building with cameras that record unusable footage. Storage is sized for the retention policy at the required frame rate and resolution, and the engineer verifies the network bandwidth: dozens of high-resolution cameras need a real backbone, not a shared office switch. Intrusion detection serves the commercial occupancies and the building's vulnerable points. Retail tenants get alarm panels for their bays with door, motion, and glass-break coverage reporting to their own monitoring; the building gets intrusion coverage on the roof hatches, the service corridors, and the unoccupied common areas. The engineer segments the security network completely — its own VLANs or physical switches, its own firewall, no path from the tenant data networks to the access-control server. Cybersecurity is part of the security design: default credentials changed, firmware maintained, and the head-end in a locked room with its own access log.

Mixed-Use Security Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the security design is finalized: • Access-control domains zoned per occupancy with separate administrators on one platform • Credentials issued per domain: residential fobs denied at office and retail doors by default • Every controlled door shown with hardware set, zone assignment, and fire-alarm interface • Fail-safe/fail-secure logic designed with the fire-protection engineer for evacuation compliance • CCTV placed at every domain boundary with purpose-matched camera selection and lighting • Video storage and network bandwidth sized for the retention policy at required resolution • Intrusion detection per commercial tenant plus building coverage at roofs and service areas • Security network segmented from tenant and BAS networks with its own secured head-end

What else do project teams ask?

Can one key fob work for the whole mixed-use building?
Technically yes, but it should not. A single credential with building-wide access defeats the domain separation the residents and office tenants were promised — a retail employee's fob should never open a residential floor. The engineer programs credentials per domain so each holder's access matches their legitimate need, and the system logs every entry. Convenience never overrides the boundary design.
What happens to security doors during a fire?
They release for evacuation per the code. The engineer designs the interface so the fire alarm unlocks the egress path — stair doors, exit doors, the doors along the evacuation route — while maintaining security everywhere else to the extent the code allows. The fail-safe vs fail-secure selection per door is documented on the security plans and reviewed with the fire reviewer, because a door that stays locked in a fire is a life-safety violation regardless of the security intent.
Who monitors the mixed-use security cameras?
Per the operating agreements: the residential association typically monitors or contracts monitoring for the residential domain, the office manager for the office domain, and each retail tenant for their own bays — often with the building's central station watching the common areas and parking. The engineer designs the video management system with partitioned viewing so each operator sees only their domain's cameras, matching the legal responsibility to the technical access.
How is package delivery secured in mixed-use housing?
With a designed package room, not the lobby floor. The engineer provides a secured package room off the residential lobby with credentialed resident access, camera coverage, and a delivery-driver procedure — coded entry, concierge handoff, or smart-locker integration — that keeps drivers out of the residential corridors. The room is sized for the e-commerce volume the unit count generates, with overflow planning for the holidays.
