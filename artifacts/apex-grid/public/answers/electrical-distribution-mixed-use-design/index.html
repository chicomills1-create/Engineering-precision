How Is Electrical Distribution Designed in Mixed-Use Projects?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Electrical distribution in mixed-use is engineered as separately metered systems per occupancy from a common service: independent switchboards, risers, and feeders per use, harmonics and fault coordination designed building-wide, and emergency and standby power allocated to each occupancy's code and operating needs.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Service Sizing and Switchboard Architecture

The engineer starts with the load calculation per occupancy — NEC Article 220 for the residential dwelling units with its demand factors, the connected-load method for retail and office with diversity applied — and combines them with a studied coincident-demand factor for the service size. The result is smaller than the sum of the individual peaks, which is the electrical version of the mixed-use diversity advantage. The main switchboard is then architected as sections: utility metering, then a main per occupancy, each feeding its own distribution switchboard. The one-line diagram shows the whole architecture on a single sheet so the utility reviewer, the plan checker, and the contractor all see the same system. Spare capacity is allocated by churn. Retail gets the most — empty breaker spaces, spare conduits to the roof and to each bay, and panel schedules with room for the next tenant's equipment. Offices get moderate spare for densification and TI cycles. Residential gets the least spare but the most circuits per square foot, since every dwelling unit needs its code-required branch circuits. The engineer documents the spare capacity on the panel schedules so future TI engineers know exactly what is available without a field investigation.

Fault Coordination, Harmonics, and Standby Power

The selective-coordination study is run for the entire building as one system. The engineer models the fault current at every panel and sets the breaker and fuse curves so a short circuit in a retail tenant space trips only that tenant's breaker — not the retail switchboard main, and certainly not the building main that would darken the residences. Ground-fault protection is applied per the code at the service and at the large feeders, and the engineer verifies the protection holds across the occupancy boundaries. Harmonics are managed by separation. Office UPS systems, VFDs on the HVAC, and LED lighting drivers all generate harmonic currents that can overheat neutrals and distort voltage; the engineer keeps those loads on their own feeders, sizes neutrals for the harmonic content, and specifies K-rated transformers where the analysis warrants. Residential feeders are routed and panelized separately so a dwelling unit never shares a neutral path with a harmonic-rich office feeder. Standby power is designed per occupancy priority: the generator and automatic transfer switches are sized for the life-safety loads plus the contracted standby loads, with load shedding that drops the lowest-priority standby loads first if the generator approaches its limit.

Mixed-Use Electrical Distribution Checklist

Use this checklist before the electrical design is finalized: • Service sized on studied coincident demand across occupancies, coordinated with the utility • Separate switchboards per occupancy with independent metering and documented spare capacity • Independent risers and feeders per use; residential arc-fault/ground-fault per code • Selective-coordination study run building-wide so faults isolate to the originating occupancy • Harmonic loads on dedicated feeders with K-rated transformers and upsized neutrals where needed • Emergency distribution serving life-safety loads building-wide per code • Standby generator with per-occupancy transfer switches and a defined load-shed priority • EV charging distribution with load management, metered per the cost-allocation structure

What else do project teams ask?

Does each mixed-use occupancy need its own electric meter?
Each occupancy needs its own measurement — either a utility-owned meter on a separate service or a submeter on its own switchboard section. The engineer designs whichever arrangement the utility allows and the legal documents require, with the metering one-line showing every meter and what it measures. Dwelling units are typically metered individually as well, with the house loads on their own meter.
How is power kept on during a utility outage?
In layers. The code-required emergency system — egress lighting, fire alarm, fire pump, elevator recall — transfers to generator or battery backup automatically and serves the whole building. Beyond that, each occupancy's standby loads transfer on their own automatic transfer switches per the priority the owner set: the office's server room, the hotel's key systems, the residential elevators. The engineer sizes the generator for the life-safety plus contracted standby load and programs the shedding order.
What causes electrical disputes between mixed-use tenants?
Almost always unmetered sharing: a retail tenant's after-hours load on the house panel, the central plant's consumption split by square footage instead of measurement, EV charging with no meter behind it. The engineering fix is measurement at every split the leases promise — submeters on each occupancy's feeders, on the shared systems, and on EV charging — specified on the drawings before the leases are signed.
Can a retail tenant upgrade their electrical service later?
Yes, if the engineer provided for it. Retail switchboards are designed with spare breaker spaces, spare conduit to the bays, and service capacity held in reserve for tenant growth — a restaurant taking over a dry-goods bay typically needs double the power. The base-building drawings document the available spare so the TI engineer can design the upgrade as a panel change rather than a service rebuild.
