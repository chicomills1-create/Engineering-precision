How Are Vivariums Designed for Animal Research Facilities?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design vivariums with barrier zoning and pressure cascades, high air-change 100-percent-exhaust ventilation, tight temperature and humidity control, programmed light cycles, and acoustic isolation.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Barrier Zoning, Pressure Cascades, and Air Changes

The barrier concept organizes the whole facility. Clean corridors supply staff and clean caging; animal holding rooms sit at controlled pressure relationships to those corridors depending on the barrier philosophy — positive to keep pathogens out of clean colonies, negative to keep allergens and agents in. Airlocks or gowning vestibules sit at each transition, and the engineer documents the cascade direction for every room so operations and maintenance never guess. Air change rates in holding rooms run high to dilute ammonia and particulates, with exhaust exceeding supply to hold the intended pressure. The engineer places supply diffusers and exhaust grilles to sweep air across the room without dead zones, keeps cage-rack exhaust connections balanced against room exhaust, and designs the system for 100 percent outside air — recirculating animal room air to other spaces is not acceptable. Redundant fans and automatic changeover protect the environment through equipment failures, because a ventilation outage in a holding room becomes an animal welfare emergency within hours.

Light Cycles, Acoustics, and Cage Wash Utilities

Lighting in a vivarium is a research instrument. Each holding room gets programmable controls that execute the species-appropriate light-dark cycle — including dawn/dusk ramping where studies require it — with manual override that logs who interrupted the cycle and for how long. Emergency lighting is designed not to blast nocturnal rooms with full light during an outage; the engineer selects fixtures and controls that protect the cycle even in alarm conditions. Acoustic design starts with equipment selection: quiet HVAC with low-velocity ductwork, vibration-isolated fans, and cage wash equipment located away from holding rooms with acoustic separation. The engineer sets background noise targets for holding areas and verifies them at commissioning, because chronic mechanical noise is a known confounder in behavioral and physiological studies. Cage wash gets its own engineering: high-temperature washers need steam or high-capacity electric service, tempered water, floor drainage with solids handling, and ventilation for heat and humidity — essentially a small industrial plant inside the facility.

Vivarium Design Checklist

A complete vivarium design protects both the animals and the research: • Barrier zoning with documented pressure cascades and airlock transitions • High air-change ventilation with 100 percent exhaust and no recirculation • Individually ventilated cage rack connections coordinated with room airflow • Tight temperature and humidity control with redundant HVAC and changeover • Programmable light-dark cycles per species with logged manual overrides • Acoustic isolation of holding rooms from mechanical and cage wash noise • Cage wash utilities: steam, tempered water, solids-handling drainage, ventilation

What else do project teams ask?

Why do vivariums use 100 percent outside air?
Animal rooms generate ammonia, dander, allergens, and odors continuously, and recirculating that air to other spaces would spread contaminants and allergens through the facility. Using 100 percent outside air — conditioned, then exhausted directly — keeps each room's air isolated. It is energy-intensive, which is why the engineer pairs it with energy recovery on the exhaust stream and careful airflow design, but the air quality and research integrity benefits make it the standard for animal holding areas.
How tight does temperature control need to be in animal holding rooms?
Typically within about one to two degrees of setpoint, with humidity held in a defined band — the exact ranges follow the species and the study protocols. The engineering challenge is holding that band with high air change rates and varying animal loads, which is why the design uses reheat, careful sensor placement away from drafts, and redundant equipment. Excursions matter: temperature swings can alter metabolism, immune response, and behavior, quietly invalidating study data.
What is a barrier facility in vivarium design?
A barrier facility is a zone operated to keep pathogens and contaminants out of the animal colony — used for immunodeficient, germ-free, or long-term breeding colonies. The engineering includes positive pressure to surrounding areas, airlocks with gowning, sterilized supplies in and controlled waste out, and often individually ventilated caging with filtered air. Everything about the barrier — airflow, traffic flow, materials — is designed so nothing unplanned crosses into the colony.
How is noise controlled in a vivarium?
Through source control, path isolation, and layout: selecting quiet HVAC equipment and low-velocity ductwork, vibration-isolating fans and pumps, locating cage wash and mechanical rooms away from holding areas with acoustic-rated construction between, and setting background noise targets that are verified at commissioning. Even corridor design matters — hard parallel surfaces that carry sound get treated. The goal is a quiet, stable acoustic environment because chronic noise is a documented stressor that confounds research.
