How Is Makeup Air Designed for Laboratory Exhaust Systems?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers size makeup air to match total lab exhaust with a slight negative offset, deliver it at low velocity away from hoods, temper it for climate, and track VAV hood modulation through integrated controls.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Exhaust Inventory and Air Balance

The air balance is the design's foundation document. The engineer inventories every exhaust point — fume hoods at design sash flow, snorkels, canopy hoods, biosafety cabinet connections, general room exhaust — and totals them at the realistic coincident peak, because not every device runs at maximum simultaneously but the design must handle the plausible worst case. Makeup air is then sized to that total minus the small offset that holds the lab negative to adjacent spaces. The balance is not a single number but a schedule across operating modes: full occupancy with hoods open, night setback with sashes closed, and every step between. VAV systems make this dynamic — the engineer documents the flow at each mode so the controls contractor programs the tracking correctly and the balancing contractor verifies it. Without this schedule, the system gets commissioned at one condition and drifts at all the others.

Tempering, Energy Recovery, and Delivery

Conditioning 100 percent outside air is expensive, which is why the tempering strategy gets engineered carefully. In heating climates the makeup air unit needs substantial heating capacity — often with energy recovery from the exhaust stream as the first stage. Runaround loops and heat pipes recover heat without mixing airstreams, which matters because lab exhaust cannot be recirculated; the engineer selects the recovery technology that fits the exhaust's corrosiveness and the climate's economics. Delivery location is a containment issue. High-velocity supply jets aimed across a fume hood face will defeat the hood's capture, so the engineer uses low-velocity, high-induction diffusers positioned away from hoods, sometimes supplying through a perforated ceiling or dedicated low wall grilles. The design keeps supply air speeds low in the hood zone and verifies with smoke visualization at commissioning that makeup air delivery supports rather than disrupts containment.

Laboratory Makeup Air Design Checklist

A complete makeup air design keeps the lab balanced in every mode: • Exhaust inventory with coincident peak flow and per-device design rates • Makeup air sized to exhaust total minus the corridor pressure offset • Air balance schedule across full-flow, setback, and turndown modes • Tempering capacity for the climate with energy recovery on the exhaust stream • Low-velocity delivery away from fume hood faces to protect containment • Integrated controls that track VAV hood modulation with makeup air response • Pressure deviation alarming with commissioning across the full operating range

What else do project teams ask?

What happens if a lab does not have enough makeup air?
The lab goes excessively negative: doors become hard to open, fume hood face velocities drop below rated values, and air gets pulled from unintended places — wall cavities, adjacent labs, corridors — carrying whatever those spaces contain. In extreme cases the exhaust fans cannot move their design flow at all because they are fighting the building's resistance. Every one of these degrades containment and comfort, which is why the engineer balances exhaust against dedicated makeup air rather than hoping the building leaks enough.
How does makeup air affect fume hood performance?
Directly. Makeup air delivered as a high-velocity jet across the hood face creates turbulence that spills contaminants out of the hood, while too little makeup air starves the hood of the flow it needs for rated face velocity. The engineer designs low-velocity delivery away from hoods and proves it with smoke tests at commissioning — watching that supply air drifts gently into the room without disturbing the hood's capture zone. Hood performance and makeup air design are one problem, not two.
Can energy be recovered from laboratory exhaust air?
Yes, with the right technology. Because lab exhaust cannot be recirculated or mixed with supply air, the design uses air-to-air recovery that keeps the airstreams separate — runaround glycol loops, heat pipes, or plate exchangers with verified separation. The engineer selects for the exhaust's corrosiveness and the climate: in cold climates the savings are large enough to justify significant recovery investment, and the reduced tempering load also shrinks the makeup air unit itself.
Should makeup air track VAV fume hoods automatically?
Yes — that is the entire point of a VAV lab. As sashes close and hood exhaust drops, the makeup air must ramp down in step to hold the room's pressure relationship; otherwise the lab swings positive when hoods close and the cascade collapses. The engineer designs the tracking sequence with appropriate response times — fast enough to hold pressure, stable enough not to hunt — and commissions it across the full sash range, not just at the endpoints.
