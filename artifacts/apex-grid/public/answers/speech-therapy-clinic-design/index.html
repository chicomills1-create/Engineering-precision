How Do You Design a Speech Therapy Clinic Around Sound?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Speech therapy clinics engineer quiet therapy rooms through low-noise HVAC, high-performance partitions and seals, sound-treated evaluation rooms, and video infrastructure — with lighting for observing articulation.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The concise answer

Speech therapy clinics engineer quiet therapy rooms through low-noise HVAC, high-performance partitions and seals, sound-treated evaluation rooms, and video infrastructure — with lighting for observing articulation. Hearing is the clinical instrument in speech therapy, and the building is either part of that instrument or interference. I design every surface and system in a therapy room by asking what it does to sound: does it absorb it, block it, or generate it? Only the first two answers are acceptable.

The listening room, engineered

Partitions get the detailing they deserve: sealed top and bottom tracks, backer boxes on outlets, solid doors with perimeter seals. These are the details that separate a room that tests quiet from one that is merely built to a partition schedule. I verify the acoustic design against the actual noise sources — the specific air handler, the adjacent corridor, the plumbing in the wall. Electrical and low-voltage design covers therapy-room data for documentation, video recording with good sightlines, and lighting controls that let therapists adjust for different activities. Emergency power and fire protection follow the occupancy classification. Plumbing is light: restrooms, a staff break area, clinical handwash where the protocols require it. Structurally, most speech clinics are standard office buildouts — the engineering intensity is almost entirely in the acoustic and mechanical detailing, which is exactly where I focus the design effort.

What I get right on speech therapy projects

Speech clinics are small projects where the details are the project. A single noisy diffuser can compromise every room it serves. Here is what I prioritize.

  • Design HVAC for the ear first: quiet equipment, low velocities, and diffusers selected for sound output
  • Detail partitions, doors, and seals for genuine speech privacy and low background levels
  • Give formal evaluations a dedicated, repeatable, sound-treated room with recording
  • Locate the clinic and its rooms away from mechanical, traffic, and playground noise
  • Light for observation: bright, glare-free light on faces and mouths for articulation work

What else do project teams ask?

How quiet do therapy rooms need to be?
Quieter than a standard office or exam room. I target low background sound levels through a combination of quiet HVAC equipment, low-velocity ductwork, sound-absorbing finishes, and well-sealed partitions and doors. The test is simple: can the therapist hear the difference between similar speech sounds from across the room? If the HVAC masks it, the room fails clinically.
What makes a good evaluation room?
A dedicated, sound-treated room for formal speech and language assessments, with controlled acoustics — not too live, not too dead — consistent lighting, and minimal visual distraction. I keep it away from noise sources, give it its own HVAC zone so testing conditions are repeatable, and wire it for audio-video recording. Standardized testing needs standardized rooms.
How does HVAC noise get controlled?
By selecting quiet equipment, sizing ductwork for low air velocities, choosing diffusers for low sound output, and locating air handlers away from therapy rooms. I also isolate duct-borne sound between rooms so one room's system does not leak noise into the next. The mechanical engineer and the acoustic requirements have to be designed together, not sequentially.
What about pediatric speech therapy?
Child-friendly durability — washable finishes, robust fixtures — plus rooms sized for a child, parent, and therapist with toys and materials. Observation windows let parents watch without distracting the child, which needs acoustic detailing so the window does not become a sound leak. Lighting should be bright and cheerful with glare control for tabletop work.
