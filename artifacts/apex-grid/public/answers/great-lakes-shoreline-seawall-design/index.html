How Are Great Lakes Shoreline Seawalls Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Great Lakes shoreline seawalls are designed by a licensed engineer who studies wave action, ice shove, and soil conditions at the site, then sizes a wall - typically steel sheet pile, concrete, or rock - to resist those forces while managing drainage and permitting through the agencies that govern the shoreline.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Wave, Ice, and Soil Forces

The design starts with the site, not the wall. The engineer reviews the shoreline's exposure and orientation, the lakebed profile offshore, historical lake-level records, and any history of erosion or previous wall failures at or near the site. A geotechnical investigation establishes the soil profile behind and beneath the wall - its strength, its permeability, and its frost susceptibility - because the wall's stability analysis depends on the actual soils, not assumed ones. Sites on the open lake face a different wave climate than sites in a protected bay, and the design wave, ice load, and crest elevation follow from that distinction. Each force gets its own design case, and the wall must satisfy all of them. Wave impact and overtopping govern the face and crest design; ice shove governs the wall's mass and stiffness and the cap detail; fluctuating lake levels govern the range of water pressure the wall sees, since a high lake raises both the wave attack elevation and the hydrostatic load; and the retained soil behind the wall adds its own lateral earth pressure, increased when the backfill is saturated. The engineer combines these per the applicable load combinations and checks sliding, overturning, and bearing - the classic retaining-wall failure modes - plus global slope stability of the whole shoreline behind the wall.

Wall Types and Permitting

Three wall families dominate Great Lakes shorelines, and the site picks among them. Steel sheet pile walls - interlocking steel sections driven into the lakebed - suit deep water and soft soils, go up fast, and take ice shove well, but need corrosion protection and eventually recoating below the waterline. Cast-in-place or precast concrete walls suit sites where appearance matters and foundations are good, with the concrete mix and reinforcement detailed for freeze-thaw and splash-zone exposure. Rock revetments and rubble slopes are not walls at all but armored slopes that absorb wave energy rather than reflecting it, often the best answer where space allows and where reflected waves would scour neighboring properties. Permitting is a multi-agency process the engineer plans for from the start. Work in or near navigable waters triggers federal review, the state's environmental agency reviews shoreline alterations and wetland impacts, and the local city, village, or township handles zoning and building permits - with unincorporated shoreline under county or township jurisdiction following a different submittal path than incorporated cities. Timelines vary widely by agency and by season, so the engineer sequences the applications early and designs to the most restrictive applicable standard, avoiding the redesign loop that comes from permitting after the design is finished.

What else do project teams ask?

What is ice shove and why does it matter for seawalls?
Ice shove is the force of wind-driven lake ice piling against the shore in winter - sheets and ridges of ice that can push with tons of force per foot of wall. It buckles steel, dislodges caps, and shoves wall sections landward. Great Lakes seawalls must be explicitly designed for ice loading based on the site's exposure and ice history; it is often the governing load case.
Which agencies permit a Great Lakes seawall?
Typically three levels: federal review for work in navigable waters, the state environmental agency for shoreline and wetland impacts, and the local municipality - city, village, or township - for zoning and building approval. Unincorporated shoreline follows county or township processes instead of city ones. Each authority has its own application, standards, and timeline, and all must be satisfied.
How long does a steel sheet pile seawall last?
A well-designed and coated steel sheet pile wall in the Great Lakes typically serves for several decades. Its lifespan depends on the steel thickness specified, the corrosion protection system, water chemistry, and ice exposure. The engineer designs for a target service life with appropriate section thickness and coatings, and the owner should plan for periodic inspection and recoating.
Why do seawalls fail at the base?
Toe scour is the most common cause: wave action excavates the lakebed at the wall's base, removing the soil the wall bears on and exposing more of the wall to wave forces. The design counters this by founding the wall below the expected scour depth or armoring the toe with stone. Drainage failure behind the wall is the other classic cause - trapped water builds pressure that pushes the wall lakeward.
