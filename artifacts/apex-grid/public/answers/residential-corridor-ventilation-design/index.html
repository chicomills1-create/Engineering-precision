How Is Residential Corridor Ventilation in Mixed-Use Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Residential corridor ventilation in mixed-use is engineered with a dedicated outdoor-air system that positively pressurizes corridors relative to retail and units, supplies tempered fresh air continuously, and shares no equipment or ductwork with any commercial occupancy.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Pressurization Strategy and Stack Effect

The engineer designs the pressure cascade deliberately: corridors highest, dwelling units slightly lower, retail and back-of-house lowest. This cascade means every leakage path flows away from the cleanest air — corridor air pushes into units under their doors, unit air pushes toward exhaust, and nothing flows upward from the retail into the housing. The differentials are small — 0.02 to 0.05 inches of water column between zones — but they must hold across the building's height and across the seasons, which is where stack effect complicates the design. Stack effect is the building breathing with temperature. In winter, warm indoor air rises, pressurizing the top floors and depressurizing the bottom — fighting the corridor pressurization at the upper levels and reinforcing it at the lower. In summer the pattern reverses. The engineer accounts for stack effect in the DOAS sizing and the pressure design, sometimes with floor-by-floor balancing dampers or with the supply air delivered to each corridor level independently rather than from a single riser. The commissioning plan tests the pressure relationships in both heating and cooling seasons, because a cascade that holds in October can fail in January.

DOAS Equipment and Intake Placement

The corridor DOAS unit is selected for continuous duty and quiet operation — it runs whenever the building is occupied, and its intake and discharge are near residential windows. The engineer specifies the filtration level (typically MERV 13 or better for corridor supply), the tempering coils for the climate, and energy recovery — an enthalpy wheel or heat-pipe — where the exhaust airstream justifies it. The ductwork is sized for low velocity and low noise, with the supply delivered to each corridor through diffusers placed to sweep the space without blowing directly at unit doors. Intake placement is a site-planning exercise. The engineer locates the corridor DOAS intake upwind of and separated from every contaminant source: the restaurant grease exhaust, the retail general exhaust, the loading-dock truck zone, the parking garage exhaust, and the trash-room exhaust. Code minimum separation distances are the floor, not the target — the engineer adds margin where the prevailing wind or the building geometry suggests it. The intake gets bird screens, rain protection, and a location the maintenance staff can actually reach for filter changes, because a DOAS with clogged filters pressurizes nothing.

Residential Corridor Ventilation Checklist

Use this checklist before the corridor ventilation design is finalized: • Pressure cascade designed: corridors positive to units, units positive to retail and back-of-house • Dedicated DOAS serving corridors only, with no shared air handlers or ductwork with commercial uses • Stack effect analyzed for heating and cooling seasons; balancing provisions per floor as needed • Supply air tempered and dehumidified for the climate; MERV 13 or better filtration • Intake located upwind and separated from every exhaust source with margin beyond code minimums • Energy recovery applied where the exhaust airstream justifies it • Pressure relationships commissioned in both heating and cooling seasons • Intake accessible for filter maintenance with bird screens and rain protection

What else do project teams ask?

Why do apartment hallways sometimes smell like cooking?
Because the pressure cascade failed or was never designed. When corridors go neutral or negative relative to the units, cooking air migrates under unit doors into the hallway and travels. The engineering fix is positive corridor pressurization with a dedicated supply system — and the operational fix is keeping it commissioned, since a DOAS with dirty filters or a failed fan pressurizes nothing and the smells return.
Can corridor ventilation share equipment with the retail HVAC?
No. Sharing an air handler or ductwork between residential corridors and any commercial occupancy risks carrying odors — and in a fire event, smoke — between the uses, and it makes the pressure cascade impossible to control independently. The engineer gives the corridors their own DOAS with their own intake, and the retail its own systems. The separation is both an air-quality design and a life-safety design.
How much air does a corridor ventilation system supply?
Enough to pressurize the corridor and meet the ventilation code — typically designed around the corridor's volume and the leakage characteristics of the unit doors, in the range the mechanical code's ventilation rates require, plus the pressurization margin. The engineer calculates it from the air-balance model rather than a rule of thumb, because door undercuts, elevator shafts, and stair enclosures all change the answer.
Does stack effect really affect corridor ventilation?
Significantly, in any building over a few stories. Winter stack effect can add the equivalent of the entire design pressurization at the top floors and subtract it at the bottom, overwhelming a DOAS sized without it. The engineer models the stack pressures for the design heating and cooling conditions and provides the balancing — floor-by-floor dampers, independent risers, or pressure-independent controls — that holds the cascade in January as well as in October.
