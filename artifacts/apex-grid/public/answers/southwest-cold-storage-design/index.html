How Are Cold Storage Facilities Engineered for the Desert Southwest?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Southwest cold storage is engineered as one thermodynamic system: ammonia or CO2 refrigeration with heat rejection sized for 115-degree ambient, insulated-panel envelopes with warm-side vapor retarders and continuous air barriers, glycol-heated floor slabs that prevent frost heave, and dock airlocks with sealed doors that limit moisture intrusion.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Refrigeration Systems for 115-Degree Ambient

The engineer selects the refrigerant and the cycle architecture from the temperature zones and the facility's scale. Large freezer distribution favors ammonia with evaporative condensers for heat rejection — the evaporative approach exploits the desert's low wet-bulb temperatures to reject heat far more efficiently than air-cooled equipment — with the safety systems ammonia requires: gas detection, emergency ventilation, pressure relief, and the machinery-room construction the mechanical code mandates. CO2 transcritical systems suit facilities that want to avoid ammonia's toxicity profile, and the engineer evaluates their efficiency honestly against the desert's high ambient, where transcritical operation spends more hours in its less-efficient region. Heat rejection equipment is rated for the site, not the catalog's standard conditions. The engineer applies the manufacturer's capacity corrections for the design dry-bulb and wet-bulb, adds margin for dust fouling on condenser coils — monsoon dust degrades heat transfer steadily between cleanings — and designs the maintenance access that makes coil cleaning actually happen. Compressor redundancy, automatic controls with remote alarming, and emergency power for controls and critical loads complete a refrigeration design that protects millions of dollars of product through the hottest week of the year.

Envelope, Floors, and Frost-Heave Control

Envelope detailing is drawn as a continuous system on the plans: the insulation layer, the vapor retarder on the warm side, and the air barrier, each shown unbroken around the entire cold envelope with every penetration detailed. The engineer pays special attention to the transitions — wall to roof, wall to floor, panel joints, column penetrations — because field experience shows these are where vapor finds its way in. Below-grade portions of freezer walls get waterproofing and insulation rated for the exposure, and the engineer specifies the sealants and tapes by performance, not by brand habit. The underfloor heating system is engineered with the same seriousness as the refrigeration. The engineer calculates the heat flux needed to keep the subgrade above freezing under the design freezer temperature, lays out the glycol tubing or air-duct grid for even coverage with no cold spots at edges and columns, and specifies insulation above the heating layer so the heat goes down into the soil rather than up into the freezer. Temperature sensors in the subgrade report to the building controls with alarms on failure — the cheapest insurance in the building. The structural slab above is designed for the racking and traffic loads with joints and flatness to match, and the engineer sequences construction so the heating layer is tested before the structural slab buries it.

Southwest Cold Storage Checklist

Use this checklist before the permit set is issued: • Refrigeration cycle and refrigerant selected for the temperature zones and desert ambient • Heat rejection rated for site design conditions with dust-fouling margin and cleaning access • Envelope drawn as a continuous system: insulation, warm-side vapor retarder, air barrier • Every penetration and transition detailed against vapor and air leakage • Underfloor heating designed, sensor-alarmed, and tested before the slab is placed • Docks designed as airlocks: seals, high-speed doors, tempered dock temperature • Condensation control on all cold surfaces in dock and cooler areas • Compressor redundancy, product-temperature alarming, and emergency power provided • Heat recovery and rooftop solar evaluated against the refrigeration energy load • Jurisdiction confirmed: city plan check vs county review, with refrigerant-safety reviews scaled to the system

What else do project teams ask?

Ammonia or CO2 for a Southwest cold storage facility?
Both are defensible, and the engineer compares them on efficiency, safety, and scale. Ammonia is the most energy-efficient refrigerant for large freezer warehouses and has the lowest refrigerant cost, but it is toxic and requires rigorous safety design — detection, ventilation, and machinery-room construction per code. CO2 avoids toxicity concerns and performs well, though transcritical CO2 efficiency dips in extreme ambient heat. For large distribution freezers, ammonia usually wins on lifecycle cost; for smaller facilities or owners avoiding ammonia, CO2 is the strong alternative.
Why do freezer floors need heating underneath?
To stop the ground from freezing and heaving the slab. The soil below a freezer sits at sub-freezing temperatures conducted down through the floor; moisture in that soil forms ice lenses that expand with enough force to crack and lift a structural slab. A glycol tubing grid or ventilated air layer beneath the slab keeps the subgrade above freezing. The system runs continuously for the building's life, with temperature sensors and alarms — a failed heat loop shows up months later as a destroyed floor.
How is condensation controlled at the loading docks?
By treating each dock as a thermal airlock. Sealed dock doors with inflatable seals close the gap to the trailer, high-speed doors separate the dock from the cold rooms, and the dock itself is held at an intermediate temperature with its own refrigeration and dehumidification. Cold piping and surfaces in the dock area are insulated and vapor-sealed, and condensate drainage handles what cannot be prevented. Every door opening admits a pulse of warm desert air — the design assumes it and manages it.
What drives energy use most in a desert cold storage building?
Refrigeration compressors rejecting heat to 115-degree air — by a wide margin. The engineer attacks it with efficient refrigeration cycles, evaporative condensing that exploits the desert's low wet-bulb, heat recovery for dock heating and service water, LED lighting with occupancy sensors in cold rooms, and rooftop solar offsetting the daytime peak. In this building type, the refrigeration engineer's equipment selections matter more to the utility bill than anything the architect draws.
