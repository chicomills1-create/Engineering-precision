What Does Public Defender Office Design Need to Prioritize?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Public defender design engineers confidential counsel at volume: plentiful acoustically private client meeting rooms, secure discovery intake and storage, investigation and trial-prep space, and an accessible humane client interface near the courthouse. High caseloads demand workflow efficiency; privilege demands privacy in every room.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The concise answer

Public defender design engineers confidential counsel at volume: plentiful acoustically private client meeting rooms, secure discovery intake and storage, investigation and trial-prep space, and an accessible humane client interface near the courthouse. The design equation is unforgiving: huge caseloads times absolute confidentiality. The building resolves it with room count, acoustic detailing, and workflow — giving attorneys the private space and the operational support to handle volume without ever compromising a client's privilege.

Privacy at volume

The meeting rooms are the core deliverable: acoustically isolated, visually private, and numerous enough that no attorney ever has to choose between meeting a client and protecting privilege. I detail partitions, doors, and ceiling plenums against sound flanking, because the most common confidentiality failure is sound traveling over a partition through a shared plenum. Sound masking in open attorney areas adds a second layer. Discovery and investigation form the operational core: secure digital infrastructure sized for modern evidence volumes, physical exhibit storage, and workspace for investigators. The client interface — waiting, reception, wayfinding — is designed for people under stress: clear, calm, and dignified. MEP systems support long hours and dense occupancy: zoned HVAC for after-hours trial prep, robust power and cooling for the discovery servers, and lighting that keeps detail-heavy document work comfortable.

Defender office checkpoints

The right to counsel is only real if the building supports it. I verify these on every defender office project. Volume without privacy is a constitutional problem wearing an office plan.

  • Meeting-room count: sized from caseload math so privilege never waits for a free room
  • True acoustic privacy: partitions, doors, and plenums detailed against sound flanking in every client room
  • Discovery backbone: secure digital and physical evidence handling with proper IT cooling and power
  • Humane client interface: clear wayfinding, dignified waiting, courthouse proximity
  • After-hours capability: zoned HVAC and reliable power for the trial-prep reality of the work

What else do project teams ask?

Why do public defenders need so many meeting rooms?
Attorney-client privilege requires private conversation, and caseloads mean dozens of client meetings daily. If there aren't enough private rooms, meetings happen in hallways or open areas and privilege is compromised. I size the meeting-room count from the attorney headcount and meeting frequency, not from a generic office ratio — it's a constitutional requirement expressed as a room count.
How is confidentiality engineered?
Acoustic privacy in every client-facing room (proper partitions, sealed doors, background sound masking where needed), sightline management so conversations can't be lip-read or overheard from waiting areas, and secure handling of discovery materials with access control. Confidentiality has to be a property of the architecture, because attorneys can't pause privilege when the building leaks.
What is discovery handling in a defender office?
Receiving, storing, and working with the prosecution's evidence: secure digital infrastructure for massive discovery files (bodycam, surveillance, phone records), physical storage for exhibits, and investigation workspace. The IT load is significant — discovery databases need server capacity, cooling, and backup — and the chain of custody must be as rigorous as the prosecution's.
Why does courthouse proximity matter?
Defender attorneys live between office and courtroom — client meetings, hearings, jail visits, trials. Every minute of transit is a minute not on cases, and clients need to reach the office easily. I treat location and the building's interface with the courthouse (secure attorney circulation, holding-area interview rooms) as design inputs, not afterthoughts.
