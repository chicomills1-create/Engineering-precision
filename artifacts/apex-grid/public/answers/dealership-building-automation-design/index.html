How Is Building Automation Designed for Car Dealerships?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers integrate dealership HVAC, lighting, and ventilation onto one automation platform with scheduling, fault alarming, and energy submetering — standardized across sites so dealer groups manage every rooftop from one dashboard.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Integration Scope and System Architecture

The design defines exactly what the automation system touches and how. HVAC zones, exhaust and makeup air interlocks, lighting scenes and schedules, and EV charging load signals each get integration points with specified protocols — open protocols where possible so the owner is not locked to one vendor for every future device. The network architecture separates building controls traffic from the dealership's business network for security and reliability. The engineer also defines the human interface: graphics the service manager can actually read, alarming that goes to the right person (not everyone), and trend logs with enough history to diagnose problems. An automation system nobody understands is just expensive thermostats — the design includes operator training and documentation as deliverables, not afterthoughts.

Scheduling, Alarming, and Energy Monitoring

Scheduling follows dealership reality: the shop ramps before the first appointment, the showroom holds through evening hours, lot lighting shifts from merchandising to security levels after closing. The engineer programs these as the default sequences with holiday and special-event overrides the staff can actually operate. Alarming catches failures early — a rooftop unit fault, a zone temperature drifting, a lighting schedule stuck on — routed to the facilities contact with sensible priorities so real problems are not buried in nuisance alarms. Energy submetering by major load turns the platform into a management tool: the owner benchmarks sites, verifies retrofit savings, and spots the energy waste that silent systems hide for years.

Dealership Building Automation Design Checklist

A complete automation package runs the building as one operation: • Integration scope defining every system, point, and protocol on the platform • Open protocols and separated controls network for security and flexibility • HVAC scheduling matched to shop, showroom, and office hours with overrides • Lighting scenes and lot schedules with after-hours dimming • Prioritized alarming routed to the right staff with trend history • Energy submetering by major load: HVAC, lighting, EV charging, shop • Multi-site standardization with remote alarming for dealer groups

What else do project teams ask?

What systems should dealership building automation control?
At minimum: all HVAC zones with scheduling and alarming, showroom and lot lighting schedules and scenes, service bay ventilation interlocks, and energy monitoring. Strong candidates to add: EV charging load management signals, domestic hot water scheduling, and integration with the security system's occupancy data. The engineer scopes the platform to the owner's operational goals — a single store needs reliable local control, while a dealer group benefits most from standardized multi-site management.
How does building automation save a dealership energy?
Mostly by enforcing the intended operation: schedules that actually set back unoccupied zones, lighting that dims after closing instead of burning all night, alarming that catches a failed economizer or a stuck schedule before it wastes energy for months, and submetering that reveals which loads deserve attention. The savings come from visibility and discipline, not magic — the automation system makes the efficient sequence the automatic one and flags every deviation.
Should dealer groups standardize automation across sites?
Yes — standardization is where multi-site automation pays for itself. One platform, one graphics standard, and one alarming scheme across all rooftops means one trained team can manage every site, technicians diagnose remotely before rolling a truck, and energy benchmarking compares apples to apples. The engineer writes the standard during the first project and applies it to each subsequent site, with site-specific points mapped into the common framework.
What is the difference between alarming and trending in building automation?
Alarming tells you something needs attention right now — a failed unit, a temperature out of range, a schedule fault. Trending records what happened over time so you can diagnose why — the temperature drift that preceded the failure, the schedule that never quite set back. The engineer designs both: prioritized alarms routed to staff who can act, and trend logs with enough history and resolution to support real troubleshooting. Alarms without trends leave you reacting blind; trends without alarms leave you discovering problems on the utility bill.
