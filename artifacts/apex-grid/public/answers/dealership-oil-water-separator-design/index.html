How Are Oil-Water Separators Sized for Dealership Drains?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers size oil-water separators from the peak flow of all connected shop drains, select gravity or coalescing types against discharge limits, and design sediment pretreatment plus full maintenance and sampling access.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Flow Inventory and Separator Sizing

The sizing calculation inventories every connected source: trench drains in service bays, detail and wash bays, lube drains, and any outdoor wash or fuel areas tied into the system. The engineer totals the peak simultaneous flow — not the average day — because the separator must perform during the busiest wash period, and undersizing shows up immediately as oil carry-through. Residence time is the sizing criterion for gravity units: the chamber must hold the flow long enough for the design droplet size to rise to the surface. Coalescing separators achieve the same separation in less footprint using plate packs, at higher equipment cost. The engineer documents the design flow, the target effluent quality, and the safety factor so future bay additions trigger a recheck rather than an overloaded unit.

Pretreatment, Discharge Permits, and Maintenance

Pretreatment protects the separator. Sand and sediment interceptors upstream capture grit from wash water and shop floors before it fills the separator's chambers — solids accumulation is the most common cause of separator failure. The design sequences the treatment train logically: trench drain to sediment trap to separator to sanitary connection, each with cleanouts. The discharge permit shapes monitoring and sometimes sizing. Many jurisdictions require an industrial discharge permit for separator effluent with numeric oil and grease limits and periodic sampling, so the design includes sampling ports and keeps records accessible. Maintenance is engineered in: chamber access for pump-outs, hauler access for vacuum trucks, and a maintenance schedule in the O&M documentation that the owner actually receives.

Oil-Water Separator Design Checklist

A complete separator package treats the real flow and stays serviceable: • Flow inventory of all connected drains at peak simultaneous use • Gravity or coalescing separator sized for residence time and effluent limits • Sediment and sand pretreatment upstream of the separator • Discharge permit path confirmed with numeric limits understood • Sampling ports where monitoring is required • Manhole access to every chamber with hauler truck pump-out access • Maintenance schedule and O&M documentation delivered to the owner

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between gravity and coalescing separators?
Gravity separators rely on residence time — a large chamber where flow slows enough for oil droplets to float to the surface naturally. Coalescing separators add plate packs that force tiny droplets to merge into larger ones that rise faster, achieving equal or better separation in a much smaller footprint. Gravity units are simpler and cheaper; coalescing units fit tight sites and handle higher flows. The engineer selects based on available space, design flow, and the effluent limits the discharge permit requires.
Why do separators need sediment pretreatment?
Because grit and sand from wash water and shop floors settle into the separator's chambers, displacing working volume and eventually blinding the separation process. A separator filling with sediment loses residence time and starts passing oil. Upstream sand interceptors or sediment traps capture the solids where they are easy to clean out, protecting the separator's capacity. Skipping pretreatment is the fastest route to a failed unit.
How often does an oil-water separator need pumping?
It depends on loading, but most dealership separators need sludge and oil pump-outs at least quarterly, with inspections more often. The design includes the maintenance schedule, and many discharge permits require documented maintenance records. The engineer sizes access — chamber manholes, truck access — so pump-outs are routine rather than ordeals. A separator with no maintenance plan will fail; the only question is how quickly.
Can separator discharge go to the storm drain?
No. Separator effluent goes to the sanitary sewer under an industrial discharge permit — storm drains carry water untreated to waterways, and petroleum discharge there is an environmental violation. The plumbing design keeps the two systems completely separate: shop and wash drains to the separator and sanitary, roof and clean site runoff to storm. Cross-connections between the systems are one of the first things inspectors check.
