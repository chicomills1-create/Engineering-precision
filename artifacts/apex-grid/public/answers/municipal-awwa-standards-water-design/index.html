What AWWA Standards Govern Municipal Water Design?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

AWWA (American Water Works Association) standards are the consensus technical standards for drinking water infrastructure — covering pipes, valves, treatment equipment, storage tanks, disinfection, and construction practices. They're not law by themselves, but state drinking water programs and municipal specifications incorporate them by reference, making them contractually and regulatorily binding. Designing municipal water facilities without the applicable AWWA standards isn't just unprofessional — it usually violates the project specifications.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What AWWA is and why its standards govern

The American Water Works Association has published consensus standards since the early 1900s, developed by committees of utility engineers, manufacturers, and consultants under ANSI-accredited procedures. The standards carry authority through incorporation: state drinking water design standards (the Ten State Standards used across much of the country, and individual state codes elsewhere) reference AWWA standards for materials and construction; municipal standard specifications cite them for every pipe material, valve, hydrant, and meter; and project specifications invoke them for equipment and testing. When the spec says 'ductile iron pipe per AWWA C151/C150' — which it will — that's a binding requirement, not a suggestion.

The practical consequence for design engineers: the standards define the acceptable materials, the design methods, the manufacturing and testing requirements, and the installation and disinfection practices. An engineer who specifies a pipe material needs to know its AWWA standard — its pressure classes, its joint options, its corrosion-protection provisions — because the contractor will build to the standard and the inspector will inspect to it. Fluency in the applicable standards is table stakes for municipal water design.

The standards that come up on every project

Pipe and appurtenances: C900/C905 (PVC pressure pipe), C151 (ductile iron pipe), C906 (HDPE), C200/C301 (steel and concrete pressure pipe), C500-series (gate, butterfly, check, and air valves), C502 (dry-barrel fire hydrants). Every pipeline design lives in these standards — pressure class selection, joint restraint, fittings, and coatings. Storage: D100 (welded steel tanks), D103 (bolted steel tanks), D110/D115 (prestressed concrete tanks) — the structural and construction standards behind every municipal tank.

Treatment and disinfection: the D-series and treatment standards cover filtration media, the B-series covers treatment chemicals (coagulants, disinfectants — specifying chemical quality matters as much as dose), and C651 governs disinfection of water mains (the chlorination, flushing, and bacteriological testing procedure every new main must pass before going into service), C652 for storage facilities, C653 for treatment plants. Meters, backflow preventers, and service-line materials have their own standards. The engineer doesn't memorize all of them — but knows exactly which one governs each design element and designs inside it.

  • Pressure pipe: C900/C905 PVC, C151 ductile iron, C906 HDPE
  • Valves and hydrants: C500-series, C502 hydrants
  • Storage tanks: D100 welded steel, D110/D115 concrete
  • Disinfection of new facilities: C651/C652/C653
  • Treatment chemicals: B-series quality standards

How AWWA standards interact with state codes

The hierarchy runs: federal Safe Drinking Water Act → state drinking water regulations and design standards → AWWA (and ASTM, NSF) consensus standards incorporated by reference → municipal standard specs → project specifications. Each level can be stricter than the one above but not weaker. The Ten State Standards (Great Lakes–Upper Mississippi River Board) — used directly or adapted by many states — set the design criteria (detention times, loading rates, redundancy) while AWWA sets the material and construction standards; together they're the complete design code for waterworks.

NSF/ANSI 61 deserves special mention: it's the health-effects standard for anything contacting drinking water — pipe, coatings, treatment chemicals, fixtures. Every material in a water system must be NSF 61 certified; specifying a non-certified product is a compliance violation waiting for the state plan reviewer to catch. Similarly NSF 372 covers lead content. The competent municipal water engineer keeps the AWWA manual of practice (M-series — M22 for ductile iron, M23 for PVC, M31 for distribution requirements, M42 for steel pipe) alongside the standards: the manuals teach the design methods, the standards set the requirements.

What this means for project delivery

For municipalities: your standard specifications should reference current AWWA editions — outdated references (a spec citing a 1990s edition) create ambiguity contractors exploit. Updating standard specs to current standards is cheap insurance. For engineers: the submittal review process exists to verify compliance with the specified standards — pipe certifications, coating inspections, disinfection test results — and waiving it to save time is how noncompliant materials get buried.

For both: when something fails — a premature pipe failure, a coating failure, a disinfection that won't clear — the forensic question is always whether the applicable standard was followed in design, materials, and installation. Standards compliance is the liability framework of the water industry. Our designs specify current-edition AWWA standards throughout, our construction administration enforces them through submittals and inspection, and our standard-spec updates for municipal clients keep their bidding documents in the current code cycle. It's unglamorous work that prevents the failures that aren't.

What else do project teams ask?

Are AWWA standards legally mandatory?
By themselves they're voluntary consensus standards — but they become mandatory when state regulations, municipal specifications, or project contract documents incorporate them by reference, which is the near-universal practice. In effect, yes: the applicable AWWA standards bind every municipal water project.
What's the difference between AWWA standards and the Ten State Standards?
The Ten State Standards (Great Lakes-UMRB) set planning and design criteria for waterworks — sizing, detention times, redundancy. AWWA standards set material, manufacturing, and construction requirements — what the pipe, valves, tanks, and equipment must be and how they're installed and tested. They complement each other; most projects need both.
Which AWWA standard covers disinfection of new water mains?
AWWA C651 — Disinfecting Water Mains: the chlorination, flushing, and bacteriological testing procedure. Storage facilities fall under C652, treatment plants under C653. No new water facility goes into service without passing its disinfection standard.
How often are AWWA standards updated?
On roughly 5-year revision cycles per standard, through ANSI-accredited committee consensus. Project specs should cite the current edition — referencing a superseded edition can lock in outdated requirements or create compliance gaps with current state codes.
