Why Is Backflow Prevention Critical for Car Wash Plumbing?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Car wash backflow prevention protects the drinking-water supply from chemicals, reclaim water, and backsiphonage at every cross-connection — with RPZ assemblies on the service and hazard-matched protection at each chemical and hose connection.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Mapping every cross-connection

I start the backflow design with a cross-connection survey of the plumbing plans: every chemical proportioner, every reclaim connection, every boiler feed, every hose bibb, every submerged inlet, every irrigation zone. Each gets classified by hazard level and by whether the risk is backsiphonage, backpressure, or both. The survey drives the assembly schedule — RPZ where health hazards and backpressure exist, pressure vacuum breakers or atmospheric vacuum breakers where the hazard and hydraulics allow simpler devices, air gaps where the purveyor or code demands the absolute separation. Nothing connects a chemical or reclaim line to potable water without landing on this survey first.

Sizing, placement, and freeze protection

Each assembly is sized for its actual flow so pressure loss through the device does not starve downstream equipment — RPZ assemblies in particular have meaningful pressure drop, and I account for it in the booster and distribution calculations. Placement puts assemblies where they are accessible for annual testing, protected from freezing with heat trace or heated enclosures where the climate requires, and drained safely for relief-valve discharge. Outdoor RPZ installations get insulated enclosures with heat; indoor ones get floor drains and air gaps. I coordinate the locations with the architectural plans so the enclosures and clearances are real, not wishful.

Backflow prevention design checklist

The backflow design protects the public water supply when every connection is covered. I run through this checklist on each job. • Complete cross-connection survey with hazard classification at every potable connection • RPZ assembly on the main service with relief discharge piped safely to drain • Hazard-matched assemblies at chemical feed, reclaim, boiler, irrigation, and hose connections • Each assembly sized for its flow with pressure loss carried into the distribution design • Installations accessible for annual certified testing with records to the purveyor • Freeze protection and drainage provided at every assembly location

What else do project teams ask?

What makes a car wash a high-hazard facility for backflow?
The combination of toxic chemicals under pressure and multiple submerged or pressurized connections to the potable system. Concentrated detergents, acids, and waxes are health hazards if they reach drinking water, and the car wash connects them to the water supply through proportioners and feed pumps. Add reclaim water, submerged hoses, and high-pressure equipment that can create backpressure, and the purveyor has every reason to classify the site as high hazard. That classification is what drives the requirement for reduced-pressure-zone protection rather than a simpler device.
Where do backflow assemblies go in a car wash?
At the service entrance first — the RPZ assembly on the main water service protects the public main from everything downstream. Then at each internal cross-connection: chemical feed and proportioning equipment, the reclaim system's makeup connection, boiler and heating-system makeup, irrigation, and hose bibbs in the wash areas. I show every assembly on the plumbing plans with its type, size, and location, because the purveyor reviews the backflow design specifically and the plumbing inspector verifies the installations against the approved plan.
Why do RPZ assemblies need a drain?
Because their relief valve is designed to discharge water. An RPZ protects by dumping to atmosphere if either of its check valves fails or if backpressure develops — that discharge is the safety mechanism working as intended, and it has to go somewhere that will not flood the equipment room. I pipe the relief discharge to a floor drain or receptor with an air gap, sized for the discharge flow, and I never plug or cap a relief outlet. An RPZ installed without drainage will flood the room the first time it does its job.
How often must backflow assemblies be tested?
On the schedule the water purveyor sets, which is typically annual testing by a certified backflow tester, with records submitted to the purveyor. New installations get tested at startup before the water service is fully approved. I design the installations for testability — clear access to test cocks, isolation valves that hold, and enough working room for the tester — because an assembly buried behind equipment does not get tested on time. A failed test requires repair or replacement, and the purveyor can shut off water service for untested or failed assemblies.
