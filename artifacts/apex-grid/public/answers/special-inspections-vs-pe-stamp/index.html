Special Inspections vs PE Stamp: What's the Difference?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A PE stamp certifies that the design meets the code; special inspections verify in the field that the contractor built what the sealed drawings show. They are separate code functions — the engineer of record designs and seals, an independent inspector observes critical construction — and most commercial projects require both.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Two functions, one permit

Think of the permit as having a design half and a construction half. The design half ends with the PE's seal: calculations verified, details coordinated, code compliance certified on paper. The construction half is governed by the inspection program in the approved documents, and special inspections are its backbone for work that can't be checked later. Concrete buried in a foundation, welds covered by fireproofing, bolts hidden inside connections — once these are concealed, no final inspection can verify them. The code's answer is to require observation while the work is open, by someone qualified and independent. The engineer of record sits at the junction of the two halves. The sealed drawings define what the inspectors check against, and the EOR dispositions what the inspectors find. That is why the EOR's name is on both the design documents and the inspection correspondence: the design responsibility doesn't end when construction starts, it extends through verifying the design was executed.

Structural observation is the third role

Owners often hear a third term — structural observation — and lump it with special inspections. It is distinct. Structural observation is the engineer of record's own periodic site visits to assess general conformance of the structural work with the design concept: is the framing going up the way the drawings intend, are the lateral elements landing where designed? It is broader and less granular than special inspection, and it is performed by or under the EOR, not by an independent agency. Some jurisdictions and some structural systems require it explicitly; on other projects it is good practice the EOR includes in scope. The practical point is that a project can carry all three: the PE's sealed design, the special inspection program verifying critical workmanship, and the EOR's structural observation confirming the overall execution. Each answers a different question, and a well-run project staffs all three deliberately rather than discovering the gaps at final inspection.

Inspection and sealing checklist

Keep the three functions straight from permit application through closeout, and the closeout goes smoothly. Confusion between them is where delays breed. • PE-sealed drawings and calculations define the design — the baseline every inspection measures against • Special inspections listed in the approved documents, with an owner-hired independent agency engaged before construction starts • Inspection reports routed to the building official and the engineer of record as work proceeds, not batched at the end • EOR dispositions every reported deficiency — accept, repair, or replace — with the engineering rationale documented • Structural observation by the EOR scheduled at the milestones the design's risk profile warrants • Final inspection and certificate of occupancy supported by a clean inspection record and the EOR's closeout documentation

What else do project teams ask?

Who hires the special inspector — the owner or the contractor?
The owner hires the special inspector, and that is deliberate. The code requires the inspector's independence from the contractor whose work is being inspected — an inspector on the contractor's payroll has a structural conflict of interest. In practice the owner often delegates the hiring to the architect or engineer of record, but the contract and the reporting line stay owner-side. The inspector's reports go to the building official with copies to the engineer of record. If a contractor offers to 'handle' special inspections in-house, that arrangement does not satisfy the code's independence requirement.
What kinds of work typically require special inspections?
The code lists them by material and risk: concrete placement and reinforcement, structural masonry, structural steel including bolting and welding, wood framing in high-seismic or high-wind categories, soils and foundations work like driven piles or compacted fill, sprayed fire-resistive materials, and certain MEP-adjacent items like smoke control systems. The exact list depends on the adopted code edition and the project's seismic and wind design categories — a warehouse in coastal Florida and an office in Phoenix will have different inspection line items. Your approved permit documents spell out which inspections apply to your project.
Does the engineer of record perform the special inspections?
Generally no — and the roles are intentionally separate. The engineer of record may perform structural observation, which is a different, lighter-touch function: periodic site visits to confirm the general conformance of the work with the design concept. Special inspections are the detailed, item-by-item field verification performed by qualified inspectors under an approved agency. The EOR reviews special inspection reports and dispositions deficiencies, but does not self-inspect the contractor's work. Keeping the roles distinct protects everyone, including the owner.
What happens if special inspection reports show deficiencies?
The inspector documents the non-conforming work and reports it to the building official and the engineer of record. The EOR then makes the engineering call: accept as-is if it still meets the design intent, require repair per a defined procedure, or require replacement. That disposition is an engineering judgment the EOR owns — the inspector reports facts, the engineer decides consequences. Unresolved deficiencies hold up final inspection and the certificate of occupancy, which is why I tell owners to get inspection reports routed to the EOR immediately rather than letting them pile up until closeout.
