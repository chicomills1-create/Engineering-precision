FDA Food Facility Design Requirements (21 CFR 110/117 cGMP)

FDA food facility design requirements come from the current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMPs) — 21 CFR Part 117, which carries forward the facility-design principles originally written as Part 110. The regulation reads less like a building code and more like an operating philosophy: the plant must be designed so that food can be manufactured, packed, and held under sanitary conditions. Everything in your drawings — floors, walls, drainage, lighting, ventilation — gets judged against that standard.

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran

The building envelope: floors, walls, ceilings, lighting

The cGMPs require floors, walls, and ceilings that can be adequately cleaned and kept in good repair. In engineering terms: floors are sealed, sloped to drains, and coved at wall junctions; walls are smooth and impervious in wet areas; ceilings over exposed product prevent condensation and flaking. The regulation doesn't name materials — inspectors do. Standing water, peeling paint over a line, or a porous wall in a washdown zone will all draw findings.

Lighting gets its own clause: adequate lighting in all areas where food is examined, manufactured, processed, packed, or held, plus safety-type fixtures or shatter protection over exposed product. That sentence is why food plants use shatterproof lenses and sealed LED high-bays — design it in, don't value-engineer it out.

Ventilation and plumbing: the invisible compliance layer

The rule requires adequate ventilation and control equipment to minimize odors and vapors (particularly in areas where they may contaminate food) and to minimize the potential for allergen cross-contact and contamination. Practically, this means positive pressure in high-care and packaging areas, exhaust where steam and heat are generated, and air handling that doesn't blow from raw zones into finished zones. Air balance drawings are a cGMP document — your engineer should produce them.

Plumbing must be of adequate size and design, installed and maintained to carry sufficient water and to properly convey sewage and liquid disposable waste. Cross-connections between potable and non-potable systems are prohibited. Backflow prevention, air gaps at hose bibbs, and clearly identified non-potable lines are standard design responses.

Sanitary facilities and the maintainability mandate

Adequate, readily accessible toilet facilities must be provided, and the rule pointedly requires that doors to toilet rooms not open directly into areas where food is exposed unless alternate means protect against contamination — self-closing doors and positive air pressure are the design answer. Hand-washing facilities must be adequate, convenient, and furnished with running water at a suitable temperature. These read like common sense until an inspector finds a production line ten steps from a restroom door with no airlock.

The deepest requirement is the one most plants violate first: the plant must be constructed to facilitate maintenance and sanitary operations. That means equipment spaced for cleaning access, utilities routed so maintenance doesn't happen over open product, and materials that survive the chemicals the sanitation crew uses. Design for the sanitation shift, not just the production shift — the inspector walks the plant at both.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does 21 CFR 110 still apply, or is it Part 117 now?

Yes — existing plants must comply with cGMPs in daily operation, and any renovation, expansion, or new construction should be designed to the current rule. Regulators inspect against 21 CFR Part 117 (the modernized cGMP regulation, which superseded Part 110's numbering in 2015 but carries the same facility-design principles). Designing to cGMP today means 117-compliant.

What are the most common cGMP design failures?

The usual design misses are floor drainage (standing water is an automatic inspection finding), condensation over exposed product, and inadequate separation between raw and finished areas. All three are fixed in design for pennies on the dollar of retrofit cost.

What materials does cGMP require?

Food-contact surfaces must be corrosion-resistant and smooth — in practice, stainless steel or food-grade coatings — and cleanable without harboring pathogens. Structural materials around them must not shed or flake.
