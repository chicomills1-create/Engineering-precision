How Is a Chicago Alley Coach House Designed and Permitted?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A Chicago alley coach house is designed by confirming ADU zoning eligibility, structurally designing the garage and dwelling above as one building, providing fire separation and egress, sizing utility connections, and submitting permit drawings - reviewed by the Chicago Department of Buildings, which decides approvals.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Zoning Comes Before Design

Accessory dwelling unit eligibility is lot-specific. The team confirms the zoning district permits ADUs, measures the required setbacks from the alley, side lot lines, and the main house, and checks maximum height, floor area, and lot coverage against the proposed design. Parking requirements, rear-yard open space rules, and utility easements along the alley further constrain the footprint. Each of these is verified against the current ordinance, because ADU rules have expanded in phases and older summaries are unreliable. Two constraints kill more projects than any other. The first is lot coverage and open space: on a small lot with a large main house, there may simply be no envelope left for a coach house. The second is landmark district review, which can dictate roof forms, materials, and massing that reshape the design. Discovering either after drawings are complete wastes the entire design fee, which is why the zoning analysis is a formal first deliverable, not a hallway conversation.

One Building, Two Uses

Structurally, the coach house is a small mixed-use building. Foundations are designed for the combined dead and live loads of the garage and dwelling, bearing on soils verified by the geotechnical assumptions for the area, with frost-depth footings per code. The garage level is framed to carry the dwelling above, whether in wood framing over a masonry or concrete garage base or in full wood-frame construction, and the floor assembly between garage and unit provides the code-required fire separation with protected structural members. Egress and life safety follow residential code: a code-compliant stair from the unit to grade, emergency escape and rescue openings at sleeping rooms, smoke and carbon monoxide detection, and separation of the garage's vehicle use from the dwelling, including the door between them and any duct penetrations. The stair's location drives the floor plan, since it must land in the alley-facing yard without violating setbacks, and its enclosure is detailed for both fire rating and Chicago winter weather.

Utilities and Site Work

Services reach the coach house from the street or from the main house, and each path has trade-offs. A new water service and sewer connection from the street gives the unit independence but requires excavation across the lot or alley; tying into the main house's services is cheaper but must be verified for capacity and may complicate metering. Electrical service is typically separately metered, with the service size calculated for the unit's heating, cooling, appliances, and any electric vehicle charging in the garage below. Site work addresses the realities of alley construction. Stormwater from the new roof and paved areas is managed with grading, dry wells, or detention per city requirements so runoff does not flood the alley or adjacent lots. Construction access is planned around the alley's width and overhead wires, and the foundation excavation is sequenced to protect the main house, neighboring garages, and the alley itself. The permit set documents all of it, because the plan reviewer checks site work as carefully as structure.

What else do project teams ask?

What is a Chicago coach house?
A dwelling unit built above a garage, facing the alley behind the main house. It is a historic Chicago housing type that zoning prohibited for decades and the city's accessory dwelling unit ordinance has made buildable again. Most projects are one- or two-bedroom units above a two-car garage on a standard residential lot.
Can I build a coach house on any Chicago lot?
No. The lot must be in a zoning district that allows accessory dwelling units and must satisfy setbacks, height, floor-area, lot coverage, and open-space requirements. Landmark districts add design review. A formal zoning analysis of the specific lot comes before any design work.
How is fire separation handled between the garage and the home above?
The floor assembly between the garage and the dwelling is built as a code-required fire-rated separation, structural members are protected to the required rating, the door between garage and dwelling is fire-rated, and duct and pipe penetrations are sealed with listed firestop systems. The design follows the residential code's provisions for separation of vehicle and dwelling uses.
Do Chicago suburbs allow coach houses too?
Each municipality sets its own accessory dwelling unit rules: some, like Evanston and Oak Park, have their own ADU provisions, while others prohibit them outright. Unincorporated Cook County follows county zoning. The rules, and the permit path, differ by jurisdiction, so the local authority having jurisdiction is confirmed before design begins.
