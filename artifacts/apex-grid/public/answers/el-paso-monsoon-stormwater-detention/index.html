How Is El Paso Monsoon Stormwater Detained?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

El Paso monsoon runoff is captured in detention ponds, underground vaults, or regional basins that store the intense short-burst runoff and release it at pre-development rates. The storage is sized from local rainfall data and site imperviousness, and the design is reviewed through the city or county drainage approval.

Design Storms and Hydrologic Modeling

Detention design starts with the rainfall the criteria require the engineer to design for. Local drainage manuals specify the design storms, typically expressed as rainfall depths for standard durations and return periods, derived from the region's rainfall records. The engineer applies those storms to the site's drainage areas, using runoff methods suited to small urban watersheds, and develops inflow hydrographs for both the existing and proposed conditions. The developed site, with its roofs, parking lots, and streets, produces a taller, faster hydrograph than the desert it replaced, and the pond must shave that peak back down. Routing the hydrograph through the proposed pond is the core calculation. The engineer sizes the storage volume and the outlet structure, orifices, weirs, or a combination, so the pond fills during the burst and drains at the allowable release rate, emptying within the drawdown window the criteria set so the pond is ready for the next storm. Multiple design storms are checked, because the pond that handles the common storm must also safely pass the rare one, with emergency overflow routes that send extreme flows where they cannot damage buildings. The calculations, the pond grading plan, and the outlet details are submitted together for drainage review.

Ponds, Vaults, and Desert Maintenance Realities

A surface pond is a land use as well as a hydraulic structure. The engineer grades it with gentle side slopes for safety and maintenance access, sets the outlet structure where it can be inspected and cleaned, and designs the bottom to drain completely so it does not become a mosquito-breeding sump between storms. Landscaping uses desert-adapted plantings that survive both the pond's dry months and its brief inundations, and the maintenance plan assigns responsibility for sediment removal, outlet clearing, and vegetation control, because a pond full of sediment stores no stormwater. Underground detention trades land for maintenance burden. Vaults and oversized-pipe systems under parking lots preserve developable area, but every cubic foot of storage is invisible, so the design includes access hatches, sediment sumps, and a maintenance schedule the owner can actually execute. In El Paso's sediment-heavy runoff, pretreatment, forebays or hydrodynamic separators, keeps the vault from silting up. The engineer weighs these trade-offs with the owner openly: the cheapest detention to build is often the most expensive to neglect, and the AHJ's inspectors will be back to check that the approved system is still functioning years later.

What else do project teams ask?

Why does El Paso need detention if it barely rains?
Because when it rains, it pours. Monsoon bursts drop intense rainfall onto baked, nearly impervious desert ground, producing flash runoff far out of proportion to the annual rainfall total. Development replaces absorbent desert with roofs and pavement, multiplying that runoff. Detention holds the added volume and releases it slowly so downstream streets and arroyos are not overwhelmed by the new development's stormwater.
How is a detention pond sized?
The engineer models the design storms the local drainage criteria specify, computes the runoff hydrographs for the site before and after development, and sizes the pond's storage volume and outlet so the post-development peak discharge does not exceed the pre-development rate. The pond must also drain within the required drawdown time and safely pass larger storms through an emergency overflow.
Can infiltration replace detention in the desert?
Rarely without proof. El Paso's caliche layers and baked desert soils infiltrate very little during an intense burst, so the design cannot assume infiltration will absorb the design storm unless field testing demonstrates it. Where testing does show usable infiltration, it can supplement detention, but the pond or vault is still sized for the storm the criteria require.
Who reviews stormwater detention in El Paso?
The drainage reviewer of the jurisdiction where the parcel sits: the City of El Paso for city parcels, El Paso County for unincorporated parcels. Each enforces its own drainage criteria and submittal requirements, and drainage approval typically gates the building or development permit. The AHJ decides what is approved, so the engineer designs to the correct jurisdiction's manual from the start.
