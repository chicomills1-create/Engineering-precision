How Does Commercial Development Work with the Cherokee Nation?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Commercial development with the Cherokee Nation runs through tribal jurisdiction across its 14-county Oklahoma reservation, with Cherokee Nation Businesses driving much of the commercial pipeline. Trust-land projects follow tribal review and tribal-court contract jurisdiction, while fee-land projects inside cities like Tahlequah follow municipal permitting. Engineers confirm land status first, then permit with the correct authority.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Tribal Jurisdiction and the Cherokee Courts

The Cherokee Nation's governmental authority is exercised through its executive, legislative, and judicial branches, including a two-tiered court system with district courts in Tahlequah and Jay and a supreme court for appeals. For commercial work, the courts matter contractually: the Nation's standard construction terms place exclusive jurisdiction for claims in the Courts of the Cherokee Nation, waive binding arbitration, and route disputes through the Nation's chief of staff and the contractor's executive before any filing. Engineers and contractors entering Nation work should have counsel review these provisions, because they differ materially from standard AIA-style dispute terms. Regulatory jurisdiction follows the familiar land-status pattern. The Nation governs trust lands; cities govern within their limits on non-trust land; counties cover unincorporated fee areas. Because the 14-county reservation includes major population centers like Tahlequah, many commercial sites sit at the intersection of tribal, city, and county interests. Environmental review, utility service, and access can each involve a different authority than the building permit does. The project team maps every approval, building, grading, utility, environmental, and access, to its owner at the start.

Coordinating Tribal, City, and County Review

On the municipal track, Tahlequah's published process is representative: commercial and multifamily applicants submit building plans, a site plan, and a preliminary plat to the building inspector, who routes them through Technical Advisory Committee review, then planning and zoning, then city council before the permit issues. Timelines reflect committee meeting cycles, so submittals are planned around the calendar. Structural, MEP, and civil design follow the code the city has adopted, and inspections run through the city's inspectors. On the tribal track, review runs through the Nation's departments and, for Nation-owned development, through Cherokee Nation Businesses' project delivery process. Design standards follow the code the Nation applies to the project, federal grant requirements attach where federal money is involved, and cultural-resource coordination follows tribal protocols. Mixed-track projects, a Nation facility on trust land with city utility connections, for example, need both tracks coordinated so the city utility agreement and the tribal building approval do not hold each other up. Early joint coordination meetings are the standard tool for keeping the tracks aligned.

What else do project teams ask?

Does the Cherokee Nation have its own building department?
The Nation exercises governmental authority over its trust lands, and its departments review Nation and trust-land projects. Commercial projects on fee land inside municipalities follow city permitting instead. Because the 14-county reservation mixes trust, allotment, and fee land, the project team confirms the permitting authority parcel by parcel at kickoff rather than assuming one department covers the whole reservation.
What is Cherokee Nation Businesses?
It is the Nation's holding company for its commercial enterprises, operating across gaming, construction, aerospace and defense, manufacturing, technology, real estate, and healthcare. It is a major developer and employer in northeastern Oklahoma, with an estimated $1.06 billion annual economic impact on the state economy and more than 13,000 supported jobs. Much of the region's large-scale commercial construction flows through its project pipeline.
Do city permits apply to projects on Cherokee trust land?
No. On tribal trust land, the tribal government is the authority having jurisdiction, and city building departments do not issue permits there. City permits apply to fee-land projects inside city limits. Where a trust-land project needs city utilities or connects to city infrastructure, the team coordinates a utility or encroachment agreement with the city while the building itself is permitted tribally.
Why do Cherokee Nation contracts specify tribal court jurisdiction?
The Nation's standard construction terms place exclusive jurisdiction over claims in the Courts of the Cherokee Nation in Tahlequah and expressly waive binding arbitration, with a structured negotiation process before any filing. It is an exercise of tribal sovereignty over the Nation's own contracting. Engineers, contractors, and developers should have counsel review these terms before bidding, since they change the dispute-resolution and risk picture versus typical commercial contracts.
