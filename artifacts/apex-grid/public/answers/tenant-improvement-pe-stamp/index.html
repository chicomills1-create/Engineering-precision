Does a Tenant Improvement Need a PE Stamp?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Tenant improvements need a PE stamp when they modify structure, add MEP loads, or change egress or occupancy — new openings, kitchens, medical or dental buildouts, and added HVAC or electrical all trigger engineered review. Cosmetic-only TIs typically don't.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Structural review in an existing building

TI structural work is surgery on someone else's building, and the review treats it that way. I start by establishing what exists: the structural system, which walls are bearing, where the lateral elements are, and what the floor structure can carry. Proposed openings get analyzed for their effect on gravity and lateral behavior — a new storefront in a shear wall line is a lateral redesign, not a header schedule. New loads get checked against the existing structure's capacity: the rooftop unit on the TI's new HVAC, the file room's floor loading, the equipment platform. Every structural modification gets designed, detailed, and sealed as its own engineered scope within the TI set. In seismic jurisdictions — California TIs especially — the lateral check is the heart of the structural review. Openings in shear walls, removed wall segments, and added mass all change the building's seismic behavior, and the code's existing-building provisions govern what's permitted. I verify the TI's lateral design against those provisions, detail the connections and holdowns the modification requires, and confirm the work doesn't trigger a broader seismic retrofit the owner didn't budget for. That last check — scope containment — is the TI structural review's most valuable deliverable.

MEP review against base building capacity

The MEP review is a capacity study wrapped in a design review. I calculate the TI's new loads — cooling and heating for the reconfigured space, lighting and receptacle, specialty equipment from dental chairs to commercial cooking — and verify each against the base building system's documented capacity. HVAC gets the load calculation and a check that the existing system can deliver the air and the conditioning: zone by zone, not just in total tons. Electrical gets the panel and feeder analysis with the TI's loads added as the code requires. Plumbing gets fixture counts against the riser capacity and the sanitary system's ability to accept the new fixtures. Where the TI changes the space's use — retail to restaurant, office to medical — the MEP review essentially becomes new-construction engineering inside an existing shell, because the load profiles are entirely different. Grease waste, medical gas, added ventilation: the TI set designs these as new systems connecting to base building infrastructure, and the PE verifies both the new design and the connection point. Landlord approval of the MEP scope runs alongside the engineering, since the base building systems are the landlord's asset.

Tenant improvement PE checklist

A TI submittal is ready for the PE's seal when the design respects the base building's realities. The fastest TI permits come from verifying capacity before designing, not after. • Base building structural, MEP, and permit documents obtained — or field verification scoped where they're missing • Proposed demolition verified against the structural system: no load-bearing surprises • New openings and added loads structurally designed, with lateral effects checked in seismic jurisdictions • TI MEP loads calculated and verified against base building electrical, HVAC, and plumbing capacity • Accessibility scope for the alteration addressed, with structural and Method implications coordinated • Landlord approval of structural and MEP scope secured alongside the engineering

What else do project teams ask?

What TI work triggers structural engineering?
New openings in bearing walls, shear walls, or floor structures; added equipment loads like rooftop units or heavy safes and file systems; mezzanines or storage platforms; and any change to the lateral system. Even 'simple' TI demolition can trigger it — removing a wall that turns out to be structural is a classic TI emergency. I review the proposed demolition and new construction against the base building's structural drawings, verify what's load-bearing before anyone swings a hammer, and design the new headers, frames, or reinforcement. The PE's structural review is what keeps TI demolition from becoming structural damage.
How does the PE verify base building MEP capacity?
From the base building's documents and field verification: the electrical service size and the panel schedules serving the tenant space, the HVAC system's design capacity and what's currently connected, and the plumbing riser diagrams showing available connections. I build the TI's added loads — HVAC, lighting, receptacle, equipment — and check them against the documented spare capacity. Where base building documents are missing, field verification fills in: panel surveys, equipment nameplates, tracing ductwork. If the TI exceeds available capacity, the design includes the upgrade — bigger feeder, new rooftop unit, additional plumbing riser — with landlord coordination, before the seal goes on.
Do TI permits need to address accessibility?
Yes — alterations trigger accessibility requirements, and the TI design has to address them. The scope depends on the jurisdiction and the extent of the alteration: the path of travel, restrooms serving the space, entrances, and drinking fountains are the usual items. This is primarily an architectural and code-compliance scope, but where the accessibility upgrades involve structural work — ramps, restroom reconfigurations with new plumbing, widened openings — the PE's review covers the engineering. I coordinate the structural and MEP implications of the accessibility scope so the TI set doesn't get a correction round from that direction.
Who provides the base building drawings for a TI review?
Usually the landlord or property manager, and getting them early is one of the highest-leverage TI moves. The base building's structural drawings, MEP as-builts, and the original permit documents let the PE verify capacities from paper instead of from field investigation. When they're unavailable — common in older buildings with multiple ownership changes — the review includes field verification to establish the existing conditions the TI design relies on. I tell tenants to ask the landlord for the drawing set in the first lease conversation; TI engineering goes twice as fast with base building documents in hand.
