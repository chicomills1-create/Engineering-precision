How Do Grand Junction Foundations Handle Collapsible Soil?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Grand Junction's collapsible soils settle suddenly when wetted, a failure called hydrocollapse. Engineers mitigate it by overexcavating the collapsible zone and replacing it with compacted engineered fill, or by drilling piers through it to stable material or Mancos Shale bedrock, always paired with strict moisture control around irrigation, utilities, and drainage.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Why Collapsible Soils Fail When They Get Wet

Strength in a collapsible soil is an illusion maintained by dryness. The soil grains sit in a loose packing held by weak clay bridges and suction forces that vanish on wetting; add water and the structure collapses into the denser arrangement it always wanted, with the ground surface following it down. The settlement is sudden compared to ordinary consolidation, which is why buildings show distress quickly after the wetting event: a new irrigation pattern, a broken water line, or a season of heavy canal seepage. The collapse potential measured in the lab tells the engineer how severe the site's version of this behavior is. Differential wetting makes it worse. A building whose north side sits over irrigated landscaping while the south side stays dry settles unevenly, and foundations tolerate uniform settlement far better than differential. That is why the moisture-control plan treats the whole building perimeter as a system: consistent grading, consistent irrigation setbacks, and drainage that never concentrates water against one part of the foundation. Post-construction changes, adding a lawn zone against the house or regrading a swale toward the building, can undo the design years later.

Foundation Strategies for the Grand Valley

Overexcavation and replacement is the workhorse fix for moderate collapsible zones. The contractor excavates the collapsible soil beneath the building footprint to the depth the geotechnical report specifies, moisture-conditions it, and replaces it in compacted lifts as engineered fill, effectively manufacturing a non-collapsible bearing layer. The method is economical where the collapsible zone is shallow enough to dig, and it pairs well with conventional slabs and footings designed for the improved subgrade. Observation and compaction testing during the earthwork verify the fix was actually built. Where the collapsible zone runs deep, drilled piers bypass it. Shafts extend through the collapsible soils into stable material or Mancos Shale bedrock, and structural floors span between piers so the building never bears on the collapsible layer at all. The design accounts for downdrag: if the surrounding soil later wets and settles, it drags down on the pier shafts, and the engineer sizes the piers for that added load. Either strategy stands or falls on moisture control afterward, which is why the best Grand Valley foundation designs read as much like water-management plans as structural drawings.

What else do project teams ask?

What makes soil collapsible?
Collapsible soils are loose, dry, lightly cemented deposits, often wind-blown silt or alluvium, whose grains are held in an open structure by weak bonds. While dry they can carry building loads, but wetting dissolves the bonds and the grains collapse into denser packing, causing sudden settlement. The Grand Valley's geology produces these soils across the metro.
How is collapse potential tested?
The geotechnical engineer runs collapse tests on undisturbed samples, typically wetting a loaded specimen in an oedometer and measuring the resulting settlement. The collapse index classifies the soil from slight to severe. Borings map how thick the collapsible zone is, which determines whether overexcavation or deep foundations is the right fix.
Why is irrigation a foundation issue in Grand Junction?
The Grand Valley is irrigated farmland, and canal seepage, flood irrigation, and seasonal groundwater rises can wet soils beneath buildings. Irrigation aimed at or near foundations is a classic trigger of hydrocollapse. Designs set irrigation back from buildings and manage drainage so wetting stays consistent and away from the foundation.
Can you build a slab-on-grade on collapsible soil?
Only after the collapsible zone is mitigated. Overexcavation and recompaction can create a suitable bearing layer for slabs where the zone is shallow; where it runs deep, drilled piers with structural floors bypass it. An unmitigated slab on collapsible soil is a settlement failure waiting for the first wetting event.
What is the difference between collapsible and expansive soil?
They move in opposite directions for the same reason. Collapsible soil settles downward when wetted as its loose structure collapses; expansive clay heaves upward when wetted as its minerals swell. The fixes differ too: collapsible sites need densification or bypass, while expansive sites need piers below the active zone or engineered stiffened slabs.
