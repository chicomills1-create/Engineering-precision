Who Can Stamp My MEP Plans?

Professional engineers licensed in the mechanical, electrical and plumbing disciplines, in the state where the project will be built. Apex's MEP engineers review your mechanical, electrical and plumbing sets — loads, sizing, equipment selection, code compliance — coordinate them against the structural and architectural drawings, and seal each discipline when its responsible PE approves the work.

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-21

The short answer

Professional engineers licensed in the mechanical, electrical and plumbing disciplines, in the state where the project will be built. Apex's MEP engineers review your mechanical, electrical and plumbing sets — loads, sizing, equipment selection, code compliance — coordinate them against the structural and architectural drawings, and seal each discipline when its responsible PE approves the work.

Three disciplines, three seals

The M in MEP is a mechanical engineer answering for HVAC loads, ventilation, duct and piping design and energy-code compliance. The E is an electrical engineer answering for service sizing, distribution, fault protection and life-safety systems' power. The P is a plumbing engineer answering for water, waste, vent and gas systems sized to the fixture load. Some engineers hold licenses spanning more than one of these; no one's license spans a state line. The sealing engineer for each discipline must be licensed in the project's state, and the review behind each seal must be genuine — responsible charge applies to a duct system exactly as it does to a beam.

Coordination is where MEP reviews earn their fee

MEP sets rarely fail inside a discipline; they fail between them. The air handler the mechanical set specifies doesn't fit the structural steel's clearances. The electrical room shrinks after the service was sized. The plumbing riser lands where the architecture puts a corridor. A discipline-by-discipline stamp shop can seal all three sets and miss every one of those collisions, because nobody reviewed the interfaces. Apex reviews MEP as a system: the three disciplines checked against each other and against the structural and architectural sets, with clashes and gaps returned as one consolidated correction list — before the plans examiner finds them on the jurisdiction's clock instead of yours.

What to send

The MEP drawing sets at their current revision, the load and sizing calculations behind them, equipment schedules or selections where the design depends on them, and the architectural and structural sets they're coordinated against — plus the project address, and any jurisdiction comments already in hand. Scoping returns a written quote for the review with its timeline stated. Where an MEP discipline hasn't been designed yet, that's not a review engagement — it's design. Apex performs full MEP design as engineer of record; name it at intake and it will be scoped as such, honestly separated from review work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a structural engineer stamp MEP plans?
No — the seal must match the discipline. Structural licensure covers structural work; mechanical, electrical and plumbing sets are sealed by engineers licensed in those disciplines in the project's state.
Our mechanical contractor produced the drawings. Can they be sealed?
Yes, through third-party review: Apex's engineers examine the design and calculations, require corrections where the work falls short, and seal under responsible charge when it approves. Contractor-produced design is a routine intake here.
What if only one MEP discipline needs sealing?
Then the review is scoped to that discipline — with its interfaces checked against the rest of the set, because a sealed mechanical set that collides with the structure helps no one.
