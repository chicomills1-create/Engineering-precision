How Do You Bid on Municipal Engineering Contracts?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

You don't 'bid' on municipal engineering the way contractors bid on construction — in most states, public agencies must select engineers through qualifications-based selection (QBS), not low bid. The agency issues an RFQ, firms respond with statements of qualifications, an evaluation committee scores them on experience, key personnel, past performance, and approach, and the agency negotiates scope and fee with the top-ranked firm. Winning means being the most convincingly qualified and responsive firm — not the cheapest.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Brooks Act and why low-bid doesn't apply

The federal Brooks Act of 1972 requires federal agencies to select architects and engineers on qualifications, with fee negotiated after selection. Most states have 'mini-Brooks' laws extending the same principle to state and local procurement. The logic is straightforward: engineering is a professional service where the cheapest provider is often the most expensive outcome — under-designed documents generate change orders, delays, and failures that dwarf any fee savings. So the law takes price off the table during selection and puts qualifications on it.

In practice, the municipality issues a Request for Qualifications describing the project — a treatment plant upgrade, a bridge rehabilitation program, an on-call services contract — and firms respond with SF-330-style qualifications packages (the federal standard form most agencies mirror): firm background, relevant project experience, resumes of key personnel, past performance references, and a project approach. An evaluation committee scores the responses against published criteria. The top-ranked firm is invited to negotiate scope and fee; if negotiations fail, the agency moves to the second-ranked firm. Price enters only after qualifications have decided the winner.

What evaluation committees actually score

Having responded to and won municipal RFQs across the country, I can tell you what separates winning packages from the pile. First: directly relevant experience. A committee hiring for a wastewater treatment upgrade wants to see wastewater treatment upgrades — not a list of every project the firm has ever touched. Ten directly relevant projects beat fifty loosely related ones. Second: key personnel. Committees score the actual engineers who will do the work — their licenses in the jurisdiction, their project histories, their availability. A package fronted by a business-development principal with the real team buried on page 40 loses to the package where the project manager's relevant experience leads.

Third: past performance with public clients. Municipal references who will say the firm delivered on schedule, handled construction-phase issues well, and stayed within the negotiated fee carry enormous weight — committees call them. Fourth: project approach. The winning approach demonstrates understanding of the municipality's actual constraints: the funding source and its deadlines, the permit pathway, the need to keep facilities in service during construction, the local review processes. Generic approach language ('we will provide quality engineering services') scores nothing. Specific understanding of their project scores everything.

  • Directly relevant project experience — not a general portfolio
  • Key personnel licensed in the jurisdiction, actually assigned to the project
  • Municipal references committees will call
  • A project approach written around their constraints
  • Understanding of their funding source and permit pathway

On-call contracts: the recurring revenue of public work

Much municipal engineering flows through on-call (indefinite delivery/indefinite quantity) contracts: the agency qualifies several firms for 3-5 year terms, then issues task orders for individual projects as needs arise. For a firm, winning an on-call slot is winning a pipeline — dozens of small-to-medium projects without re-competing each one. For the municipality, it's a bench of pre-qualified engineers available on short notice.

On-call RFQs are scored the same way — qualifications, personnel, approach — but with emphasis on responsiveness and breadth: can this firm handle a lift station emergency in February and a treatment-plant study in June? Our integrated civil-structural-MEP team is built for exactly that breadth, and our response times are the differentiator municipalities remember when the next task order is assigned. The firms that treat on-call work as low-priority filler lose the task orders to firms that treat every task order like the project that wins the next contract.

After selection: negotiating scope and fee

Selection is step one; negotiation is step two. The agency and the top-ranked firm agree on the detailed scope, schedule, and fee — typically structured as lump sum for well-defined design work, hourly for studies and undefined scopes, or a hybrid. This is where the municipality's procurement staff earns its keep, and where the firm's honesty matters: a scope that pretends undefined work is defined will blow up during design.

My advice to municipalities evaluating proposals: score the team you'll actually get, check references with specific questions about schedule performance and construction-phase responsiveness, and be wary of the firm whose key personnel are spread across twenty pursuits. And my advice to firms: never lowball the negotiated fee to win — the municipality selected you for qualifications, and delivering excellent work at a fair fee is how you win the next RFQ. The municipal market is small and long-memoried; reputation is the business model.

What else do project teams ask?

Do municipalities have to take the lowest bid for engineering?
No — in most states, the law requires the opposite. Qualifications-based selection (federal Brooks Act and state mini-Brooks laws) mandates that agencies select engineers on qualifications and negotiate fee afterward. Low-bid selection of professional engineering services is the exception, not the rule, in public procurement.
What is an RFQ vs. an RFP in municipal procurement?
An RFQ (Request for Qualifications) asks firms to demonstrate qualifications without pricing — used for QBS selection of engineers. An RFP (Request for Proposals) typically asks for approach and pricing together — used for construction and some design-build procurements. For pure engineering services, the RFQ is the standard instrument.
How long does municipal engineer selection take?
Typically 2-4 months from RFQ issuance to executed contract: 3-4 weeks for responses, evaluation committee scoring, shortlist interviews for larger projects, then scope and fee negotiation. On-call contract selections can run longer due to the multi-year commitment.
Can a small firm win municipal work against national firms?
Regularly. Evaluation committees score relevant experience and key personnel — not firm size. A focused firm with directly relevant project experience and licensed engineers in the jurisdiction routinely beats national firms whose packages are generic. Responsiveness and local accountability are advantages small and mid-size firms should lead with.
