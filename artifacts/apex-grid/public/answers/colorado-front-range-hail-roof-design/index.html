How Are Colorado Front Range Roofs Designed for Hail?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Colorado Front Range roofs are designed for hail with impact-resistant roof coverings, solid decking and storm-rated fastening, slopes and drainage that shed cloudburst runoff, and structural capacity for snow at foothill elevations - specified for each site's exposure and reviewed by the city or county building department.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

How Hail Damages Roofs

Hailstones damage roofing by kinetic energy: a two-inch stone falling at terminal velocity strikes with enough force to fracture brittle materials and bruise flexible ones. On asphalt shingles the signature damage is granule loss at the impact point exposing the asphalt mat, plus fractures in the mat itself that may not be visible until the shingle is lifted. That damage shortens roof life because UV attacks the exposed asphalt and the fractured mat can no longer shed water reliably - a roof can be functionally failed while looking only mildly worn from the street. Each roof type fails in its own way, which is why the design is assembly-specific. Wood shakes split along the grain; concrete and clay tile crack through individual pieces while the underlayment beneath becomes the real waterproofing; single-ply membranes puncture or fracture, especially where the insulation beneath is soft; metal dents, with the dent depth depending on gauge and profile but the weathertightness usually surviving. The engineer reads hail as a load like any other - an impact load with a statistical exposure - and specifies the assembly whose tested resistance meets it, rather than hoping a standard roof survives a hailstorm it was never designed for.

Impact-Resistant Roof Assemblies

Impact resistance is specified by tested classification, not by marketing. Asphalt shingles are tested by dropping steel balls onto the shingle and examining the damage, with the highest impact class indicating resistance to the largest test impacts - the engineer writes that class into the specification along with the underlayment system, because the underlayment is the second line of defense when the covering is bruised. In hail-prone Front Range markets the top impact class has become the default specification for shingle roofs, and many insurers offer premium recognition for it, which helps the economics. Metal roofing earns its hail reputation structurally: a standing-seam steel or aluminum panel over solid decking distributes impact energy across the panel and the substrate, denting cosmetically long before it breaches. Stone-coated steel adds the profile of tile or shake with steel's impact behavior. Tile, slate, and concrete products resist hail by mass and hardness but require the structure to carry their considerable weight - the engineer checks the existing framing before specifying them on a re-roof. Low-slope assemblies get a cover board - high-density polyiso or gypsum - between the membrane and the insulation, a cheap layer that dramatically improves puncture and hail resistance. Every assembly is fastened to the wind pressures the code assigns, because Front Range hailstorms arrive with gusts that test the roof's attachment as well as its surface.

Drainage, Snow, and the Permit

A hail-resistant roof that cannot drain is a failed roof. Front Range thunderstorms deliver cloudburst rainfall - intense, short-duration downpours that overwhelm undersized gutters and scuppers - so the drainage design uses the local intensity-duration data, not a generic rule. Steep-slope roofs get gutters and downspouts sized for the cloudburst with leaf and hail-ice management; low-slope roofs get primary drains plus overflow scuppers or drains set slightly higher, so a blocked primary cannot pond the roof to structural overload. The structural engineer checks ponding stability where the code requires it, because water weighs sixty-two pounds per cubic foot and a clogged drain turns a roof into a reservoir. Elevation changes the structural design west of the metro. Foothill sites in Jefferson, Douglas, and Boulder counties carry design snow loads far above Denver's, and the roof framing, decking, and connections are engineered for the code's ground snow load at that elevation and exposure. The re-roof permit path reflects all of this: most Front Range jurisdictions require a permit for roof replacement, the application names the assembly and its impact classification, and the inspector verifies the underlayment, fastening, and flashing details. In the City and County of Denver that permit goes through Denver's building department; in the unincorporated foothills it goes to the county - each AHJ with its own submittal requirements, and each deciding whether the roof design is approved.

What else do project teams ask?

What is a Class 4 impact rating?
Class 4 is the highest impact-resistance classification for asphalt shingles under the standardized steel-ball test, indicating the shingle survived the largest test impacts without fracturing. It is the rating insurers and engineers look for in hail country. The engineer specifies the classification - verified by the product's test listing - rather than relying on a manufacturer's hail claims.
Is metal roofing better for hail?
For impact survival, generally yes. A standing-seam or stone-coated steel roof over solid decking distributes hail energy across the panel and typically dents cosmetically rather than breaching, while asphalt shingles can fracture and tile can crack. Metal costs more upfront, but in Front Range hail exposure its longer service life and insurer recognition often close the gap. The structure must still be checked, and fastening must meet the wind pressures.
Does hail design change with elevation along the Front Range?
The hail exposure is severe across the whole Front Range, but elevation changes the companion loads: foothill sites add significant snow loads to the structural design, and higher elevations see more freeze-thaw cycling that punishes marginal flashing and drainage details. The engineer designs the roof assembly for hail everywhere and adds the elevation-driven snow, wind-exposure, and drainage adjustments per site.
Do re-roof permits differ between Denver and the foothill counties?
Yes. The City and County of Denver issues re-roof permits through its building department with the city's submittal requirements, while unincorporated Jefferson, Douglas, Boulder, and Arapahoe county sites permit through their respective county building divisions. Requirements - assembly documentation, impact classification, underlayment and fastening details, inspection hold points - vary by AHJ, so the contractor confirms the jurisdiction before filing.
