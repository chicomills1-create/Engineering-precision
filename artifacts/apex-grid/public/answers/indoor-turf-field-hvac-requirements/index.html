What Are the HVAC Requirements for an Indoor Turf Field?

An indoor turf field is one of the most demanding HVAC loads in sports design: a 200-foot clear-span volume, hundreds of athletes at full exertion, heat stratifying at the ceiling, and synthetic turf reradiating warmth back at the players. ASHRAE 62.1 sets the ventilation floor, but the real design work is in distribution, zoning, and turndown.

The concise answer

Ventilate to ASHRAE 62.1 assembly-occupancy rates as the minimum, then design for the building's actual behavior: destratify the high-bay volume so heat doesn't pool at the ceiling while the turf sits cold, zone for tournament-weekend versus empty-weekday occupancy (a 100-to-1 swing is normal), and use demand-controlled ventilation keyed to CO2 and the event schedule. Exhaust high, supply low — and never let the system run flat-out around the clock.

What makes turf buildings different

Synthetic turf absorbs solar gain through any daylighting and reradiates it; the infill layer adds thermal mass that releases heat slowly. The result is a playing surface that can run several degrees warmer than the air at thermostat height. Good design answers with air movement — gentle, uniform distribution across the field — plus envelope insulation that keeps the big box from becoming a radiant oven in summer or a condensation problem in shoulder seasons.

Zoning and controls strategy

The winning move is occupancy-based zoning: full-field tournament mode, half-field practice mode, and unoccupied setback, each with its own ventilation and temperature strategy. Variable-speed air handling, heat-recovery ventilation, and CO2-based demand control let the building's energy use track its actual occupancy — the difference between a fieldhouse the owner can afford to operate and one that becomes a line-item crisis.

Frequently asked questions

How many air changes does an indoor turf building need?
Code sets ventilation by occupancy (cfm per person plus per square foot per ASHRAE 62.1), not a fixed air-change number. In practice, tournament-mode fieldhouses run high outdoor-air rates with demand-controlled ventilation that ramps with actual occupancy.
Does synthetic turf affect indoor air quality?
It can: turf and infill materials have thermal and off-gassing characteristics that good ventilation design addresses with higher air-change rates during peak use and high-level exhaust. Material selection and ventilation are designed together.
Why is my fieldhouse hot at the ceiling and cold at the turf?
Stratification — warm air rising and pooling above the occupied zone. The fix is destratification through HVAC design (supply/return placement) or supplemental high-bay fans, not simply a bigger heater.
Should a fieldhouse have air conditioning?
In most U.S. climates, yes for tournament-grade facilities: summer internal gains from occupants, lighting, and solar load through daylighting overwhelm ventilation-only cooling. The system should be zoned so cooling tracks occupancy.
