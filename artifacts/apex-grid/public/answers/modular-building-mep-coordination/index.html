How Is MEP Coordinated in Modular Building Construction?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

MEP in modular construction is designed for factory rough-in with precise module-boundary connection points, then stitched together on site; coordination drawings locate every interconnection, and commissioning is split between witnessed factory testing and full field verification of the joints.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Designing for the factory floor

I design modular MEP so the factory can build it efficiently and the site crew can finish it quickly — those are different optimizations and both have to be satisfied. In the factory, work happens with the module open: I route mains along the module's long axis, keep branch takeoffs simple, and mount equipment where a forklift or crane can set it before the module closes. Every system is designed to be testable in the module: piping gets test caps at the boundaries, ductwork gets blank-offs, and electrical gets landing points for temporary test power. The drawings carry factory-specific information that site-built drawings never need: module designations on every sheet, the datum each dimension is measured from, shipping restraints for equipment, and weather protection for open ends during transport. I also design for the lift — equipment mounted high in a module shifts the center of gravity, so I coordinate rigging points with the structural engineer and confirm that no MEP component interferes with the crane picks.

Module-to-module interconnections

The interconnection plan is the document the whole project lives or dies by. I show every duct joint, pipe coupling, conduit continuation, and control wire landing at the module boundaries, each with a tag that matches a schedule listing the connection type, size, access requirement, and test procedure. Chases are stacked vertically through the modules so risers run straight, and I hold a clear working zone at each boundary — typically a removable ceiling panel or access door positioned exactly over the joint. Connection types are chosen for field reality. Grooved pipe couplings and no-hub fittings tolerate the small misalignments that field setting produces; gasketed flanged duct joints with draw bands go together fast in tight chases; electrical connections use terminal blocks or plug connectors in accessible junction boxes rather than splices buried in walls. For controls, I home-run the low-voltage wiring to a panel in each module and land the inter-module trunk at a single labeled terminal strip per boundary, so the controls contractor can ring out the building one boundary at a time.

Split commissioning checklist

Commissioning a modular building means proving the factory work and the field joints separately, then proving the integrated building. I write the split into the commissioning specification so nothing falls between the factory and the site. • Factory: witnessed pressure tests on all piping, duct leakage tests on assembled runs, insulation verification before close-up, and electrical continuity and megger testing — with reports shipped with each module • Receiving: verify certification labels, confirm no transit damage to MEP components, and check that every interconnection stub matches the coordination drawings before modules are set • Field joints: re-test every site-made pipe joint, verify duct joint sealing, torque-check electrical terminations, and photograph concealed fire-stopping before close-up • Integrated building: full-system startup, functional performance testing of HVAC sequences, domestic water balancing, and whole-building air leakage testing to prove the joints perform as designed

What else do project teams ask?

What happens if module connections do not line up on site?
That is the failure mode the whole coordination effort exists to prevent, and when it happens it is expensive — field rework on a set module means opening finished walls and ceilings. Prevention is dimensional discipline: I dimension every stub and connection point from a single module datum, require the factory to verify critical dimensions before shipping, and specify connection hardware with realistic tolerance (slip couplings, flexible connectors, oversized sleeves). When a mismatch still occurs, the fix is designed by the engineer of record — never improvised — because the connection still has to meet the code for the system it serves.
Who inspects MEP work done inside the factory?
The factory's certification program does. Modular factories operate under state-approved quality programs with third-party inspection agencies that witness the concealed work before modules are closed up — analogous to a rough-in inspection, but performed under the factory's certification rather than the site building department's. The site inspector then verifies the label, the setting, the interconnections, and the site-built work. I make sure the project specifications name the certification program and require the inspection records to be delivered with the modules.
Can modular buildings achieve the same energy performance as site-built?
They can exceed it. Factory assembly gives tighter control over air sealing, insulation continuity, and duct sealing than field work typically achieves — the building is assembled in a dry, well-lit shop by crews doing the same detail all day. The risk points are the module-to-module joints, which is exactly where I focus the air-barrier and insulation detailing. With continuous air barriers designed across the joints and verified with whole-building testing, a modular building can hit aggressive energy targets reliably.
How do you handle fire-stopping between modules?
As a designed joint, not as generic caulk. Every penetration through a module boundary — and the gap between modules themselves — gets a listed fire-stop system selected for the actual joint width, movement, and rating required. I detail the annular space around penetrating items with the clearances the listed system needs, because a fire-stop detail that cannot be installed as drawn is worthless. Rated corridors and shafts that cross module lines get special attention in the coordination model, and I require field verification photos of the concealed joints before close-up.
