Who Seals Delegated Design on an Architect's Project?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Each delegated component is sealed by the PE in responsible charge of that component's design — usually the specialty subcontractor's engineer. The architect must specify the delegation precisely, require sealed submittals from state-licensed PEs, and have the EOR review delegated designs for conformance.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Writing delegation the AHJ will accept

Plan reviewers see delegated design every day, and they know what a complete delegation looks like: the permit drawings identify each delegated component, state the design criteria it must meet, and note that sealed submittals will follow (either with the permit set or as deferred submittals per the AHJ's policy). What gets bounced is the set that gestures at delegation without defining it — a general note saying "trusses by others" with no criteria, no reactions, and no indication of who engineers them. I write the delegation notes as part of the EOR's scope: explicit component lists, explicit criteria, explicit deliverables. The deferred-submittal question has to be settled with the AHJ before the permit strategy is promised. Most jurisdictions accept deferred delegated submittals, but the list of what qualifies varies — and the permit drawings must call out the deferred items. I confirm this in the pre-submittal phase and document the AHJ's position, because the architect's permit schedule depends on it. A delegation strategy the AHJ has not agreed to is not a strategy.

The EOR-to-delegated-engineer handoff

This handoff is the most under-managed interface on architect-led projects. The EOR's design makes assumptions — truss bearing conditions, curtain wall anchor loads, equipment weights and electrical demands — and the delegated engineer needs those assumptions as design criteria, not as archaeology. I deliver them as a written criteria package: loads, reactions, deflection limits, interface details, and the code edition the component must meet. The delegated engineer designs to that package, seals the submittal, and returns the actual reactions and connection demands, which the EOR then verifies against the primary structure. When this handoff is managed, delegated design is efficient: specialists engineer their components better than a generalist EOR could. When it is not managed, the project gets the worst of both worlds — a component designed in isolation and a primary structure that never accounted for it. Architects sometimes assume the two engineers will "work it out"; in my experience they work it out only if the architect (or the GC) runs the handoff as a scheduled deliverable with dates. Put the criteria package and the conformance review on the project schedule like any other milestone.

Delegated-design checklist for architects

No delegated component should reach the field without passing through this list. • Specs name each delegated component, its design criteria, and the sealed-submittal deliverable from a state-licensed PE • EOR issues a written criteria package (loads, reactions, limits, code edition) to each delegated engineer • Seal map tracks every delegated component: responsible PE, license number, state, submittal status • EOR reviews each delegated submittal for conformance and documents the review in writing • AHJ's deferred-submittal policy confirmed before the permit schedule is set — delegated items called out on the permit drawings

What else do project teams ask?

What belongs in the specs for a delegated structural component?
The component description, the applicable code and design criteria (loads, deflection limits, performance requirements), the required deliverable — sealed shop drawings and supporting calculations from a PE licensed in the project state — the submittal schedule, and the requirement that the delegated design conform to the EOR's stated design criteria. I also specify that the delegated engineer must provide the reactions and connection demands their component imposes on the primary structure, because the EOR cannot verify the main frame without them. Vague specs produce vague bids; precise specs produce sealed submittals that actually coordinate.
Does the EOR review delegated submittals, or just receive them?
The EOR reviews them for conformance with the design intent and the stated criteria — this is a defined professional role, not a courtesy glance. The EOR does not redesign the component and does not take responsible charge of it (the delegated PE does that), but the EOR confirms the component fits the building: reactions match the primary structure's design, penetrations and supports coordinate, and the performance criteria are met. I document these conformance reviews in writing. Architects should carry an EOR scope line for delegated-submittal review — it is real engineering time, and projects that skip it discover the misfit in the field.
How is MEP design-build delegation sealed?
The mechanical or electrical subcontractor's licensed PE seals the delegated MEP design — the drawings, the load calculations, the equipment selections — after performing the design under responsible charge. The architect's documents carry the performance criteria (capacities, efficiencies, code compliance targets), and the project's MEP EOR reviews the delegated submittal for conformance with those criteria. In California, Title 24 compliance documentation has to reconcile with the delegated design, which is a coordination item I flag early. The seal on a delegated MEP package is only as good as the performance spec it was designed to — weak criteria produce compliant-but-wrong systems.
What happens when a delegated component's engineer is in another state?
The component cannot be sealed for your permit until a PE licensed in the project state takes responsible charge of it. National fabricators and specialty subs often have engineers licensed in many states, but "many" is not "yours" — I have seen curtain wall packages stall because the fabricator's engineer was licensed everywhere except the project state. The architect's spec should require the delegated PE's license state upfront, and the GC's submittal tracking should verify it at award. Fixing it late means the component gets redesigned or re-reviewed under a newly engaged local PE, on the project's critical path.
