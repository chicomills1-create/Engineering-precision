What Are Municipal Bridge Inspection Requirements?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

Every public bridge in America over 20 feet long falls under the National Bridge Inspection Standards (NBIS): routine inspections at least every 24 months by qualified inspectors, with more frequent intervals for bridges in poor condition or with fracture-critical members. Inspections produce NBI condition ratings (0-9 scale) for deck, superstructure, substructure, and culvert, plus load ratings that determine whether weight posting is required. Municipalities own roughly half the nation's bridges — and compliance is the owner's legal responsibility.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The NBIS framework municipalities live under

The National Bridge Inspection Standards, administered by the Federal Highway Administration and implemented through each state DOT, set the rules for every public highway bridge longer than 20 feet. Routine inspections happen on a maximum 24-month cycle; bridges rated in poor condition, with fracture-critical members, or with underwater elements needing attention get inspected more often — 12-month intervals are common for the worst cases. Underwater inspections run on 60-month cycles (more often where scour is a concern), and fracture-critical members get hands-on, arm's-length inspection on their own schedule.

Each inspection produces National Bridge Inventory condition ratings on the 0-9 scale for deck, superstructure, substructure, and channel/channel protection, plus an appraisal of structural adequacy and serviceability. These ratings feed the state's bridge management system and determine federal funding eligibility — a bridge rated 4 or below (poor) on any major component enters the replacement-or-rehabilitation conversation. The inspection report isn't paperwork; it's the legal record of the bridge's condition and the basis for every load-posting and closure decision the owner makes.

Load ratings and postings: the math that keeps trucks honest

Separate from condition inspection, every bridge needs a load rating — the engineering analysis (per AASHTO's Manual for Bridge Evaluation) that determines how much live load the structure can safely carry. Ratings are expressed as inventory (the load the bridge can carry indefinitely) and operating (the maximum permissible) levels, in HS truck equivalents or HL-93. When the operating rating falls below legal loads, the bridge must be posted with weight limits — and the municipality is legally responsible for the posting and its enforcement.

This is where small municipalities get surprised: a bridge that looks fine can require posting because the rating analysis — not the visual condition — governs. Deterioration, section loss, unanticipated truck traffic growth, or simply the more refined analysis methods now required can all reduce a rating. We perform load ratings and re-ratings, design the posting signage layouts, and — more usefully — design the rehabilitations (deck overlays, beam strengthening with FRP, bearing replacement) that remove postings and restore full capacity. A posted bridge is a liability and an economic drag on the routes it serves; the engineering goal is always to un-post it.

Scour: the number-one bridge killer

More bridges fail from scour — floodwaters eroding the streambed around foundations — than from any other cause. NBIS requires scour appraisal for every bridge over water: is the foundation scour-critical, and what's the plan of action? The plan of action for scour-critical bridges includes monitoring during floods, and where analysis shows vulnerability, countermeasures: riprap armoring, guide banks, deepened footings on rehabilitation projects, or in the worst cases, closure during flood events.

Our hydraulic engineers run the scour analysis (HEC-18 methodology), and our structural engineers design the countermeasures. For municipalities, the practical program is: know which of your bridges are scour-critical, have a flood-monitoring protocol with named responsible people, and program countermeasure construction before the flood — not after. Scour failures are the failures that make the news and the courtroom; they're also among the most preventable with disciplined inspection and timely countermeasures.

What municipalities must actually do

The compliance checklist for a municipal bridge owner: maintain an inventory of every qualifying bridge; ensure inspections happen on the required intervals by qualified personnel (most small cities contract this to the state DOT or a consulting firm — which is us, routinely); act on the findings — postings posted, critical findings addressed on the required timelines; maintain the inspection records; and program rehabilitation and replacement through the state's transportation improvement program for funding eligibility.

Critical findings — conditions requiring prompt action — come with response deadlines measured in days and weeks, not budget cycles. A municipality that defers a critical finding is accepting legal exposure it doesn't want. Our role for municipal clients: inspection program management, load ratings, rehabilitation design, scour analysis and countermeasures, and the funding applications (federal-aid bridge programs, state bridge funds) that pay for the work. The bridges are the municipality's; the compliance burden doesn't have to be carried alone.

What else do project teams ask?

How often must a municipal bridge be inspected?
At least every 24 months for routine inspection under NBIS, with 12-month intervals common for bridges in poor condition or with fracture-critical members. Underwater portions are inspected at least every 60 months. The state DOT oversees compliance, but the inspection responsibility belongs to the bridge owner — the municipality.
What does a bridge condition rating of 4 mean?
On the 0-9 NBI scale, 4 is 'poor' — advanced deterioration that affects structural capacity. A bridge rated 4 or below on deck, superstructure, substructure, or culvert is classified as in poor condition, which triggers eligibility for federal replacement/rehabilitation funding and prioritization in the state's program.
Who is responsible if a municipal bridge fails?
The bridge owner — the municipality — bears legal responsibility for inspection, posting, and timely action on deficiencies. That's why documented inspection programs, prompt response to critical findings, and engineered load postings aren't bureaucratic overhead; they're the liability shield.
What is a fracture-critical member?
A steel tension member whose failure would cause partial or full collapse of the bridge — no redundancy. Fracture-critical members require hands-on, arm's-length inspection on a maximum 24-month cycle. Many older steel bridges have them; modern designs avoid the detail.
