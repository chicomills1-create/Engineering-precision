How Are Cleveland Roofs Designed for Lake-Effect Snow?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Cleveland roofs are designed for lake-effect snow using ASCE 7 ground snow loads with added drift, sliding, and unbalanced load cases, continuous load paths into the structure, and drainage that handles rapid spring melt - with the building official approving the final design through the local plan review process.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Designing for Lake-Effect Snow

Lake-effect snow is a local phenomenon with structural consequences. When arctic air crosses Lake Erie's warmer water, it loads up with moisture and unloads it in narrow, intense bands - the Snowbelt east of Cleveland routinely receives double the snowfall of the west side. The building code accounts for this through the ground snow load, and the engineer starts there, then applies the code's exposure, thermal, and importance factors to get the design roof snow load. A heated, occupied building melts snow faster than an unheated warehouse, and an essential facility like a hospital carries a higher importance factor - the same roof geometry can have very different design loads depending on what is underneath it. The load path must be continuous from the roof surface to the foundation. Snow load on the deck transfers to joists or purlins, then to beams and girders, then to columns and walls, and finally to the foundation - every connection in that chain is designed for its share. The engineer pays special attention to the connections, because a beam that is adequate in bending can still fail if its hanger or bearing seat was never checked for the full snow load. On wood-frame buildings this means verifying joist hangers and bearing; on steel it means checking the open-web joists, which have strict limits on unbalanced loading that the engineer must respect.

Ponding, Meltwater, and Older Roofs

Ponding is the slow-motion failure mode of flat roofs. As snow melts, water collects in any low spot; the water's weight deflects the roof further, which deepens the low spot, which collects more water. The engineer breaks this cycle by designing the roof with enough slope and stiffness that ponding cannot progress, and by providing overflow drainage - scuppers or overflow drains set above the primary drain inlet elevation - so a frozen or clogged primary drain does not turn the roof into a bathtub. Parapets make ponding worse by trapping water, which is why overflow scuppers through the parapet are a code-driven detail, not an aesthetic choice. Existing buildings need verification, not assumptions. Many Cleveland commercial roofs were designed decades ago under lighter snow provisions, and each reroofing since may have added another layer of membrane and insulation - dead load that quietly consumes the capacity meant for snow. The engineer's verification starts with the original drawings if they exist, then field-verifies the actual framing size, spacing, and condition, and finally compares the structure's real capacity against today's code loads. Where capacity falls short, the retrofit options range from adding framing members to removing excess roofing layers to posting the roof for a reduced snow load with a monitoring plan - the owner gets an engineered answer, not a guess.

What else do project teams ask?

What is a drift load and why does it matter in Cleveland?
A drift load is the concentrated pile of snow that wind deposits against parapets, rooftop units, and steps between high and low roofs. In lake-effect country these drifts can be several feet deep and far heavier than the uniform snow load, acting on a small area of roof. Engineers calculate drift height and surcharge from the code's provisions and design the framing beneath for that concentrated weight.
How much snow can a typical flat roof hold?
It depends on the building's age, framing, and design code - there is no single number. A roof designed to current code for Cleveland's ground snow load handles the code-prescribed uniform, drift, and unbalanced cases with a safety margin. Older roofs, or roofs carrying extra layers of reroofing, may have less margin. An engineer can calculate the actual capacity from the framing and compare it with today's loads.
Does roof snow work need a permit in Cleveland?
Structural work - reframing, adding members, or changing the roof structure - requires a permit through the City of Cleveland's building department, with plan review of the snow load calculations. Simple snow removal does not. In unincorporated Cuyahoga County the county handles permits with its own submittal process. The authority having jurisdiction makes the final call on what needs review.
When should snow be removed from a roof?
Removal should be considered when accumulation approaches the roof's rated capacity, after major lake-effect events, or when drifting has piled snow deep against parapets and equipment. Warning signs include visible sagging, new cracks in interior finishes, doors sticking, and unusual creaking. Removal itself must be done carefully - piling removed snow in one spot or striking the membrane with tools can cause the damage it is meant to prevent.
