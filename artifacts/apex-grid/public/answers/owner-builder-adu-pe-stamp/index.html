Do Owner-Builders Need a PE Stamp for an ADU in California or Arizona?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Often yes: ADUs that leave prescriptive construction — second stories, hillsides, garage conversions, engineered shear walls — need a PE's review and seal, with California adding seismic detailing and Title 24 reconciliation. Even pre-approved city ADU plans need PE re-review and re-seal when site-adapted.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

California ADU engineering: seismic and Title 24

The California ADU review centers on two things the rest of the country treats lightly. First, seismic: the lateral system gets a full engineering review — shear wall capacities calculated for the site's seismic parameters, holdown selections verified against uplift, drag struts and collectors detailed, and the load path traced continuously from roof to foundation. California plan reviewers read lateral detailing closely on ADUs because small structures with big openings (garage doors, sliding glass) are where lateral design most often fails. I detail ADU lateral systems as carefully as I would a custom home's — the earthquake does not grade on square footage. Second, Title 24: the energy compliance documentation must reflect the sealed ADU design — envelope values, window performance, water heating, and any space conditioning — and the reviewer reconciles the forms against the drawings. ADUs trigger the same Title 24 compliance path as any new dwelling unit, and the most common ADU energy correction I see is documentation modeled on different equipment than the drawings show. I reconcile the compliance model as part of the review, before sealing. Owner-builders sometimes assume the ADU's size exempts it from energy rigor; it does not, and the PE's review is where that gets settled.

Arizona ADU engineering: soils and drainage

Arizona's ADU review is shaped by the desert site, not by seismic demand. The foundation review leads: the Valley's expansive soils mean the PE verifies the foundation design against the geotechnical report's recommendations — pier depths, slab detailing, moisture barriers — with the same seriousness a California engineer brings to shear walls. I see ADU foundations under-designed more often than any other ADU element in Arizona, usually because the owner-builder assumed a conventional slab works everywhere. On expansive clay it does not, and the PE's review is the backstop. Drainage is the second Arizona emphasis: the ADU's grading must handle monsoon hydrology without sending water at the main house or the neighbor, and Maricopa County and the Valley cities review grading plans with that storm in mind. The review verifies the grading concept, the finished-floor elevations relative to floodplain and drainage paths, and the utility connections' capacity — sewer in particular, where the ADU adds dwelling-unit load to a lateral sized for one house. Arizona's ADU engineering is less about surviving earthquakes and more about surviving the site: soils, water, and the infrastructure the ADU plugs into.

Owner-builder ADU checklist

ADUs are small projects with real engineering — run the checklist and the permit stays small too. • Confirm with the AHJ whether your ADU design stays prescriptive or needs sealed engineering — assume the latter until proven otherwise • Commission the soils investigation early; foundation design waits on it in both California and Arizona • For garage conversions, budget a full lateral-system engineering effort — the structure is being redesigned, not remodeled • Using pre-approved city plans? Plan for PE review and re-seal of the site adaptation — soils, setbacks, utilities, energy • Reconcile Title 24 (CA) energy documentation against the sealed design before submittal, not after the correction

What else do project teams ask?

Does California's ADU streamlining law eliminate the engineering review?
No — the streamlining laws limit discretionary review, cap fees, and impose shot clocks on approvals, but they do not waive building code or engineering requirements. The ADU still has to be structurally sound under the code, which means engineered design wherever the prescriptive path does not cover it, sealed by a licensed PE. What the laws do change is the timeline pressure: with the city on a 60-day clock, the engineering review-and-seal has to be efficient, because a permit held up on engineering still counts against everyone's schedule. I run ADU reviews on a fast cadence precisely because the statutory clock does not pause for a slow engineer.
What is the hardest engineering part of a garage-conversion ADU?
The lateral system. A garage was designed (if it was designed at all) as a utility structure — wide openings, minimal shear walls, often no holdowns — and converting it to a dwelling means the PE must engineer a lateral system that was never there: new shear walls, holdowns, drag struts, and connections that deliver the loads to the foundation. The review also verifies the existing slab and foundation can carry dwelling loads, which they were never asked to do. I treat garage conversions as structural redesigns wearing a remodel's clothes — the review effort surprises owner-builders who expected a light touch, but the seal has to certify a dwelling's structure, not a garage's.
Do I need a soils report for an ADU?
Many California and Arizona jurisdictions require one for new detached ADUs, and even where it is not strictly required, the PE needs to know what the foundation bears on. In the Valley's expansive soils or on any hillside, I will not seal a foundation design without geotechnical data — the failure modes are too well documented. Where the AHJ allows presumptive designs, the PE can sometimes work from conservative presumptive soil values, but that is the PE's professional call under responsible charge, not the owner's cost-saving preference. Budget the soils investigation into the ADU from the start; discovering the requirement at plan check adds weeks.
Can I use my city's pre-approved ADU plans without an engineer?
You can use them as drawn for the conditions they were approved for — but most sites require adaptation (grading, setbacks, utility connections, foundation adjustments for actual soils), and adapted plans need a licensed PE's review and re-seal. The pre-approval certifies the prototype; your site is not the prototype. I review the adaptation: foundations against your soils report, lateral detailing against your seismic parameters, energy compliance against the as-adapted design — then re-seal the site-specific set. Cities are explicit about this boundary, and plan check enforces it. The pre-approved plan saves design time; the PE's review makes it your permit.
