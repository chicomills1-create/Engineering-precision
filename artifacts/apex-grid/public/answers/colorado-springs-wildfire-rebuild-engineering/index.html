How Does Wildfire Rebuild Engineering Work in Colorado Springs?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Wildfire rebuild engineering in Colorado Springs starts by assessing what survived - foundations, slabs, and retaining walls are tested for heat damage - then designs a new fire-hardened structure with ember-resistant vents, noncombustible siding and decking, tempered glazing, and defensible-space grading, permitted through the city's wildland-urban interface requirements.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What the Fire Left Behind

The assessment phase treats every surviving element as suspect until tested. Concrete foundations and slabs are evaluated for heat effects: petrographic examination or core strength testing reveals how deep the damage goes, because surface appearance is unreliable - concrete can look intact while having lost a large fraction of its strength. Reinforcement bond is checked where exposure was severe. The engineer documents the testing, the results, and the keep-or-remove recommendation for each element, because the building official will ask for exactly that record before approving foundation reuse. Site infrastructure gets the same treatment. Retaining walls are checked for heat and lateral damage, underground utilities are inspected or camera-checked where fire exposure is suspected, and grading is surveyed - wildfire can alter drainage patterns by destroying vegetation and baking soils hydrophobic. Hazardous debris and ash are handled under the applicable environmental rules before reconstruction starts. Only when the site is characterized does the design move forward; building a hardened home on a compromised foundation or an unstable slope wastes the entire investment.

Designing the Fire-Hardened Replacement

The hardened envelope is a system, not a material. Ember-resistant vents with fine screening at the eaves, foundation, and gables deny the ignition source that destroys most wildfire homes. Boxed eaves eliminate the open-rafter ember traps; noncombustible or ignition-resistant siding, decking, and fencing break the fuel path to the walls; Class A roofing and tempered glazing complete the shell. Each detail is drawn and specified - vent models, eave construction, deck material approvals - because WUI plan review checks the details, not the concept. Behind the envelope, the structure is designed to current codes with the lessons of the local fires embedded: connections and diaphragms per the structural provisions, defensible-space grading that doubles as drainage design, and access and water supply coordinated with the fire district's requirements. Landscaping plans specify plant types and placement by defensible-space zone, keeping flammable vegetation away from the building while stabilizing slopes. The result is a home engineered for the site's actual fire exposure, documented to satisfy both the building official and the owner's insurer.

What else do project teams ask?

Can I reuse my foundation after a wildfire?
Sometimes - but only with testing. Concrete exposed to wildfire heat can lose strength and reinforcement bond beneath a normal-looking surface, so the engineer cores or otherwise tests the foundation and documents the results. If testing shows the concrete is sound, reuse is designed and permitted; if not, replacement is the only responsible answer. The building official reviews that documentation closely.
What ignites most homes in a wildfire?
Embers, not direct flame contact. Wind-blown embers enter through vents, lodge in open eaves and gutters, and ignite decks, fences, and siding. That is why the rebuild design concentrates on ember-resistant vents, boxed eaves, noncombustible materials near the ground, and breaking fuel paths - denying embers entry and fuel matters more than any single wall material.
What is defensible space, and who designs it?
Defensible space is the landscaped buffer around a building, zoned from noncombustible ground cover at the walls out through reduced fuels to thinned native vegetation. The civil engineer grades it for drainage and slope stability while the landscape design selects plant types and placement by zone. It is part of the permit package in WUI areas, not an afterthought.
Do Colorado Springs and El Paso County have different wildfire building rules?
The construction principles are the same, but the review path differs. Inside Colorado Springs, WUI-area projects go through the city's review with its wildland-urban interface requirements and checklists. In unincorporated El Paso County - including areas like Black Forest - the county is the AHJ with its own forms, reviewers, and timelines. The engineer confirms jurisdiction and pulls that AHJ's WUI checklist before designing. The AHJ decides approvals.
