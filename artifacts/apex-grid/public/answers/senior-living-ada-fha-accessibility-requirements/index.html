What accessibility rules apply to senior living facilities?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Senior living accessibility has two federal layers: the ADA governs public and common areas as public accommodations, and the Fair Housing Act requires accessible design in covered multifamily dwellings — adaptable units, accessible routes, and usable kitchens and baths. State codes can exceed both. And the residents' reality — wheelchairs, walkers, low vision — argues for exceeding minimums where it matters.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The two federal layers

ADA Title III treats the facility's public areas like any place of public accommodation: accessible entrances, routes, restrooms, counters, and parking. The Fair Housing Act reaches inside the units: in covered buildings, ground-floor units (and all units in elevator buildings) need accessible entrances on an accessible route, usable doors and hallways, accessible environmental controls, reinforced bathroom walls for grab bars, and usable kitchens and bathrooms. Nursing facilities, as health-care occupancies, effectively design all resident areas to institutional accessibility standards.

Where senior living exceeds minimums

Minimum code produces a building a wheelchair can enter; good senior design produces one residents actually enjoy. Corridors wide enough for two wheelchairs to pass, handrails both sides, lever hardware throughout, zero-threshold showers, contrasting stair nosings, acoustic control for hearing aids, and generous turning space in every unit bathroom — none of this is strictly required everywhere, and all of it shows up in occupancy and satisfaction.

Common compliance failures

Slopes on 'accessible' routes that exceed maximums, cross-slopes at curb ramps, maneuvering clearances eaten by door swings, grab-bar blocking omitted behind finished walls, and unit bathrooms that meet the letter of the FHA while failing real wheelchair use. The fix is detailing accessibility in the construction documents — not leaving it to field interpretation — and verifying in the field before finishes close.

What else do project teams ask?

Does the ADA apply inside resident units?
The ADA's public-accommodation provisions generally reach common areas rather than private dwelling units; the Fair Housing Act's design requirements cover the units in multifamily buildings. Nursing resident rooms follow institutional accessibility expectations.
Are all units required to be fully accessible?
No — the FHA requires adaptable design in covered units, with a smaller number typically built fully accessible. State rules and the facility's license category can raise the bar; market positioning often raises it further.
What about existing buildings converting to senior living?
Change-of-occupancy triggers accessibility upgrades along the path of travel and in altered areas, with the scope set by the code's alteration provisions and the AHJ. Budget for it early — it is never as cheap as hoped.
