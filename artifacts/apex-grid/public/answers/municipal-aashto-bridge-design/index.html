What AASHTO Standards Govern Municipal Bridge Design?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

Municipal bridges are designed to the AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications — the national code for highway bridges, required for any bridge using federal funds and adopted by every state DOT. It governs structural loading (HL-93), load-and-resistance-factor design methodology, seismic design, hydraulic scour analysis, and durability detailing. Municipalities don't write their own bridge code; they invoke AASHTO through their state DOT's bridge design manual.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

LRFD: the design philosophy

Load and Resistance Factor Design — the AASHTO LRFD Specifications' core — designs bridges so factored loads don't exceed factored resistances, with the factors calibrated to achieve uniform reliability across load types and materials. Dead loads get lower factors (they're predictable); live loads, wind, and seismic get higher ones (they're not). It's a probabilistic framework: the code targets consistent safety margins whether the bridge is a 30-foot prestressed slab or a 300-foot steel girder — which is why a municipality's small bridge gets the same rigorous methodology as an interstate structure.

The design vehicle is HL-93: the design truck (or tandem) plus the design lane load, placed to maximize effects, with dynamic load allowance (impact) and multiple-presence factors for loaded lanes. Pedestrian loads, vehicular collision on barriers and piers, wind, temperature, stream pressure and buoyancy, vessel collision where navigable — the LRFD load combinations cover them all. For the municipal engineer, the practical point is that 'a small bridge' is never a small design: the code's full load framework applies at every span, and shortcuts in loading assumptions are where under-designed bridges come from.

What the code demands beyond the girders

Seismic design: AASHTO's seismic provisions (and each state's seismic design criteria) require bridges in seismic zones to be designed for the design earthquake — with detailing (confinement, seat widths, restrainers) that prevents collapse even where full elastic design would be uneconomical. A municipal bridge in California, the Pacific Northwest, the New Madrid zone, or the Intermountain West gets seismic design as a matter of code, not judgment.

Hydraulics and scour: the code requires hydraulic analysis for waterway opening sizing and scour analysis at the design flood (and scour-stability checks at the superflood). Foundations must be designed so scour doesn't undermine them — deep foundations, riprap and countermeasures, or monitored plans of action for scour-critical existing bridges. Geotechnical: every bridge needs a foundation investigation and geotechnical recommendations — the number of municipal bridge problems traceable to skimped geotech would embarrass the profession. Durability: concrete cover, corrosion protection, deck waterproofing, joint detailing — the provisions that determine whether the bridge lasts 75 years or 30.

  • HL-93 loading with dynamic allowance and load combinations
  • Seismic design and collapse-prevention detailing
  • Hydraulic and scour analysis (design flood + superflood checks)
  • Geotechnical investigation for every structure
  • Durability detailing: cover, corrosion protection, joints

How municipalities actually get bridges designed

The delivery path for a municipal bridge: the state DOT's Local Public Agency program (federal-aid projects administered through the state) or state/local funding with the state DOT's bridge design manual as the governing supplement to AASHTO. Either way, the design goes through the DOT's review — hydraulics report, scour analysis, structural calculations, plans — and the municipality's engineer needs to know the DOT's submittal requirements cold, because review cycles are where bridge schedules go to die.

Our structural engineers design municipal bridges — replacements, rehabilitations, culverts, pedestrian structures — inside the AASHTO LRFD framework and the applicable state DOT manual, with in-house hydraulics for the scour analysis and geotechnical coordination for foundations. Rehabilitation design (deck replacement, superstructure replacement on existing substructure, widening, seismic retrofit, FRP strengthening) is the larger share of municipal bridge work nationally — most cities need their bridges fixed more than they need new ones. And every design carries the load rating analysis, because the rating is the document that determines whether the bridge posts, restricts, or serves at full capacity.

What else do project teams ask?

Do small municipal bridges really need full AASHTO design?
Yes. AASHTO LRFD applies to all highway bridges regardless of size, and any bridge using federal funds must comply. States don't maintain a separate 'small bridge' code — the same specifications govern a 25-foot town bridge and an interstate interchange, scaled to the structure.
What is HL-93 loading?
The AASHTO design live load: a design truck (HS-20 equivalent) or design tandem, whichever governs, plus a uniform lane load of 0.64 kips per linear foot — placed on the bridge to produce maximum force effects, with impact and multiple-lane factors. Every new bridge is designed for HL-93.
Who approves a municipal bridge design?
The state DOT — through its Local Public Agency/federal-aid program or its bridge office review — approves designs for bridges on public roads, applying the AASHTO LRFD Specifications plus the state's bridge design manual. The municipality owns the bridge; the DOT governs its design standards.
What's the difference between bridge design and load rating?
Design (AASHTO LRFD) sizes a new bridge for HL-93 and all applicable loads. Load rating (AASHTO Manual for Bridge Evaluation) analyzes an existing bridge's safe carrying capacity, determining whether posting or restriction is needed. New designs get rated too — the rating is the legal basis for the bridge's posted capacity.
