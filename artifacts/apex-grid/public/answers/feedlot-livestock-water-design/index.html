How Are Feedlot Water Systems Designed for Large Herds?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Feedlot water systems are built around peak demand: wells and one to two days of tank storage, looped distribution mains sized for the farthest pen, freeze-proof waterers on properly drained pads, tested water quality, and standby power behind the pumps.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Demand, Wells, and Storage Sizing

The engineer builds the demand curve before sizing anything: head count by pen, per-head consumption at summer peak, dust-control draw, and a growth factor for the operation's expansion plans. Wells are then tested for sustained yield — not just the driller's air-lift number — and the engineer compares firm well capacity against the peak day with one well assumed out of service. Where groundwater is limited or poor quality, the design may blend sources or add treatment, and the engineer documents the water right or permit the withdrawal requires. Storage bridges the gap between well yield and instantaneous demand. Tanks are sized for one to two days of peak use, elevated or pressurized to hold system pressure when pumps cycle, and the engineer provides the level controls and alarms that keep tanks full without overflowing. Tank placement considers both hydraulics and biosecurity: high enough to feed the loop by gravity during an outage, and sited where service trucks can reach without crossing clean cattle areas.

Distribution Piping and Freeze Protection

The distribution layout is a looped network with isolation valves at every branch, drawn so any single break or repair takes down the smallest possible area. The engineer sizes mains for peak simultaneous flow — every waterer drawing at once on a hot afternoon — and checks residual pressure at the farthest, highest waterer. Thrust blocking or restrained joints go at every bend and tee, because a blown-apart main under a pen is a flooding and mud emergency. Freeze protection is detailed for the coldest expected conditions, not the average winter. Mains are buried below the local frost line with bedding and warning tape; risers to waterers are insulated or heat-traced; and the waterers themselves are the freeze-resistant type with insulated bodies and protected valves. The engineer also plans for the shoulder seasons, when a sudden freeze catches the system with summer settings — drain-down points and valve positions are marked on the as-builts so the crew can winterize quickly.

Feedlot Livestock Water Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the water system goes to bid: • Peak-day demand calculated from head count, climate, ration, and dust-control draw • Well yield tested for sustained output; water rights or withdrawal permits secured • One to two days of peak-demand storage with level controls and alarms • Looped distribution mains with isolation valves; no pen on a single dead-end run • Pipe sized for peak simultaneous flow with residual pressure checked at the farthest waterer • Mains buried below frost line; risers insulated or heat-traced • Freeze-proof waterers on drained concrete pads, sited on shared fence lines where practical • Source water tested for TDS, sulfates, and nitrates; treatment designed where needed • Standby power behind well and booster pumps with automatic transfer

What else do project teams ask?

How much water does a feedlot steer drink per day?
Roughly 8 to 12 gallons in mild weather and 15 to 20 gallons or more in summer heat, with high-grain finishing rations pushing intake higher. The engineer uses the peak number for sizing — the system must satisfy every animal on the hottest day of the year, not the average day — and adds dust-control demand on top.
How do feedlot waterers avoid freezing?
With a combination of burial depth, insulation, and waterer design. Mains run below the frost line, risers are insulated or heat-traced, and the waterers are freeze-resistant units with insulated bodies and protected valves. The engineer designs for the coldest expected snap and marks winterization points on the as-builts for fast crew response.
What water quality problems affect cattle performance?
High total dissolved solids, sulfates, and nitrates are the usual culprits — they depress intake, cause digestive upset, and drag down gain. The engineer tests the source before design and specifies treatment or an alternate source where chemistry is off, since bad water quietly taxes every animal on the yard.
What happens when the well pump loses power?
That is what storage and standby power are for. Tank storage carries the operation for a day or two, and the well and booster pumps sit on the standby generator's critical-load list with automatic transfer. The engineer sizes the generator for pump starting current — motors draw several times their running amps at startup — not just the running load.
