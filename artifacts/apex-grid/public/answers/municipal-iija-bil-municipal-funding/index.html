How Does IIJA/BIL Funding Work for Municipal Infrastructure?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, 2021) committed $1.2 trillion to infrastructure, with historic water investments flowing mainly through expanded State Revolving Funds: tens of billions in supplemental SRF capitalization — roughly half required as principal forgiveness/grants to disadvantaged communities — plus dedicated funding for lead service line replacement and emerging contaminants (PFAS). Municipalities capture it through their state's SRF Intended Use Plan, competitive EPA grant programs, and transportation formula funds for bridges and roads.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Where the water money flows

The BIL's water headline is the SRF expansion: roughly $50 billion for water infrastructure, the largest federal water investment in history. The supplemental capitalization flows through the existing Clean Water and Drinking Water SRFs — same state programs, massively larger checkbooks — with the requirement that about half of the supplemental funds go as principal forgiveness or grants, targeted to disadvantaged communities. Dedicated carve-outs: $15 billion for lead service line replacement through the DWSRF, and billions for emerging contaminants (PFAS and others), with the EC funding effectively 100% principal forgiveness.

What this means practically: a small disadvantaged community's water project can now be financed at 50-100% forgiveness — the federal government paying for infrastructure that the community's rate base never could. A mid-size city's treatment upgrade gets a larger, cheaper SRF loan than the base program ever offered. But the money moves through the state IUP process — the same priority ranking, the same engineering-report prerequisite, the same annual cycle. The IIJA didn't create a new application; it supercharged the existing one. Municipalities positioned with engineered, ranked projects captured the money; municipalities without projects watched it go to their neighbors.

Beyond the SRF: competitive grants and transportation

The BIL's competitive grant programs reward the prepared: EPA's Water Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act (expanded funding), sewer overflow and stormwater reuse municipal grants, drinking water system resilience grants, and the BRIC/FEMA hazard-mitigation programs for flood-resilient infrastructure. Competitive grants are scored — project merit, readiness, disadvantaged-community benefit, climate resilience — and the applications demand engineered project scopes with real cost estimates. A concept sketch doesn't score; a 30% design with a benefit-cost analysis does.

Transportation is the BIL's other municipal pillar: the Bridge Formula Program (dedicated funding for bridge replacement and rehabilitation, including off-system municipal bridges), PROTECT grants for resilience, Safe Streets for All for safety, and the core federal-aid highway program's increased authorizations flowing through state DOTs to local agencies. Municipal bridge programs — inspection, load rating, rehabilitation design — are the engineering that converts bridge-formula dollars into projects. Every BIL dollar, in every program, ultimately requires engineering to become a project.

  • SRF supplemental: ~$50B water, ~half as forgiveness/grants
  • Lead service lines: $15B dedicated DWSRF funding
  • Emerging contaminants (PFAS): billions, effectively 100% forgiveness
  • Competitive grants scored on readiness and merit
  • Bridge Formula Program for off-system municipal bridges

Build America, Buy America and the compliance layer

BIL funding carries expanded domestic-content requirements: Build America, Buy America (BABA) applies to federal infrastructure assistance, requiring iron, steel, manufactured products, and construction materials to be produced in the United States. For water projects, this layers over the existing American Iron and Steel (AIS) requirements — and the waiver process, product availability, and documentation are real project considerations, not boilerplate. Material selection during design has to account for BABA compliance; discovering a specified product isn't compliant during construction is a change order and a schedule hit.

The full compliance stack on BIL-funded work: Davis-Bacon prevailing wages, AIS/BABA domestic content, environmental review (state processes for SRF, NEPA for WIFIA and competitive grants), disadvantaged-business-enterprise goals, and single-audit requirements. Each is manageable; together they're a program-management discipline. Our engineers design BABA-compliant from the start — domestic product verification during specification, not after bid — and our construction administration enforces the documentation (de minimis waivers, product certifications) that keeps disbursements flowing. The funding is historic; the compliance is non-negotiable.

The capture strategy for municipalities

The municipalities capturing BIL money share a playbook: (1) A capital improvement plan with engineered project scopes — not wish lists. (2) Projects entered in the SRF IUP with complete engineering reports, ranked and ready. (3) Disadvantaged-community documentation where applicable, unlocking forgiveness. (4) Competitive grant applications built on real preliminary engineering. (5) An engineer who sequences deliverables to funding calendars — because every program has a cycle, and missing the cycle means waiting a year.

The window matters: BIL supplemental SRF appropriations run through their authorization horizon, and the states are programming the money now. The municipalities that will have captured the most BIL funding a decade from now are the ones whose engineers had projects ready when the money moved. If your capital plan is still a spreadsheet of ideas, the honest advice is: start the engineering now. The funding rewards readiness, and readiness is engineered. Our municipal practice exists substantially to create that readiness — the plans, the reports, the designs, the applications — at the pace the funding windows require.

What else do project teams ask?

How does a city actually get IIJA/BIL water funding?
Overwhelmingly through the state SRF programs: get the project engineered, complete the SRF engineering report, apply for the Intended Use Plan priority list, and close the loan (often with substantial principal forgiveness). Competitive EPA grants are the second path, requiring scored applications built on preliminary engineering.
Is BIL water funding a grant or a loan?
Mostly loans — but with historic grant-like elements: roughly half the supplemental SRF capitalization must go as principal forgiveness or grants to disadvantaged communities, and the emerging-contaminant and lead-line carve-outs carry heavy forgiveness. A disadvantaged community can see 50-100% of project cost forgiven.
What is Build America, Buy America (BABA)?
The BIL's domestic-content requirement for federally assisted infrastructure: iron, steel, manufactured products, and construction materials must be produced in the U.S. It applies alongside the existing American Iron and Steel rules for water projects, with waiver processes for unavailable products. Compliance is verified through design specifications and construction documentation.
Is BIL funding still available?
The supplemental SRF appropriations are being programmed through their authorization horizon — states are actively awarding the money through IUP cycles now. Competitive grant programs continue on their announced schedules. The practical answer: the window is open, it's competitive, and readiness (engineered projects) is what captures it.
