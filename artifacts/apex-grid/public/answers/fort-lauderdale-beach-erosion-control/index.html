How Is Fort Lauderdale Beach Erosion Controlled?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Fort Lauderdale beach erosion is controlled with periodic sand renourishment, dune restoration with native plantings, and in some reaches groins or breakwaters that slow longshore sand loss. The engineer designs the project around FDEP permits, turtle-nesting windows, and post-storm surveys that measure whether the placed sand is performing as modeled.

Sand Renourishment: Design and Borrow Sources

A renourishment project is a designed earthwork, just one placed by dredge instead of trucks. The engineer starts with the design beach: the width and elevation needed to provide the target storm damage reduction, derived from wave modeling and historical storm impacts on this specific shoreline. Then comes the sand budget analysis — how much sand the beach loses per year to longshore transport and storms — which sets the renourishment volume and the expected interval before the next cycle, typically on the order of years rather than decades. The design includes overfill to account for the rapid initial adjustment, when the placed sand reshapes itself to the wave climate and some fraction leaves the project area. Borrow source engineering is where projects succeed or fail. The engineer cores candidate offshore borrow areas, testing grain size distribution against the native beach sand — too fine and it washes away quickly, too coarse and it changes the beach character and turtle nesting suitability. Color matters too: dark sand absorbs heat and can affect nest temperatures, so the specifications set limits. The dredging plan is designed around environmental constraints: buffers from reef and hardbottom habitat, turbidity controls, and pipeline corridors that avoid seagrass. The engineer also designs the placement operation itself — where the pipe lands, how the sand is graded, and the final tilling and escarpment removal that leave a beach ready for both people and nesting turtles.

Dunes, Structures, and the Permit Path

Dune restoration is engineered as a protective system, not just landscaping. The engineer sets the dune crest elevation based on the storm surge and wave runup the project is designed to withstand, sizes the dune width so it can sacrifice sand to a storm and still stand, and specifies the planting plan — species, spacing, and fertilizer regimes — that establishes the root matrix holding it together. Sand fencing is laid out in the engineer's plans to steer windblown sand into the dune rather than onto the road. Because dunes are dynamic, the design includes a maintenance and monitoring plan: annual profile surveys, replanting after storm damage, and triggers for when renourishment of the dune itself is warranted. Where structures are justified, the engineering is about managing the whole shoreline, not just the project frontage. Groins are designed in series with a sand bypassing or renourishment program that feeds their downdrift side, because an engineer who traps sand without replacing it downdrift has designed someone else's erosion problem. Breakwaters are sited and sized from wave modeling to reduce energy without creating stagnant water or navigation hazards. The permit path reflects this complexity: state environmental permits for the work itself, federal review where waters and wildlife are involved, and coordination with Broward County's beach program, which typically leads the big renourishment efforts while the city handles local dune and access projects. The engineer packages the modeling, the environmental surveys, and the monitoring plan into submittals each agency can review on its own criteria — and those agencies, not the engineer, issue the approvals.

What else do project teams ask?

Why does Fort Lauderdale lose beach sand?
The dominant cause is interruption of the natural longshore sand supply. Inlets and jetties updrift trap sand that would historically have drifted down the coast, and development has hardened shorelines that once supplied sand through natural erosion. Storms then remove sand faster than the diminished supply replaces it, producing the chronic erosion the renourishment program is designed to offset.
How often does a beach need renourishment?
It depends on the local erosion rate, but planning cycles of roughly 5 to 10 years are typical for engineered projects on Florida's southeast coast. The engineer designs each cycle's volume from measured sand loss since the last placement, and major hurricanes can force an off-cycle renourishment when a single storm removes years of accumulated sand.
Who leads beach projects — the city or the county?
In Broward County, the county typically sponsors the large-scale renourishment projects through its beach management program, coordinating with the state and federal cost-share partners, while the City of Fort Lauderdale handles local dune restoration, beach access, and upland coordination. The engineer confirms the project sponsor and the review track early, because county-led and city-led projects follow different procurement and permitting paths.
Why does construction have to avoid turtle nesting season?
Florida's sea turtles nest from roughly March through October, and beach construction — dredge pipes, heavy equipment, altered sand profiles — can destroy nests or deter nesting females. State and federal permits therefore restrict construction windows or require intensive monitoring protocols with nest relocation by permitted personnel. The engineer builds the construction schedule around these windows from the start rather than discovering them during permitting.
