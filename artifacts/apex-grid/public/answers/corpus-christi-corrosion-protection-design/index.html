How Is Corrosion Protection Designed for Corpus Christi Marine Structures?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Corrosion protection for Corpus Christi marine structures is designed as a layered system: high-performance coatings on steel, cathodic protection on submerged piles, corrosion-resistant reinforcement and low-permeability concrete in the splash zone, and detailing that drains saltwater - matched to the structure's exposure zone and design life.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Exposure Zones and Material Selection

The engineer divides every marine structure into exposure zones because each corrodes differently. The atmospheric zone above the splash line faces salt spray and humidity; the splash and tidal zone faces wet-dry cycling, the most aggressive condition; the submerged zone faces continuous immersion with lower oxygen. Coatings carry the atmospheric zone, upgraded concrete and corrosion-resistant reinforcement carry the splash zone, and cathodic protection carries the submerged steel. Getting the zone boundaries right matters - protecting the wrong zone wastes money while the real damage goes unchecked. Material selection follows the zones and the required design life. A 25-year service structure might use conventional reinforcement with good cover and a quality coating system; a 75-year wharf justifies stainless reinforcement in the splash zone and a duplex coating system - hot-dip galvanizing plus topcoat - on the steel. The engineer also considers galvanic compatibility: connecting dissimilar metals underwater without isolation creates a corrosion cell, so isolation details and dielectric separation are part of the design, not field fixes.

Detailing, Inspection, and Service Life

Details decide whether the protection system survives. The engineer eliminates water traps - sloping all horizontal steel, adding weep holes, using drip edges - seals joints that would otherwise pump saltwater in and out with the tide, and keeps the splash zone as simple as geometry allows. Connections get extra coating thickness and are positioned for future inspection access, because a connection nobody can see is a connection nobody maintains. Concrete is detailed with adequate cover and crack control, since the best mix in the world fails if cracks let chlorides reach the steel. The design package includes a corrosion management plan: what to inspect, how often, and what triggers repair or recoating. Anode consumption is monitored electrically, coating condition is surveyed visually and with thickness gauges, and concrete is checked for chloride ingress and delamination. Planning maintenance at design time is what separates a structure that reaches its design life from one that needs emergency repair at year fifteen.

What else do project teams ask?

What is cathodic protection and where is it used?
Cathodic protection is an electrochemical technique that makes the steel structure the cathode of a corrosion cell, so corrosion cannot occur on it. Sacrificial anodes - blocks of zinc or aluminum alloy - corrode instead of the pile, or an impressed-current system drives protective current from a rectifier. It is used on submerged steel piles, sheet piling, and underwater structures where coatings alone cannot be maintained.
Why is the splash zone the worst for corrosion?
The splash zone is alternately wetted by waves and dried by sun and wind, which concentrates chlorides on the surface and drives them into the material with each cycle. It also has abundant oxygen, unlike the submerged zone. That combination - chlorides, oxygen, and cycling - makes the splash zone corrode faster than either the fully submerged or the fully atmospheric zones.
How do coatings and galvanizing differ?
Coatings are barrier systems - paint-like layers that keep saltwater and oxygen away from the steel, requiring surface prep and periodic renewal. Hot-dip galvanizing is a metallurgically bonded zinc coating that protects as a barrier and sacrificially - if scratched, the zinc corrodes first. Duplex systems use both: galvanizing plus a topcoat, for the longest service life in severe exposures.
Who reviews marine structures at the Port of Corpus Christi?
Facilities on port property go through the Port of Corpus Christi's own engineering review rather than the city's. Private marine structures elsewhere in the city permit through Corpus Christi development services; unincorporated county sites use Nueces or San Patricio County permitting; and work in navigable waters needs Corps of Engineers permits. Confirming which AHJ controls the site comes before design starts.
