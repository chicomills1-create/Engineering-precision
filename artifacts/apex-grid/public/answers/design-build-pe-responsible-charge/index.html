Who Holds Responsible Charge on a Design-Build Project?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Responsible charge sits with the licensed PE who actually directed and controlled the engineering — not the firm, not the PM, not an uninvolved PE. Design-build firms need a written responsible-charge map tracing every engineered scope to a named PE, documented through review records at each milestone.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Mapping responsible charge across the team

The map starts with the engineering scopes: structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, civil — each assigned to a named PE licensed in the project state. Then the delegated scopes get added as they are awarded: the steel connection engineer, the truss engineer, the curtain wall engineer, the fire protection designer, each with their PE and license state. The map shows the boundaries explicitly — where the structural EOR's scope ends and the delegated steel engineer's begins — because boundary disputes are where responsibility goes to die. I review the map with the project leadership at kickoff and update it at every major procurement milestone. The map also records the supervision structure: which designers and engineers work under each PE's direction, so that "direct supervision" is a described reality rather than an asserted one. On multi-state programs, the map is maintained per project (licenses do not cross state lines) while the firm's program template keeps the format consistent. It is a simple document — a table, really — and it is the single most important piece of paper in the project's engineering file.

Protecting the PE's engineering authority

The structural risk in design-build is commercial pressure on engineering judgment. The firm wants the permit fast, the budget tight, the redesign minimal — and the PE is the person whose license requires them to say "not yet" when the review is not complete. Healthy firms protect that authority explicitly: the PE's engineering decisions are final within the engineering scope, schedule pressure gets resolved by resourcing the review (more reviewers, phased reviews) rather than by compressing it, and no one — not the PM, not the principal, not the client — can direct the PE to seal work the PE has not reviewed. I put this in the firm's engineering policy, not just in project agreements, because it has to hold on the hardest project, not just the easy ones. The practical protections are procedural: review milestones with acceptance criteria that cannot be skipped, a revision protocol that requires re-review of corrections before sealing, and a clear escalation path when schedule and engineering conflict (the answer is always more review resources or a revised schedule, never a premature seal). I have watched firms with these protections sail through board inquiries and disputes; I have watched firms without them explain to a board why a PE sealed a design they could not describe. The authority protection is not bureaucracy — it is the firm's license to keep practicing.

Responsible-charge checklist for design-build firms

Make responsible charge visible, documented, and defensible on every project. • Written responsible-charge map at kickoff: every engineered scope traced to a named, state-licensed PE, updated as delegated scopes award • Supervision structure described per PE — who works under whose direction — so direct supervision is a record, not a claim • Milestone review records: the PE's comments, revision directives, calculation verification, and re-review of corrections, filed per project • Firm policy protecting the PE's engineering authority: no seal before the review is complete, regardless of schedule pressure • Boundary definitions between EOR scopes and delegated scopes — no engineered work floating without its responsible PE

What else do project teams ask?

Can responsible charge be split across multiple engineers on one project?
Yes — and on any significant design-build project, it must be. The structural PE holds responsible charge of the structural design, the mechanical PE of the mechanical, and so on; delegated components like trusses or curtain wall sit with their own engineers. What cannot happen is a gap: every engineered scope needs its responsible PE, and the splits need to be documented so there is no ambiguity about who directed what. I build the responsible-charge map at project setup and update it as delegated scopes are awarded. Split responsibility with clear boundaries is professional; blurred responsibility with no boundaries is a violation.
What does 'direct supervision' actually require of the PE?
It requires the PE to exercise real direction and control over the engineering: assigning the work, reviewing the assumptions and methods, checking the calculations, directing revisions, and reviewing the final product before sealing. It does not require the PE to personally perform every calculation — engineering teams exist — but it does require the PE to be genuinely in charge of the technical decisions. Reviewing a finished drawing set once and sealing it is not direct supervision; neither is sealing work performed by another firm's engineers without any involvement. State boards have disciplined engineers for both. I document supervision through the review record: comments issued, revisions directed, corrections re-reviewed.
Does the design-build firm's PM hold any responsible charge?
Only if the PM is also the licensed PE who directed the engineering — the title "project manager" confers no engineering authority. On design-build projects the PM typically manages schedule, budget, and coordination, which are commercial functions, while the PE holds the professional responsibility for engineering decisions. Problems arise when the PM's schedule pressure overrides the PE's engineering judgment — compressing the review, skipping the re-review of corrections, pushing a seal before the review is complete. I advise firms to draw this line explicitly: the PM owns the schedule, the PE owns the engineering, and the PE's refusal to seal an unreviewed design is not insubordination — it is the license working as intended.
What happens if responsible charge is challenged after the seal?
The board investigates, and the investigation turns on the record: who directed the engineering, what the supervision looked like, whether the calculations were genuinely reviewed. A firm with milestone review records, documented revision directives, and a clear responsible-charge map answers the challenge with evidence. A firm with only the sealed drawings and a PE who cannot describe the engineering decisions behind them is in serious trouble — potential discipline for the engineer and liability exposure for the firm. This is why I treat documentation as part of the engineering work itself, not as administrative overhead. The record is the proof that the seal meant something.
