How Are Feedlot Truck Scales Designed for Accurate Weighing?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Truck scales are engineered as financial instruments: full truck-length decks, settlement-proof foundations, straight level approaches, drainage and cleaning access, and legal-for-trade certification with calibration access for the scale's whole life.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Scale Length, Type, and Foundation Engineering

The engineer starts with the design vehicle: the longest truck-trailer combination the scale will ever weigh determines the deck length, with no split weighing. Seventy feet covers most semi combinations, and the engineer confirms it against the operation's actual haulers. Pit versus pitless is then decided on site conditions — high water table and poor drainage favor pitless; tight sites with limited approach room may favor pit — and the engineer details the chosen type completely, including the pit's drainage, ventilation, and personnel access where applicable. Foundation design follows the geotechnical report. The engineer sizes piers or footings for the concentrated axle loads, designs against settlement and frost heave, and details drainage that keeps water away from the foundation soils. The scale deck's steel or concrete structure is coordinated with the foundation so load paths are clean and the deck stays level; the engineer specifies the concrete mix, reinforcement, and curing for a deck that takes impact loading for decades without cracking into inaccuracy.

Approaches, Certification, and Operations

Approaches make or break accuracy. The engineer designs straight, level, paved approaches at least a full truck length on each side, so the vehicle rests on the scale without pushing, pulling, or binding against a grade. Turning movements on or immediately beside the scale are eliminated from the traffic plan, and the engineer grades the approaches to drain away from the deck — standing water on the approaches becomes ice, mud, and weighing error. Legal-for-trade certification is planned from the start. The engineer confirms the state weights-and-measures requirements — test-weight handling, approach clearances, indicator visibility — and designs the scale house, signage, and traffic flow around them. The indicator goes where the operator sees the truck and the ticket; lighting covers night weighing; and the data connection ties weights into the operation's feed and cattle records. Calibration access — to load cells, junction boxes, and the understructure — is detailed so service takes hours, not days, and the engineer sets the recalibration schedule the operation will follow.

Feedlot Truck Scale Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the scale is built: • Deck length fits the longest truck-trailer combination entirely; no split weighing • Pit vs. pitless selected for water table, drainage, and approach geometry • Foundation engineered from the geotechnical report against settlement and frost heave • Straight, level, paved approaches at least one truck length each way • Approaches graded to drain away from the deck; no turning on the scale • State legal-for-trade certification requirements confirmed and designed in • Scale house or indicator placed for truck visibility, ticketing, and night lighting • Test-weight and calibration access to load cells, boxes, and understructure • Weights tied into feed and cattle record systems; recalibration schedule set

What else do project teams ask?

How long should a feedlot truck scale be?
Long enough to hold the entire truck-trailer combination at once — typically 70 feet or more for semis. The engineer confirms the length against the operation's actual haulers, because weighing a combination in two parts introduces error that defeats the scale's purpose.
What is the difference between pit and pitless scales?
A pit scale sits flush with the roadway over a constructed pit; a pitless scale sits above grade on a low foundation with short ramps. Pitless suits high water tables and is easier to service; pit suits tight sites. The engineer chooses on drainage, soils, and approach geometry — and details the foundation either way so it never moves.
Who certifies a feedlot truck scale?
The state's weights-and-measures authority, for scales used in commercial transactions — which covers feed bought and cattle sold. The engineer designs to the state's legal-for-trade requirements and provides the access and documentation certification needs, then the operation keeps the scale on a regular recalibration schedule.
Why do scale approaches need to be level?
Because a truck on a grade pushes or binds against the scale, and the load cells read that force as weight. Straight, level approaches let the vehicle rest neutrally on the deck. The engineer holds the approach grade flat for at least a truck length each way and keeps turning movements off the scale.
