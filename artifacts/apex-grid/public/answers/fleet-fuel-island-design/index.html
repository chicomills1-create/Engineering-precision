How Is Fleet Fuel Island Design Engineered for Private Fleets?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A private fleet fuel island is engineered for the fleet's own vehicles: high-flow dispensers sized to the fueling window, cardlock tracking of every gallon to vehicle and driver, and a drive-through layout with clearances, containment, and fire-code compliance for the fleet's largest equipment.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Sizing Dispensers and Pumps to the Fueling Window

The design starts with the fleet's operating schedule: how many vehicles fuel, when, and how many gallons each takes. That window — often a tight morning or evening block — sets the required dispenser count and flow rate: the engineer divides the total gallons by the window and the per-vehicle fueling time to get simultaneous dispenser demand, then sizes the submersible turbine pumps, product piping, and tank compartments so the system delivers full flow at every active dispenser. Piping hydraulics matter at fleet flows. High-flow diesel at 30-plus gpm per dispenser needs larger product piping and carefully designed manifolds to avoid starving the farthest island; the engineer runs the hydraulic calculation for the peak simultaneous case and sizes accordingly. Tank compartments are matched to daily consumption and the delivery schedule — a fleet that burns 2,000 gallons of diesel a day needs compartment capacity and delivery frequency that never let the island run dry mid-window.

Cardlock Tracking and Private-Yard Layout

The authorization system ties fuel to accountability. The engineer specifies the cardlock controller, the credential type — fleet cards, driver PINs, or vehicle-mounted tags — and the data integration with the fleet management software, including odometer-prompt configuration and exception reporting for transactions that do not reconcile. The controller and its network gear go on backed-up power so a blip does not lose the morning's transaction data. The yard layout is drawn around the fleet's equipment: swept-path analysis for the longest vehicle, canopy height for the tallest plus margin, drive-through lanes so vehicles never back up, and island protection rated for the fleet's weights. Containment grading, leak detection, and emergency shutoff meet the fuel-code requirements, and the engineer submits the private installation to the fire marshal — city or county depending on the yard's jurisdiction — because private does not mean unregulated.

Private Fleet Fuel Island Design Checklist

Confirm these with the fleet manager and fire marshal: • Fueling window and per-vehicle gallons documented; simultaneous dispenser demand calculated • High-flow dispensers, pumps, and piping sized hydraulically for the peak simultaneous case • Tank compartments matched to daily consumption and delivery logistics • Cardlock credentials, authorization rules, and fleet-software data integration specified • Controller and network gear on backed-up power; transaction data reconciles with fleet system • Swept-path analysis for the fleet's longest vehicle; canopy clearance for the tallest • Drive-through lanes with heavy-duty island protection; no backing maneuvers required • Fire-marshal permit for the private installation — city or county agency per the yard's jurisdiction • Spill containment, leak detection, and emergency shutoff per fuel-code requirements

What else do project teams ask?

How is a fleet fuel island different from a gas station?
No public customers, much higher flow rates, and transaction tracking instead of retail payment. Fleet islands use high-flow dispensers sized to a tight fueling window, cardlock systems that log every gallon to a vehicle and driver, and layouts built for the fleet's specific trucks — but the fuel storage, leak detection, and fire-code requirements are the same as a retail station's.
What flow rate do fleet diesel dispensers need?
Commonly 30 gallons per minute or more per hose, versus about 10 gpm at a retail diesel dispenser. The engineer sizes the dispensers, pumps, and piping from the fleet's fueling window: total gallons divided by available time and acceptable per-vehicle fueling duration sets the required simultaneous flow.
Does a private fuel island need permits?
Yes. A private fleet fuel island is still a motor fuel dispensing facility under the fire and building codes — tanks, piping, dispensers, and electrical all get permitted and inspected, and the fire marshal reviews the installation. Private means no public customers, not no regulation.
How does the fleet track who took fuel?
Through the cardlock controller: each transaction requires a credential — fleet card, driver PIN, or vehicle tag — and logs vehicle, driver, product, gallons, and odometer to the fleet management system. The engineer coordinates the data format with the fleet's software so fuel records reconcile automatically.
