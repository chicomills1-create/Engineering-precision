How Is Elevatoring Designed for Stacked Mixed-Use Buildings?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Elevatoring for stacked mixed-use is engineered as separate banks per occupancy — each sized by its own traffic analysis with appropriate controls — plus secured residential cars, physically separated lobbies, and scheduled service elevators serving all uses without crossing public paths.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Traffic Analysis and Bank Sizing

The engineer performs the standard vertical-transportation calculations independently for each bank: population per floor, the five-minute handling capacity as a percentage of that population, and the interval — the average time between car arrivals at the lobby. Office buildings target roughly 12 to 14 percent handling capacity with 25- to 30-second intervals at the morning peak; residential buildings accept lower handling capacity with 60- to 90-second intervals reflecting their distributed demand. Car size, speed, and count are iterated against these targets, and the engineer documents the assumptions — floor populations, peak factors — so the analysis can be revisited if the program changes. Destination dispatch changes the office bank's math. By grouping passengers for the same floors into the same car before they board, destination dispatch cuts the number of stops and improves both handling capacity and interval — often allowing one fewer car than a conventional system would need. The engineer evaluates the premium against the core space saved, since every elevator shaft removed is leasable area gained on every floor. For the residential bank, conventional collective control is usually sufficient and simpler for residents; the engineer matches the control sophistication to the demand pattern rather than defaulting to the premium system everywhere.

Security, Service, and Life-Safety Integration

The access-control integration is drawn car by car. Residential cars require credential before the floor buttons activate; office cars require credential for after-hours operation with free access during business hours per the tenant's policy; service cars operate on a managed schedule with building-staff override. The elevator lobbies get CCTV coverage, emergency phones in every car, and intercom to the management office. The engineer coordinates the elevator controls with the access-control contractor so the two systems commission as one — a credential system the elevator ignores is a security design that exists only on paper. Life-safety integration covers recall, stretcher access, and seismic design. On fire alarm, the elevators recall to the designated floor per the code's sequence, with fire-fighter's service available in the designated cars; the engineer coordinates the recall sequence with the fire-alarm design. At least one car per bank accommodates a stretcher, which sets minimum car dimensions the architect must hold. Seismic switches, counterweight guards, and the seismic design of the guide rails follow the code for the project's seismic design category — and the engineer verifies the machine-room-less or machine-room equipment against the structural engineer's anchorage design.

Stacked Mixed-Use Elevatoring Checklist

Use this checklist before the elevatoring design is finalized: • Traffic analysis completed per bank: office up-peak, residential distributed, retail public • Handling capacity and interval targets set per occupancy and met by the car count, size, and speed • Destination dispatch evaluated for the office bank against core-space savings • Residential cars on credential control with a secured, separated residential elevator lobby • Service elevator sized for the largest freight load with dedicated loading access • Freight scheduling rules coordinated with the property management plan • Fire recall sequence, stretcher accommodation, and seismic requirements in the spec • Elevator controls integrated and commissioned with the access-control system

What else do project teams ask?

How many elevators does a mixed-use building need?
As many as the per-occupancy traffic analyses require — there is no mixed-use rule of thumb. A typical stacked project might need three to four office cars for the morning peak, two residential cars for distributed demand, one or two public retail cars, and one service car. The engineer sizes each bank to its own demand pattern and documents the analysis, since the program's unit count and office density drive the answer more than the building's height alone.
Can office workers ride the residential elevators?
Not in a properly designed building. The residential cars require resident credentials and open only to the secured residential lobby and residential floors — office workers have no credential and no path to the residential lobby. The separation is physical (distinct lobbies), electronic (access control), and operational (management policy). Shared use would defeat the residential security the buyers or renters were promised.
What is destination dispatch and does mixed-use need it?
Destination dispatch is an elevator control system where passengers enter their floor at a lobby kiosk and are assigned to a specific car, grouping same-floor passengers to cut stops and wait times. It pays off in the office bank's morning up-peak, where it can reduce the required car count. The residential bank rarely needs it — distributed demand does not benefit much from grouping — so the engineer typically specifies it for the office bank only.
How are move-ins handled without disrupting the building?
Through the service elevator on a scheduled, managed basis. The property management plan reserves the freight car in time blocks, requires protective coverings in the service corridor and car, and routes all move-in traffic through the loading area — never through the residential lobby or the public elevators. The engineer sizes the service car and corridor for full move-in loads and provides the access-control overrides the schedule needs.
