How Is an Existing Retail Space Converted Into a Laundromat?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A retail-to-laundromat conversion starts with a structural and MEP capacity survey, then engineers the upgrades — slab reinforcement, new water and drainage, upsized electrical and gas services, and full dryer exhaust with makeup air — plus change-of-occupancy code compliance.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The feasibility survey: what the building gives you

Before design starts, I survey what exists: slab thickness and condition, structural system, ceiling heights, column spacing, existing electrical service size and spare capacity, water service and meter size, sanitary sewer location and invert, gas availability and pressure, and roof capacity for new equipment. I identify the ventilation paths — where dryer exhaust can legally and practically terminate — and flag the code triggers the conversion will set off. The survey produces a feasibility report with the upgrade scope and a realistic cost range, so the owner decides with eyes open. Skipping this step is how conversions become money pits.

Sequencing the structural and MEP upgrades

The work sequences from the hardest to change outward. Structural reinforcement and slab work come first — thickened pads, trench saw-cutting, equipment curbs — coordinated with the final equipment layout so nothing gets poured twice. Underground plumbing and the utility corridor follow, then the new electrical service and distribution, gas piping, and water heating plant. Dryer exhaust ductwork and makeup air units go in as the interior builds out, with fire protection, lighting, and finishes following. I phase the drawings and the construction sequence so each trade's work supports the next, and the landlord's requirements — work hours, common-area protection, roof warranties — are built into the plan.

Conversion risks to manage early

The risks that sink retail conversions, handled upfront: • Inadequate slab discovered late — survey it before signing the lease, not after • Utility upgrade lead times — order service upsizes the week the project is approved • Landlord restrictions on roof penetrations, exhaust terminations, and work hours • Hidden conditions in older buildings: asbestos, undersized sewers, unknown structure • Parking and zoning — a laundromat's traffic and hours may need approvals retail didn't • Budget contingency sized for a conversion, not new construction — surprises are the norm

What else do project teams ask?

Can any retail space become a laundromat?
Most can, but the cost varies enormously with the building's starting point. A ground-floor space with a robust slab, high ceilings, good utility access, and a cooperative landlord is a strong candidate; a second-floor space with a thin slab, low ceilings, and no gas service can cost more to convert than new construction. The feasibility survey — structure, utilities, ventilation paths, and code — answers the question with numbers before the owner commits to a lease.
What structural work does a conversion typically need?
Evaluation of the slab for washer loads and vibration is the big one — many retail slabs need thickening, reinforcement, or isolated pads under the heavy machines. I also check the roof structure for new exhaust fans and makeup air units, and the floor for trench drains and utility corridors that get saw-cut into the slab. The structural engineer designs the reinforcement, and the MEP design coordinates every penetration and load with it.
How are utilities brought into a former retail space?
Usually with significant upgrades: a larger water service and meter, new sanitary drainage with lint handling where the old system can't cope, an upsized electrical service with new distribution, and gas service sized for the dryer and heating load. I coordinate each utility with its provider early — service upgrades have lead times — and I route everything through the new utility trench or corridor designed into the slab work. The existing building's utility locations often dictate the equipment layout.
What code issues come with a change of occupancy?
A retail-to-laundromat conversion typically triggers accessibility upgrades to current standards, fire protection design for the new hazard classification, plumbing and mechanical code compliance for the new systems, and sometimes energy code upgrades. The authority having jurisdiction determines exactly what the change of occupancy requires, and I address it in the permit documents from the start — discovering code triggers mid-construction is how budgets die.
