How Do Colorado Springs Foundations Handle Expansive Soil and Frost?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Colorado Springs foundations must satisfy the Pikes Peak Regional Building Code: a 30-inch minimum frost depth, severe weathering, and site-specific snow and wind criteria. On the area's expansive Pierre Shale-derived clays, designs use drilled piers bearing below both the frost line and the moisture-active zone, with structural floors and strict drainage.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

PPRBC Climatic Design Criteria That Drive Foundation Depth

The Pikes Peak Regional Building Code's climatic and geographic design table is the starting point for every structural design in the region. It fixes the frost line at a 30-inch minimum, assigns severe weathering for concrete and masonry durability, and sets the wind, snow, and seismic criteria by elevation. Below 7,000 feet the ground snow load is 43 psf; at or above 7,000 feet it jumps to 57 psf, with flat-roof values of 30 and 40 psf respectively, and ice barrier underlayment is required at or above 7,000 feet. These are code minimums: the structural engineer designs to them and to the geotechnical report, whichever demands more. Frost depth and expansive soil interact. A footing placed exactly at the 30-inch frost minimum is protected from frost heave but still sits inside the moisture-active clay on an expansive site, so the geotechnical engineer sets the real bearing depth from swell testing. On the worst clays, drilled piers extend tens of feet to bedrock or stable strata, with the 30-inch frost line becoming a footnote rather than the controlling criterion. The lesson for owners: frost depth is the floor of the design, and the soils report sets the ceiling.

Designing for Two Kinds of Ground Movement

Swell and frost heave move soil for different reasons and demand the same answer: get the bearing below the movement. Expansive clay swells when it absorbs water, exerting upward pressures that can lift lightly loaded buildings; frost heave lifts soil when ice lenses grow in freezing, moisture-rich ground. A foundation that defeats one but not the other still moves, which is why Colorado Springs designs address both in a single system: piers bearing in stable material below the active zone and the frost line, grade beams tying pier tops together, and structural floors spanning over a void so heaving soil never bears on the slab. Water control serves both masters. Positive grading carries surface water away from the building, gutters discharge clear of the foundation, and irrigation is kept back from the walls, because water feeds both clay swell and the moisture that becomes ice lenses. The landscape plan is part of the foundation design on expansive sites: high-water plantings stay away from the building, and grade changes must not dam water against walls. Consistent, moderate soil moisture is the goal, since desiccation can cause settlement as the clay shrinks.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the frost depth in Colorado Springs?
The Pikes Peak Regional Building Code sets a 30-inch minimum frost line depth. All footings, piers, and foundations must bear below that depth. On expansive soils, the geotechnical engineer typically requires much deeper bearing, below the moisture-active clay, so frost depth is the minimum rather than the design depth.
Which agency reviews building permits in Colorado Springs?
The Pikes Peak Regional Building Department is the regional agency serving Colorado Springs, El Paso County, and several surrounding jurisdictions. It administers the Pikes Peak Regional Building Code, reviews plans, and performs inspections. One regional department means consistent code interpretation across the metro.
What snow load applies to a Colorado Springs project?
Under the PPRBC, the ground snow load is 43 psf below 7,000 feet of elevation and 57 psf at or above 7,000 feet, with flat-roof snow loads of 30 and 40 psf respectively. The engineer confirms the site's grade-plane elevation and designs for drift, unbalanced, and sliding snow per the structural code.
Why is drainage critical on Colorado Springs clay?
Water drives both threats the foundations face: it swells expansive clay and feeds the ice lenses behind frost heave. Grading that ponds water against the building, gutters that discharge at the foundation, and irrigation aimed at the walls all undermine the foundation design. Positive drainage away from the structure is a permanent requirement, not a construction-phase detail.
Do higher-elevation areas around Colorado Springs have different rules?
Yes. At or above 7,000 feet, the code raises snow loads and requires ice barrier underlayment, and mountain-fringe areas like the Black Forest add wildfire provisions to the review. The engineer confirms the site's elevation-driven criteria and any local amendments with the building department before finalizing the design.
