Does an ADU Need a PE Stamp?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Most ADUs beyond simple single-story conventional builds need a PE stamp for structural review and — in California — Title 24 energy compliance. Streamlined ADU planning laws didn't waive engineering; two-story, hillside, and conversion ADUs all get full review-and-seal.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Structural review for small buildings

ADU structural review is full-building engineering at small scale, and small scale doesn't mean simple. I verify the gravity system — floor and roof framing sized for the spans, bearing conditions, header sizes over the generous openings ADU plans love — and then the lateral system, which is where ADUs most often need real design. Compact footprints with window and door openings on every wall leave limited shear wall length, so the holdown schedule, shear wall aspect ratios, and connection detailing get the same rigor as a much larger building. Two-story ADUs add overturning and diaphragm checks that single-story conventional construction never faces. Foundations follow the site. On stable soils with a straightforward geotechnical picture, the review verifies a conventional foundation design. On expansive clay — common across Texas metros and parts of Arizona — the PE designs or verifies pier depths, slab stiffening, and the moisture detailing that keeps the soil stable. Hillside ADUs in California add retaining, drainage, and slope-stability considerations that push firmly into engineered territory. The sealed structural set shows the design criteria, the calculations, and the details the contractor builds from.

Energy, utilities, and the California layer

In California, Title 24 compliance is a coequal part of the ADU engineering package. I verify the energy compliance documentation against the actual design: envelope insulation and glazing values matching the plans, the HVAC system's efficiency and sizing, water heating, and the solar-readiness provisions the code requires. ADUs are small enough that envelope and equipment choices swing the compliance margin significantly — a value-engineered window package can fail the model — so the reconciliation between forms and drawings happens before sealing, not at plan check. Utilities get engineered attention everywhere. The electrical design shows the ADU's service — new service or feeder from the house panel — with the load analysis proving capacity. Plumbing shows the connection to the site's sewer or septic with the fixture count the system can serve; septic-constrained sites in particular need the capacity question answered before design, not after. Fire separation between the ADU and the primary dwelling, and to property lines, gets verified against the code for the construction type and proximity. These are the items that stall ADU permits when they're left to the contractor's judgment.

ADU PE review checklist

An ADU submittal is ready for the PE's seal when the small building gets the full engineering treatment. ADUs earn corrections in direct proportion to how 'simple' someone assumed they'd be. • Structural review: gravity framing, lateral system with detailed shear walls and holdowns, foundation designed for the site's soils • Geotechnical input where expansive clay, hillside, or AHJ requirements demand it — Texas and Arizona ADUs especially • Title 24 energy compliance documented and reconciled with the design for California ADUs • Electrical service or feeder design with load analysis proving capacity for the ADU's loads • Plumbing connection to sewer or septic with capacity confirmed; fire separation verified • Local AHJ intake expectations confirmed early — ADU submittal requirements vary widely by city

What else do project teams ask?

Did California's ADU laws eliminate the engineering requirements?
No — they streamlined planning approval, not engineering. The state's ADU statutes limit what cities can require on the zoning side: setbacks, parking, owner-occupancy, and discretionary review. But the building code still applies in full, and where the code requires engineered design — structural beyond conventional construction limits, Title 24 energy compliance — a licensed PE's review and seal is still required. I see this confusion constantly: an owner assumes 'ministerial approval' means 'no engineering,' then learns at the building counter that the structural and energy documents are still required. Plan for the engineering from the start.
Do garage-conversion ADUs need structural engineering?
Usually, yes — conversions change the structural demands. Opening up the garage front for living space removes the wall that was bracing that side, so a new lateral system — moment frames or shear walls — has to be designed. The slab gets evaluated for living-space use: moisture, levelness, and whether it was built as a structural slab or a thin garage pour. New plumbing penetrations, the electrical service rework, and Title 24 energy compliance for the converted envelope all land in the engineered set. A garage conversion looks simple and engineers like anything but; the PE's review catches the lateral and slab issues that make or break these projects.
What soils issues affect ADU foundations in Texas and Arizona?
Expansive clay in much of Texas and parts of Arizona makes ADU foundations an engineered decision, not a default detail. The PE reviews the geotechnical information — or requires it where the AHJ does — and designs the foundation for the site: pier depths, slab stiffening, and moisture control appropriate to the soil's swell potential. Small structures are actually more vulnerable to differential movement because they lack the weight to resist it. I won't seal an ADU foundation on expansive soil from a generic detail; the design has to answer the site's soil, and the sealed drawings show how.
Can a manufactured or prefab ADU skip the PE review?
The unit itself may carry state approvals or manufacturer engineering, but the site work doesn't skip review. Foundations, utility connections, anchorage to the foundation, and any site-built elements — stairs, decks, carports — still need engineering for the specific site's soils, seismic, and wind criteria, sealed by a PE licensed in the state. The AHJ permits the installation, not just the box, and the installation is where site-specific engineering lives. I review prefab ADU projects as foundation-and-site engineering engagements, verifying the manufacturer's anchorage requirements against the designed foundation.
