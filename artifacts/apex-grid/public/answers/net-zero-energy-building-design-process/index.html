What Is the Engineering Process for Net-Zero Energy Buildings?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Net-zero design sets a numeric EUI target first, reduces loads through envelope and system efficiency, sizes renewables only to cover the remaining demand, and verifies actual performance with commissioning and metering.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Setting the EUI target

I set the target before the design develops far enough to lock in bad decisions. The benchmarking step pulls EUI data for the building type — offices, schools, multifamily, retail — in the project's climate zone, and I present the team with the median, the efficient quartile, and what the best measured buildings achieve. The target lands between the efficient quartile and the best-in-class, aggressive enough to force real design choices but grounded enough that the team believes it. Everyone signs the target: owner, architect, and engineers, because net-zero dies the moment one party optimizes for something else. The target also gets translated into design rules the team can use daily. An EUI target of 25 means the envelope needs certain U-values and airtightness, the lighting needs a certain power density, and the HVAC needs a certain system type — I work those implications out early so the architects are not designing a glass box that the engineers must somehow make net-zero. When the energy model shows the design drifting over budget, the target gives me the standing to push back on scope creep that would break the energy case.

Efficiency before renewables

The efficiency phase follows the load hierarchy. Envelope first: I push insulation levels, airtightness, and glazing performance past code minimums, because envelope improvements pay back for the life of the building with no moving parts. Then internal loads: efficient lighting with good controls, and a realistic plug-load budget — in offices, plug loads are often the largest single end use, and no HVAC system can fix a building full of wasteful equipment. Then HVAC: I select systems for part-load efficiency and controllability, not just full-load ratings, since buildings spend most hours far from design conditions. Energy modeling is the thread through all of it. I model early with a simple massing model, refine as the design develops, and run option comparisons — envelope package A versus B, system type X versus Y — so decisions are made on modeled EUI impact rather than rules of thumb. The model also sizes the renewables: once the proposed design's EUI is stable, the remaining annual consumption divided by the local solar resource gives the array size. That number is always smaller — and cheaper — than the array the project would have needed without the efficiency work.

Net-zero process checklist

A net-zero project stays on track when the sequence is enforced and the target is treated as a hard constraint. Skip a step and the economics fall apart. • Benchmark the building type and climate, set a signed EUI target before design development • Model early and continuously — every major design decision gets an EUI impact check • Drive loads down in order: envelope, lighting and plug loads, HVAC efficiency and controls • Size renewables only after the efficiency package is locked, from the modeled remaining demand • Confirm utility interconnection, net-metering, and export-credit rules with the serving utility during design • Commission every energy-significant system and install end-use sub-metering for first-year verification

What else do project teams ask?

What is EUI and why does net-zero start there?
Energy use intensity — annual energy per square foot — is the single number that tells you whether a building is lean or wasteful, independent of its size. Net-zero means the building produces as much energy as it consumes over a year, so the EUI target defines exactly how much renewable energy is needed. Starting with EUI keeps the team honest: you cannot offset your way out of a bad envelope without paying for it in array size. I set the target from benchmarked data for the building type and climate, then manage the design to it like a budget.
Should we just put solar on the roof of our existing building?
You can, but do the efficiency work first or you will buy more solar than you need. I start with an energy audit that finds the cost-effective load reductions — lighting, controls, envelope fixes, HVAC upgrades — because every kilowatt-hour eliminated is a kilowatt-hour the array never has to generate. On many buildings the efficiency measures pay for themselves faster than the solar does. The right sequence is reduce, then produce; skipping the first step is how projects end up with a roof full of panels on a building that still wastes energy.
How is a net-zero building verified?
By measuring it. The design energy model predicts the EUI, commissioning proves the systems were installed and are operating as designed, and sub-metering tracks actual consumption by end use through at least the first year. I compare the measured data to the model, investigate the gaps — there are always gaps — and tune the building until it performs. A net-zero claim without a year of meter data is a projection, not a result.
Does net-zero require batteries?
No — net-zero is an annual energy balance, and the grid serves as the balancing mechanism in most projects. Batteries add resilience and can improve the economics where utilities charge demand fees or time-of-use rates, but they are not required to reach net-zero energy. I evaluate storage on its own merits: outage resilience, demand-charge reduction, and utility program incentives. Do not let a battery vendor tell you net-zero is impossible without their product.
