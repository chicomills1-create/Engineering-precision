What Does a Structural PE Stamp Cover in St. Petersburg, FL?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A structural PE stamp in St. Petersburg certifies the gravity system, lateral system, and foundations after a Florida-licensed PE verifies the calculations under responsible charge — with hurricane wind load paths, flood-zone foundations, and coastal corrosion detailing getting the closest scrutiny.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The St. Petersburg submittal path

Structural permits in St. Petersburg go through the city's Construction Services, where the sealed drawings, calculations package, geotechnical report, and flood documentation are submitted for plan review and routed to the discipline reviewers. I confirm the current intake checklist before submitting, because submittal standards evolve and a set that fails intake never reaches a reviewer. The structural sheets need to show the design criteria — wind design parameters, exposure, and risk category — the code references, the geotechnical report, and the flood zone standing with the design flood elevation. Corrections come back consolidated per cycle, and the resubmittal answers every item with clearly clouded changes. Wind, flood, and foundation sheets get the most preparation before the first submittal, because that's where this city's reviewers live.

Wind, water, and salt: the coastal review

The St. Petersburg structural review runs on three coastal tracks at once. Wind is first: the PE verifies the design wind pressures for the site's exposure, checks the main wind-force-resisting system as a continuous load path from roof covering to foundation, and separately verifies components and cladding — because the building envelope takes the beating in a hurricane. Water is second: flood zone provisions, elevated construction, foundation systems for saturated soils and scour, and drainage that doesn't undermine the structure it protects. Salt is third: corrosion-resistant material selections and detailing for structural steel, connections, and reinforcement in a marine-adjacent environment. A PE who reviews one track and waves at the other two hasn't reviewed a St. Petersburg building. The sealed set shows all three resolved.

St. Petersburg structural submittal checklist

A structural package is ready for the PE's review — and then for Construction Services — when these items are resolved. Coastal completeness is the theme: wind, water, and salt, all detailed. • Florida-licensed PE in responsible charge, identified in the seal block • Geotechnical report with boring logs, foundation recommendations, and groundwater conditions • Flood zone standing with design flood elevation and flood provisions detailed • Wind design parameters, exposure, and risk category shown on the drawings • Continuous wind load path with every connection detailed to its calculated demand • Corrosion-resistant materials and detailing for the coastal environment

What else do project teams ask?

Why does wind dominate the structural review in St. Petersburg?
Because the Florida Building Code's wind provisions control lateral design for coastal Pinellas County, and hurricanes are the design event. The PE verifies the wind speeds and exposure for the site, checks the main wind-force-resisting system and the components-and-cladding design separately — because the roof covering, windows, and wall cladding see the highest pressures — and confirms every connection in the load path: roof-to-wall, wall-to-foundation, with uplift anchorage sized for the calculated reactions. The corrections I see most are discontinuous load paths and cladding attachments that assumed inland conditions. On a peninsula city, the wind review is the review, and the sealed set has to show its work.
How do flood zones change the foundation review?
Flood zones move the foundation discussion from bearing capacity alone to elevation, buoyancy, and water. The PE checks the site's flood zone standing, verifies the lowest floor elevations against the design flood elevation, and confirms the foundation system suits both the geotechnical report and the flood provisions — pile or deep foundations where scour and saturated soils demand them, anchorage against buoyancy and lateral flood loads, and breakaway or elevated construction details where the zone requires. Enclosures below the elevated floor get checked for flood openings. I coordinate the structural flood detailing with the architectural elevations, because a beautiful rendering at the wrong elevation is a permit rejection. Flood provisions are structural work in St. Petersburg, full stop.
What are Florida's PE seal requirements for a permit submittal?
In Florida, engineering is regulated by the Florida Board of Professional Engineers, and the sealing engineer must hold a Florida PE license and take responsible charge of the work — the seal represents the engineer's direct professional responsibility. The city requires the sealed drawings and supporting calculations for permit review, and Florida's threshold rules determine which projects require a licensed engineer's seal in the first place. For your St. Petersburg project, the practical meaning is the standard one: the PE who seals your structural drawings actually reviewed the design, verified the calculations, and marked the revisions. Confirm the Florida license early, especially if your design team is based out of state.
How fast can a PE review and seal structural drawings in St. Petersburg?
The engineering review typically takes one to three weeks, driven by project size and drawing completeness — a clean set with the geotechnical report and flood documentation in hand moves fast, while a set with an unresolved lateral scheme or missing site data takes longer because the PE has to send it back first. After sealing, the city's Construction Services review adds its own cycles, so I budget at least one correction round on top of published review times. Coastal projects carry extra homework — wind analysis, flood provisions, corrosion detailing — so delivering the complete package on day one is what keeps the review on the short path.
