How Is a Detroit Basement Engineered to Stay Dry?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A Detroit basement is engineered to stay dry by relieving hydrostatic pressure with interior or exterior perimeter drains tied to a sump pump, sealing walls with waterproof membranes, grading soil away from the foundation, and managing the city's heavy clay soils that trap water against old foundation walls.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Relieving Hydrostatic Pressure

Hydrostatic pressure is the force that drives most basement leaks: water saturating the soil outside the wall pushes inward, finding every crack, tie hole, and porous spot in old concrete. Perimeter drains relieve that pressure by giving water an easier path. On the exterior, a perforated pipe bedded in washed stone at the footing level collects groundwater and carries it to a sump pit or a gravity outlet; the stone and filter fabric keep Detroit's clay fines from clogging the pipe over time. On the interior, a channel cut into the slab edge at the wall-footing joint performs the same function, with weep holes drilled through the wall base to drain the block cores in masonry foundations. The sump system is the heart of the interior approach. The engineer sizes the pit to hold the inflow during a design storm, selects a pump with enough head to lift water to the discharge point, and routes the discharge line well away from the foundation - dumping sump water next to the house just recycles it. A check valve on the discharge line prevents backflow, an airtight pit cover with a sealed discharge penetration keeps soil gas and moisture out of the basement air, and a high-water alarm gives the owner warning before the floor gets wet. Where the water table is persistently high, the design may call for a duplex pump arrangement so a second pump carries the load if the first fails.

Detroit's Clay Soils and Aging Foundations

Southeast Michigan clay is the quiet adversary in every Detroit waterproofing job. It swells when wet, shrinks when dry, drains almost not at all, and holds water against foundation walls for days after a storm. An engineer designs around this by keeping clay away from the wall wherever possible: exterior excavations are backfilled with granular, free-draining material rather than the native clay that came out of the hole, and surface grading uses compacted soil sloped away from the house so roof and yard water never reaches the wall in the first place. Downspout extensions are part of the same system - a downspout dumping at the foundation corner can deliver more water to one spot than the drain was designed to handle. Older Detroit foundations add their own complications. Early-1900s block and poured walls may have deteriorated mortar joints, honeycombed concrete, or no exterior damp-proofing at all, and decades of freeze-thaw have widened cracks that now admit water freely. The engineer distinguishes waterproofing problems from structural problems before designing: a wall that is merely leaking gets drainage and sealing, while a wall that is bowing inward under lateral soil pressure gets a structural repair - wall anchors, steel bracing, or rebuild - before or alongside the waterproofing. Treating a structural wall as a waterproofing problem wastes the owner's money and leaves the real hazard in place.

What else do project teams ask?

Is interior or exterior waterproofing better for a Detroit basement?
Exterior waterproofing is the more complete solution because it stops water before it reaches the wall, with a membrane and footing drain on the outside. Interior drainage is less disruptive and less expensive, and it effectively manages water that does get in - but it does not waterproof the wall itself. The right choice depends on lot access, soil conditions, and whether the wall has structural distress; an engineer evaluates the site before recommending one.
Does a sump pump need a backup system?
Yes, in Detroit's storm climate a backup is strongly recommended. Power outages often arrive with the same storms that produce the heaviest inflow, so the design typically includes a battery-powered backup pump or a water-powered backup tied to the municipal supply, plus a high-water alarm. Without backup, the system is unprotected exactly when it is needed most.
What permits does basement waterproofing need in Detroit?
Within the city of Detroit, waterproofing work that alters drainage, plumbing, or the structure generally requires a permit through the Buildings, Safety Engineering and Environmental Department, with inspections of the drain and sump installation. In unincorporated parts of Wayne County, the county handles permitting with a different submittal path and review timeline. The authority having jurisdiction decides what is approved, so the engineer confirms the permit requirements before work begins.
How does freeze-thaw damage basement walls?
Water trapped in small cracks and porous concrete expands when it freezes, wedging the crack wider with each winter cycle. Over decades this turns hairline cracks into leaking ones and can spall the face of block walls. Good drainage reduces the water available to freeze, and sealing cracks before winter slows the cycle - which is why waterproofing and crack repair are usually done together.
