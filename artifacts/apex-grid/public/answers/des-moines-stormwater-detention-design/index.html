How Is Stormwater Detention Designed for Des Moines Developments?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Stormwater detention for Des Moines developments is designed by calculating the site's pre- and post-development runoff, then sizing a pond, underground vault, or chamber system that releases water no faster than pre-development rates, with outlet controls engineered for design storms and plans reviewed through the city's stormwater permitting process.

Hydrologic Modeling and Storage Sizing

The sizing analysis begins with the watershed. The engineer maps drainage boundaries from survey data, breaks the site into subcatchments by surface type — roof, pavement, lawn, existing field — and assigns runoff parameters to each. Pre-development conditions are modeled as the baseline the design must match; post-development conditions reflect the new impervious area. Design storms from the city's manual, with their prescribed rainfall depths and distributions, are run through both models to produce inflow hydrographs. Storage volume comes from the difference between those hydrographs. The engineer selects a basin footprint and depth that fit the site, then iteratively routes storms through the proposed storage with the outlet controls in place, adjusting until peak outflows meet the release-rate limits for every required storm event. Freeboard above the maximum water surface, sediment storage volume, and maintenance access are all part of the final grading plan. The calculations are stamped and submitted as the engineering basis the reviewer checks — a detention design without traceable hydrology does not get approved.

Outlet Structures and Water Quality

The outlet structure meters the basin. A typical design stacks functions vertically: a low-flow orifice near the basin bottom drains the water-quality volume slowly over a day or more, a weir or riser at a higher elevation controls the larger design storms, and an emergency spillway at the top passes the extreme event if everything else is overwhelmed. Each element is sized with hydraulic equations for its flow regime, and the engineer details trash racks and anti-seep collars so the structure performs for decades without clogging or piping failures. Water-quality treatment rides along with quantity control. A sediment forebay at the inlet drops out coarse sediment before it reaches the main basin, and many Des Moines designs include a permanent pool or extended-detention volume that holds the first flush long enough for pollutants to settle. The engineer sizes these features to the city's water-quality storm and details outlet protection — riprap or energy dissipators — where concentrated flow leaves the structure, preventing the erosion the whole system is meant to stop. Maintenance provisions, from access ramps to sediment removal depth markers, are drawn into the plans because a basin that cannot be maintained eventually fails its purpose.

City Review and Construction Certification

Inside Des Moines, the stormwater submittal goes through the city's development review process with civil plans, hydrologic and hydraulic calculations, an operations and maintenance plan, and often a stormwater management agreement recorded against the property. Reviewers check the modeling inputs, verify release rates against the manual, and confirm the basin fits within easements and setbacks. Review timelines run on the city's development calendar, and resubmittals address reviewer comments before approval. In unincorporated Polk County or adjacent cities, the technical design is the same but the administrative path changes: different manuals, different portals, different fee schedules, and different inspection staff. The engineer identifies the authority having jurisdiction before modeling begins. After construction, most jurisdictions require an as-built survey of the basin and outlet plus an engineer's certification that the facility was built per the approved plans — the permit is not truly closed until that certification is accepted.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between detention and retention?
Detention temporarily stores stormwater and releases it at a controlled rate through an outlet structure — the basin drains dry between storms. Retention holds a permanent pool of water and releases only overflows. In the Des Moines area, detention is more common for meeting peak-flow limits, while retention or extended detention adds water-quality treatment by holding the first flush longer.
Why can't Des Moines developments use infiltration instead of detention?
Infiltration works only where soils drain well. Much of the Des Moines area has clay-heavy soils with slow infiltration rates, so an infiltration basin would stay full and fail. The engineer tests or references soil data during design; where infiltration is infeasible, detention with a controlled outlet is the engineered solution that still protects downstream properties.
Who maintains a detention basin after construction?
The property owner, typically under a recorded maintenance agreement required at permit approval. The design includes an operations and maintenance plan covering sediment removal, outlet inspection, mowing, and embankment checks. The city or county can enforce the agreement if the basin is neglected, because a silted-up basin no longer controls runoff.
Does the review differ inside Des Moines versus unincorporated Polk County?
Yes. Des Moines reviews detention under its own stormwater manual and development process, while unincorporated Polk County and neighboring cities apply their own standards, portals, fees, and timelines. The hydrologic engineering is equivalent, but the submittal package must match the reviewing jurisdiction's manual — the engineer confirms the authority having jurisdiction before starting the modeling.
