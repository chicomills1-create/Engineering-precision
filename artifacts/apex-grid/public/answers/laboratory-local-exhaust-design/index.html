How Is Local Exhaust Ventilation Designed for Laboratories?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers match capture devices to each operation, size exhaust for rated face velocity with proper ductwork, provide balancing makeup air, and discharge stacks to prevent re-entrainment into the building.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Capture Devices and Face Velocity Design

Device selection follows the operation. Fume hoods handle open chemical work with their enclosed sash opening; snorkel or elephant-trunk arms capture at a point source like a small reaction or instrument exhaust; canopy hoods collect hot, buoyant plumes rising from ovens or baths; slot hoods serve long, low sources like parts-cleaning tanks. The engineer sizes each for capture velocity at the contaminant release point — enough to overcome room air currents and the thermal buoyancy of the plume, documented from the hood listing and good-practice guidance. Face velocity is the operating metric for hoods, and the design holds it across sash positions. Constant-volume hoods are simplest but waste energy; variable-air-volume hoods modulate exhaust to maintain face velocity as the sash moves, with a minimum flow for containment. The engineer shows the sash working height on the drawings, specifies the velocity range, and requires an airflow monitor with audible alarm on every hood so users know immediately if containment is lost.

Ductwork, Stack Discharge, and Re-entrainment

Laboratory exhaust ductwork is engineered for what it carries: corrosive-resistant materials where acids are exhausted, welded or sealed construction, proper slope and drainage for condensate, and fire and smoke considerations where flammables are involved. The engineer routes ductwork to minimize pressure loss while keeping runs accessible for inspection, and sizes fans for the worst-case filter loading so performance does not decay between maintenance. The stack is where many designs fail. Exhaust must discharge with enough momentum to clear the roof's recirculation zone — typically well above the roofline with a minimum discharge velocity — and located so the wind cannot carry it into the building's own intakes or operable windows. The engineer evaluates intake and stack locations together, considering prevailing winds and adjacent taller structures, because re-entrainment turns the building's ventilation system into a contaminant distribution system.

Laboratory Local Exhaust Design Checklist

A complete local exhaust design captures contaminants and keeps them captured: • Capture device matched to each operation with documented capture velocity • Fume hoods sized for rated face velocity at the sash working position • VAV or bypass controls that hold velocity as sash position changes • Airflow monitors with audible alarms on every hood • Corrosion-appropriate duct materials with drainage and inspection access • Makeup air balanced to total exhaust so hoods achieve rated flow • Stack height, velocity, and location designed to prevent re-entrainment

What else do project teams ask?

What face velocity should a laboratory fume hood maintain?
The design target is set from the hood's listing and the nature of the work — commonly in the range that balances containment against turbulence, with the sash at its designated working height. Too low and contaminants escape; too high and the airflow itself becomes turbulent and spills contaminants out. The engineer specifies the range, the controls hold it as the sash moves, and commissioning measures it at multiple sash positions with the room in its normal operating state.
Why do fume hoods need dedicated makeup air?
A hood can only exhaust the air the room provides. If the building does not supply matching makeup air, the lab goes negative, doors become hard to open, and the hood's face velocity drops below its rated value — silently. Worse, the negative pressure can pull air (and contaminants) from adjacent spaces. The engineer balances total exhaust against dedicated makeup air, often with a slight offset to hold the lab negative to the corridor, and proves the balance at commissioning.
What is re-entrainment and how is it prevented?
Re-entrainment is exhausted contaminants being drawn back into the building through air intakes, operable windows, or doors — the exhaust stack discharges, the wind carries the plume, and the building inhales its own waste. Prevention is stack height and discharge velocity that clear the roof's aerodynamic wake, separation distance from intakes, and discharge location upwind of intakes relative to prevailing winds. The engineer evaluates all three together because a tall stack in the wrong location still fails.
Can a snorkel arm replace a fume hood?
Only for the right operations. Snorkel arms capture contaminants at a point source — an instrument vent, a small heated process — and work well when the source is fixed and the arm can sit close to it. They do not provide the enclosure, sash protection, or spill containment of a fume hood, and they are easily defeated if the source moves or room air currents are strong. The engineer selects snorkels for true point sources and hoods for open chemical handling, never substituting one for the other on cost alone.
