What Are the MEP Design Requirements for a Brewery?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A brewery is designed around process steam or hot water plus glycol fermentation cooling, sloped chemical-resistant floors with trench drainage and wastewater pretreatment, CO2 monitoring in fermentation areas, and a tasting room separated from production by construction and HVAC zoning.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Process utilities: steam, glycol, and water

I build the utility design from the production plan: batch size, batches per week, fermentation schedule, and packaging. The heat source — steam boiler or hot-water plant — is sized for the peak brewhouse demand with recovery that matches the brewing rhythm, and the steam or hot-water distribution is insulated, properly trapped and drained, and controlled for the repeatable temperatures brewing demands. The glycol chiller and loop are sized for the worst-case fermentation load, with N+1 or backup provisions discussed honestly against the cost of losing a tank to a chiller failure. Water gets the same process treatment. Brewing liquor is treated to the recipe's mineral profile; cleaning and cooling water is metered separately; and backflow protection isolates the process from the potable system at every connection. Compressed air for process use is oil-free and dried, and CO2 for carbonation and purging is distributed from bulk or cylinder storage with the ventilation and monitoring the fire marshal requires. Every utility is designed with maintenance access and isolation valves placed where the brewer can actually reach them — a process plant that cannot be serviced becomes a process plant that fails.

Floor drainage and wastewater

Brewery floors are a safety and sanitation system. I slope all process and washdown areas to trench drains sized for the flow — washdown hoses, tank cleaning, spills — with grates that handle foot and forklift traffic and materials that survive caustic cleaners and thermal shock. The drainage network separates process wastewater from sanitary and storm flows, and the process side runs through the pretreatment train: solids screening, pH adjustment, and the sampling manhole the discharge permit requires. Flooring material selection is part of the drainage design: chemical-resistant, slip-resistant, coved at walls, and detailed so thermal cycling from hot washdown does not delaminate it. I coordinate the floor, drain, and equipment layouts so every tank, the brewhouse, and the packaging line drain to the right place — and I verify the sewer authority's limits before the plumbing design is finalized, because the pretreatment design follows the permit, not the other way around.

CO2 safety and tasting-room separation

The CO2 safety design covers every space where fermentation gas can accumulate: cellars, brite-tank areas, walk-in coolers, and low or enclosed process spaces. I specify detection at the low mounting height where CO2 pools, with alarms that are audible and visible in the space and ventilation interlocks that clear the area automatically. The design also addresses the bulk CO2 storage and distribution — secured cylinders or bulk tanks, proper ventilation, and the seismic and fire-marshal provisions for compressed gases. The tasting room is designed as a separate world: its own HVAC zone with hospitality-grade comfort and ventilation, acoustic separation from the production floor, and finishes that belong in a customer space. The separation is physical — walls, doors, and pressure relationships that keep production humidity, noise, and CO2 out — and regulatory, since the tasting room's liquor licensing, health-department food service, and occupancy requirements differ from the production side. I design the boundary once, carefully, because reworking it after the health department or liquor authority reviews the plans is expensive.

What else do project teams ask?

Does a brewery need steam or is hot water enough?
It depends on the brewhouse size and design. Smaller brewhouses often run well on high-temperature hot water, which is simpler and safer to operate; larger kettles and direct-fire-equivalent performance usually call for a steam boiler. I size the heat source from the kettle volume, the brew schedule, and the peak demand — heating strike water while the kettle is firing, for example — plus growth headroom. Either way, the distribution is designed as a food-grade system with proper insulation, condensate handling for steam, and controls that give the brewer repeatable temperatures batch after batch.
How does the glycol system work in a brewery?
A central chiller cools a loop of food-grade glycol, which circulates to jackets on each fermenter and brite tank; individual control valves on each tank hold it at its fermentation temperature. I size the chiller for the peak load — the most tanks crashing or fermenting simultaneously on the hottest day — plus the cold-liquor and process loads, and I design the piping with the insulation and vapor barriers that prevent condensation in a humid cellar. Glycol temperature control is what makes the beer consistent from batch to batch, so the design treats it as a process-critical system with monitoring and alarming, not just another HVAC loop.
Why is CO2 monitoring required in breweries?
Because fermentation releases large volumes of carbon dioxide, which is odorless, heavier than air, and displaces oxygen — a silent asphyxiation hazard in cellars, walk-in coolers, and any low or enclosed space. I design CO2 detection with audible and visual alarms plus ventilation interlocks in every area where CO2 can accumulate, with sensors mounted low where the gas pools. Staff training and confined-space procedures complete the safety picture, but the engineering comes first: monitored, ventilated spaces where the hazard is designed out as far as possible.
What wastewater permits does a brewery need?
Typically an industrial wastewater discharge permit from the local sewer authority, covering pH, temperature, solids, and organic loading — brewery effluent is high-strength, with spent grain, yeast, and caustic cleaners. I design the pretreatment to the permit's limits: screening for solids, pH adjustment, and sometimes flow equalization, with a sampling point the authority can access. The permit application runs alongside design, not after it, because the pretreatment equipment needs space, drainage, and utility connections that are cheapest to provide on paper. High-strength surcharges are real money, so the design also looks at reducing load at the source.
