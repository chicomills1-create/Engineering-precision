How Does the Edwards Aquifer Shape San Antonio Foundations?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

The Edwards Aquifer shapes San Antonio foundations because building over its recharge and contributing zones means karst limestone with voids and sinkhole risk, triggering TCEQ Edwards Aquifer protection plan requirements, deeper geotechnical investigation, and foundations — often drilled piers socketed into sound rock — designed around voids and groundwater rules.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Investigating Karst Before Design

You cannot design a foundation on karst with a standard boring grid and hope for the best. The engineer starts with a geologic review of the site's position relative to known faulting and sinkhole features, then specifies an investigation program scaled to the risk: borings extended into rock, rock coring with recovery and rock-quality measurements, and close boring spacing under the building footprint. Where the borings suggest voids or highly variable rock, geophysical methods such as electrical resistivity can map anomalies between borings and guide supplemental drilling. The goal is a three-dimensional understanding of where the competent rock actually is. The engineer then makes the bearing decision from that picture. Sound limestone with good recovery gets drilled piers socketed a specified depth into rock, with the socket length designed from the rock's measured quality rather than assumed values. Zones of broken or voided rock are either bridged — piers placed to verified rock on either side — or avoided by shifting the building footprint where the site allows. The specifications require the drilling contractor to log the rock encountered in every shaft and to notify the engineer of unexpected voids, because the final pier depths are confirmed in the field, not just on paper.

Building Under Aquifer Protection Rules

The TCEQ Edwards Aquifer protection plan shapes construction methods, not just paperwork. Over the recharge zone, the plan restricts how deep excavations and piers can go relative to the water table, requires controls on concrete washout and drilling fluids, and mandates permanent best management practices for stormwater quality. The engineer details pier installation methods — casing through the upper soils, controlled concrete placement — so the foundation work itself cannot open a conduit for surface contaminants into the aquifer. Dewatering, if needed, is designed with discharge controls that satisfy the plan. Coordination between the structural and environmental work happens early, because the two can conflict. A pier layout optimized purely for structure might call for depths or locations the protection plan restricts; a stormwater basin placed for water quality might sit where the geotechnical engineer least wants a concentrated infiltration source over karst. The engineer resolves these tensions in design, producing a foundation that bears safely on verified rock and a site plan that keeps the aquifer protection intact. Both the TCEQ and the local authority having jurisdiction must approve their respective pieces.

What else do project teams ask?

What is karst and why does it complicate foundations?
Karst is limestone terrain dissolved by slightly acidic groundwater into voids, channels, sinkholes, and irregular rock surfaces. It complicates foundations because the ground's bearing capacity can change dramatically over a few feet — a boring can show solid rock while a void hides nearby. San Antonio's Edwards limestone is karstic, so foundations over the recharge zone need deeper investigation and designs that bear on verified sound rock or bridge the weak zones.
What is a TCEQ Edwards Aquifer protection plan?
It is the environmental compliance plan the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality requires for regulated development over the Edwards Aquifer recharge zone. It addresses construction-phase pollution prevention, permanent water-quality controls, and restrictions on excavation and activities that could affect the aquifer. Foundation design and installation methods must comply with it alongside the structural code.
Why are drilled piers common over the recharge zone?
Drilled piers can be extended through the unreliable upper karst — voids, soil-filled sinkholes, fractured rock — and socketed into competent limestone verified by coring. This bypasses the zone where bearing is uncertain and puts the building's loads on rock the engineer has actually confirmed. Each shaft is field-logged during drilling so unexpected voids are caught before concrete is placed.
Do city and county permitting differ over the aquifer zones?
Yes. Inside San Antonio, the city's development services reviews the project while the TCEQ reviews the aquifer protection plan; in unincorporated Bexar County, county permitting runs alongside the TCEQ submittal with its own portal and schedule. The recharge, contributing, and transition zones each carry different TCEQ requirements, and a site can straddle them — so the engineer maps every boundary and confirms each authority having jurisdiction before design starts.
