How Should K-12 Classroom HVAC and Ventilation Be Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

The concise answer

K-12 classroom HVAC design provides ventilation, heating, and cooling for learning spaces per mechanical code and energy code. The design provides the required outside air per occupant, controls CO2 through demand-controlled ventilation, keeps equipment noise low enough for teaching, maintains steady temperatures through occupancy swings, filters air for health, and meets the energy code — in California, the 2025 California Energy Code / 2025 Standards, effective January 1, 2026 — with systems the district can afford to run and maintain. Three goals, no tradeoffs allowed: healthy air, quiet rooms, and affordable operation. Cheap systems deliver at most one. I design for all three by spending the budget where it matters — ventilation effectiveness, acoustic distribution, and efficient equipment — instead of spreading it thin across features nobody asked for.

What good classroom air requires

Ventilation effectiveness comes first: outside air delivered to the breathing zone, not short-circuiting from supply to return across the ceiling. Displacement or well-designed mixed systems with CO2-based demand control keep air fresh when rooms are full and save energy when they're empty. Filtration at MERV-13 or better is now the expectation — it caught on for good reasons — which means the air handlers have to be selected for the added fan energy, not just fitted with better filters and hoped for the best. Thermal control has to handle the school day's swings: a classroom empty at 7 a.m., full by 8, baking in afternoon sun on the west side. Zoning per classroom or per small group of rooms, with controls teachers can actually understand, beats one big system serving a whole wing. And maintainability is a design criterion, not an afterthought — filters accessible without a ladder truck, equipment a district tech can service, controls with remote monitoring so problems get caught before parents notice. The best school HVAC is the kind nobody thinks about for twenty years.

What can change the scope and cost

New construction vs. modernization is the biggest fork — retrofitting ventilation and quiet into existing classrooms is harder than designing it in. Special spaces (labs, shops, kitchens, gyms) each need their own systems. And district standards often dictate equipment types. Match the system to the district's maintenance reality; the fanciest system fails if nobody can service it.

  • New vs. retrofit: existing buildings constrain equipment locations, ductwork, and electrical capacity
  • Special spaces: science labs, shops, and kitchens need dedicated exhaust and ventilation
  • District standards: many districts mandate specific equipment for parts and training commonality
  • Climate: hot climates need serious cooling; cold climates need heating and freeze protection
  • Funding source: bond and state-funded projects carry procurement and review requirements

What else do project teams ask?

How much outside air does a classroom need?
The mechanical code sets it by occupancy — roughly 10 CFM per person plus a per-square-foot component, which for a classroom of 30 works out to several hundred CFM of outside air. But the smarter target is CO2: keeping it near 1,000 ppm or below, which research ties to better cognitive performance. Demand-controlled ventilation uses CO2 sensors to deliver full outside air when the room is full and back off when it's empty — saving energy without starving occupied rooms. Our designs target the learning outcome, not just the code minimum.
Why is classroom HVAC so noisy?
Usually because the equipment sits right at the classroom — unit ventilators under windows, rooftop units directly overhead — with minimal acoustic treatment, selected for first cost. Teaching spaces want background noise around NC-35 or lower. The fixes are design decisions: remote equipment with ducted distribution, low-velocity ductwork, lined ducts, and vibration isolation. A classroom where the teacher shouts over the HVAC is a classroom where learning loses. We design the distribution for quiet from the start because retrofitting quiet is brutally expensive.
Does this guarantee permit approval?
No. Engineering documents support a defined project scope, while the authority having jurisdiction controls its interpretation, completeness decision, review queue, and approval.
What should I send for an initial review?
Send the project address, plain-language scope, current drawings, existing-condition records, relevant calculations or comments, schedule, and the authority or code information already available. The responsible engineer will identify gaps.
