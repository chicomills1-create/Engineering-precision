What Does a PE Check Before Sealing MEP Drawings in Henderson, NV?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Before sealing MEP drawings in Henderson, the PE verifies HVAC loads and equipment against desert design conditions, checks plumbing risers and fixture compliance, confirms the electrical distribution agrees end to end, and coordinates the MEP layouts with the structure.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The pre-seal verification pass

The PE's verification runs discipline by discipline. Mechanical starts with the load calculations: Henderson-appropriate outdoor design conditions, realistic internal gains for the occupancy, and ventilation per code with the outside-air load fully accounted for — in this climate, ventilation air is a major cooling load, not a footnote. Equipment gets matched to the loads at peak and part-load, with controls sequences checked for design intent. Plumbing runs from fixture layouts through the risers: counts per occupancy, pipe sizing and slopes, venting, water heating, and specialty systems like grease waste detailed to code. Electrical runs from the service calculation through the riser to the panel schedules: loads, breaker and feeder sizing, fault current, and overcurrent coordination all in agreement. Every discrepancy becomes a marked revision, and the PE re-checks the corrections before sealing.

Cooling-dominated design, done right

In Henderson, the mechanical design is the building's largest energy story, and the PE's review treats it that way. I check that the load calculations reflect the envelope actually on the architectural drawings — glazing areas, shading, and orientation matter enormously when the sun is this strong — and that internal gains reflect the real occupancy and equipment. Equipment selection gets verified against the loads with attention to part-load efficiency, because Henderson buildings spend most of their hours at part-load even though they're designed for the peak. Controls sequences get read for intent: economizer logic, ventilation control, and staging that actually delivers the efficiency the equipment is capable of. The sealed drawings need to show a system engineered for the desert, not a mild-climate design with bigger units.

Henderson MEP pre-seal checklist

An MEP set is ready for the PE's verification pass — and then for the city — when these items are resolved. Heat-driven mechanical design and internal coordination are the themes. • HVAC load calculations with southern Nevada design conditions and ventilation fully accounted for • Equipment schedules matching the mechanical plans at peak and part-load performance • Plumbing risers, fixture counts, and water heating coordinated and code-compliant • Electrical riser, panel schedules, and service calculation in full agreement • Controls sequences that deliver the design intent, not just equipment schedules • MEP layouts coordinated with structural framing and architectural backgrounds

What else do project teams ask?

Why is HVAC sizing the centerpiece of a Henderson MEP review?
Because the design problem is extreme and unforgiving. Henderson summers run brutally hot, so the cooling load is enormous and equipment that is even slightly undersized will run flat-out and still lose the space on the worst afternoons. Oversizing isn't the answer either — oversized equipment short-cycles, wastes energy, and struggles with humidity control during monsoon spikes. The PE verifies that the load calculations used appropriate southern Nevada design conditions, realistic internal gains, and correct ventilation quantities, then checks the equipment selections against both full-load and part-load performance. Getting this right before the seal goes on is the difference between a building that works in August and one that generates service calls.
What should I send a PE to start an MEP review-and-seal in Henderson?
Send the complete MEP drawing set — mechanical, plumbing, and electrical plans — plus the HVAC load calculations, equipment cuts or schedules, the architectural backgrounds, and any plumbing or electrical calculations already prepared. For TI or remodel work, include as-built or field-verified information on the existing systems. I also want the project narrative: occupancy, operating hours, and any specialty systems like commercial kitchens. A review can only be as good as its inputs, and Nevada's responsible-charge standard means gaps get filled before the seal goes on, not after — so complete inputs up front are what make a predictable review timeline possible.
What do PE review and seal services cost for a Henderson MEP project?
Cost follows scope and complexity, not a flat fee. A straightforward TI with a clean starting package costs far less than a ground-up commercial building — the review hours scale with the number of systems, the coordination complexity, and how much revision the set needs. What I'd budget for is the engineering review itself plus at least one round of responding to the PE's markups; the city review cycles are separate. The expensive version of this engagement starts late, with an incomplete package and a permit deadline — rush conditions and redesign cost more than a planned review. Engage the PE early with complete drawings and the fee stays proportional to the actual engineering.
Does the plumbing review cover water conservation in Henderson?
Yes — fixture compliance and hot water design get checked against the adopted plumbing and energy codes, which in Nevada's desert reality carry real weight. I verify fixture flow rates and counts per code, check that hot water systems are sized for the actual demand rather than a template, and confirm recirculation and controls where the code requires them. On multifamily and hospitality work, the domestic hot water design gets particular attention because it's both a code item and an operating-cost item. Water-efficient design here isn't just good practice; it's the regulatory environment, and the sealed set has to show it.
