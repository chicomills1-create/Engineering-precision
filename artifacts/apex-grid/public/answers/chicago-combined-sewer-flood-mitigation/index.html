How Does Chicago Mitigate Combined-Sewer Basement Flooding?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Chicago mitigates combined-sewer basement flooding with overhead sewer conversions using ejector pumps, backwater valves on gravity lines, foundation-drain disconnection where allowed, and sump systems with battery backup - designed to the Illinois plumbing code and permitted through the Chicago Department of Buildings or the local municipality.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Why Combined Sewers Flood Basements

A combined sewer is sized for some design storm, and every storm beyond that uses the pipes as pressure conduits. When inflow exceeds capacity, the hydraulic grade line climbs, and any basement fixture or floor drain connected below that elevation becomes an outlet for the surcharged system. Basements flood not from water coming through walls but from sewage backing up through the plumbing, which is why the fix is plumbing separation rather than waterproofing alone. Building-level mitigation accepts the municipal system as it is. The engineering goal is to break the hydraulic connection between the surcharged sewer and the basement interior, so that when the street sewer pressurizes, the basement fixtures are isolated from it. Overhead sewers achieve this by lifting waste above the surcharge level; backwater valves achieve it by closing against reverse flow. Neither reduces the surcharge itself, which is why the engineer distinguishes between flood prevention at the building and flood control at the system level.

The Overhead Sewer Conversion

The conversion replumbs the basement. Existing basement fixtures are disconnected from the gravity building drain and rerouted to a sealed ejector pit set in the basement floor. Duplex pumps in the pit lift the waste through a discharge line that rises and ties into the building sewer at an elevation above the expected surcharge, with a check valve preventing drain-back. The engineer sizes pumps for peak fixture discharge, sets pit volume for pump cycling, and specifies alarms and alternating controls so a failed pump announces itself before the pit overflows. Remaining gravity connections get backwater valves: full-bore valves installed on the building drain that allow normal outward flow and slam shut against reverse flow from the street. Valve selection accounts for the waste stream, since valves in kitchen or laundry lines foul faster, and the design provides accessible cleanouts for the periodic cleaning the valves require. The converted system is tested and inspected before concealment, with the installation documented for the permit record.

Site Water Management Around the Building

Reducing inflow to the combined system helps the building and the neighborhood. Foundation drain tiles that currently discharge to the combined sewer are rerouted to a dedicated sump pit with its own pump discharging to grade or a dry well, where the municipality allows the disconnection. Downspouts are cut from the sewer connection and extended away from the foundation, and grading is reshaped to carry surface water away from the building rather than toward it. Power reliability is designed in, not assumed. The storms that surcharge sewers frequently knock out power, so ejector and sump pumps get battery backup systems sized for the expected outage duration or connections for standby generators. The engineer coordinates pump loads with the electrical service, since a conversion that trips the panel during a storm has solved nothing. Together, separation, drainage, and backup power form the complete mitigation package.

What else do project teams ask?

What is an overhead sewer?
A plumbing arrangement where basement fixtures drain to an ejector pit instead of directly to the street sewer, and pumps lift the waste up to discharge into the building sewer above the elevation that sewers reach when they surcharge. Because the discharge point sits above the flood level of the sewer system, surcharging cannot push sewage back into the basement.
Do backwater valves require maintenance?
Yes. The valve's flapper must move freely and seat fully to stop reverse flow, so it needs periodic inspection and cleaning, especially on lines carrying kitchen or laundry waste that can foul the mechanism. The design must provide accessible cleanouts; a valve that cannot be reached cannot be maintained and should not be installed.
Will an overhead sewer guarantee a dry basement?
No. An overhead sewer greatly reduces the risk of sewage backup through plumbing, which is the most destructive form of basement flooding, but it cannot stop water seeping through foundation walls and floors or entering through window wells. Foundation waterproofing and drainage address those paths separately, and no system is proof against every storm.
Do Chicago suburbs handle combined-sewer flooding differently?
Each municipality owns and maintains its local sewers, while the Metropolitan Water Reclamation District handles regional conveyance and treatment. Some suburbs mandate overhead sewers for new construction or offer cost-share programs for conversions; others rely on homeowner initiative. Unincorporated Cook County sites follow county requirements. The local authority having jurisdiction decides what is required and what is permitted.
