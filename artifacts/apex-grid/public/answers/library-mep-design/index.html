How Is MEP Designed for a Modern Library?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Modern library MEP zones the building by need: preservation-grade humidity control for the stacks, quiet HVAC and managed daylight for reading rooms, and flexible, occupancy-driven systems for community rooms and maker spaces.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Collection preservation: humidity and light

I start the preservation design from the collection: general stacks, special collections, and archives each get the control level their material warrants. General stacks get stable humidity control with good filtration; special collections and archives get tighter bands, often with redundant humidification or dedicated systems. The control sequences prioritize stability — modulating control, no aggressive setbacks in collection zones, and alarming on both drift and rate-of-change. Light in collection areas is a dose, and I design it as one: low illuminance, UV-filtered sources, and occupancy-based control so stack aisles are lit only when someone is in them. Compact shelving areas get lighting coordinated with the shelving operation — lights that follow the open aisle rather than lighting the whole range. For archives and rare materials, I design to the institution's exhibition and storage policies, and I make sure the monitoring — temperature, humidity, and light logging — gives the preservation staff the data they need to prove the environment is doing its job.

Quiet HVAC for reading spaces

Reading rooms, quiet study areas, and meeting rooms get the acoustic treatment in the HVAC design: equipment located away from the quiet zones, ductwork sized for low velocity with acoustic lining where needed, and diffusers and grilles selected for low noise generation. I review equipment submittals against the noise criterion the way I review them against capacity — a unit that moves the air but hums in the reading room fails. Vibration isolation completes the quiet design: isolated equipment, flexible duct and pipe connections, and careful routing so mechanical systems do not flank noise into the quiet zones through structure. The controls keep it quiet in operation too — no slamming dampers, no hunting valves, no equipment short-cycling through the night. Commissioning includes verifying background sound levels in the finished reading rooms with the systems running, because the design criterion is a measured result, not a calculation.

Community rooms and flexible systems

Community rooms, maker spaces, digital labs, and children's areas are where the library's program changes fastest, so I design their systems for change. Each flexible space gets its own HVAC zone with demand-controlled ventilation, lighting with simple preset scenes and daylight response, and power and data distribution that supports reconfiguration — the room that is a lecture hall tonight and a maker workshop tomorrow should not need an electrician between events. The plumbing and specialty systems follow the program: maker spaces may need compressed air, dust collection, or specialty exhaust; children's areas need durable, cleanable finishes and ventilation for high occupancy; meeting rooms with kitchenettes need the plumbing and exhaust their use demands. I keep the control interfaces simple and labeled for library staff — event mode, setback, and off — because the most flexible system in the world is useless if the staff cannot operate it confidently. Flexibility that requires an engineer to activate is not flexibility.

What else do project teams ask?

What humidity do library book stacks need?
Stable, moderate relative humidity — the exact band is set with the library's preservation guidance, but stability matters more than the precise number. I design stack zones with dedicated humidification and dehumidification, tight control bands, and sequences that avoid cycling, because it is the swings that cockle pages, stress bindings, and invite mold. Rare and special collections get a tighter, separately controlled zone. Monitoring and alarming on drift are part of the design — a slow humidity failure in the stacks can damage thousands of volumes before anyone notices.
How do you balance daylighting with protecting the books?
By putting the daylight where the people are and keeping it away from the collections. Reading rooms, lobbies, and community spaces get generous, glare-controlled daylight with UV management; stack areas get low, controlled electric light. I coordinate shading, glazing selection, and the lighting controls so the two strategies coexist — daylight-responsive dimming in the people zones, stable low light in the stacks. The floor plan and the MEP zoning follow the same logic: the preservation boundary is a real line in the building, and the systems respect it.
Why does library HVAC need to be quiet?
Because a library's product is concentration, and mechanical noise destroys it. Reading rooms, study areas, and meeting spaces get HVAC designed to low background-noise criteria — remote air-handling equipment, low duct velocities, acoustic treatment, and quiet diffusers selected for their sound output at design airflow. It costs more in ductwork and equipment selection than a standard commercial design, but a library where patrons hear the air handler is a library that failed its primary mission. I protect the noise budget through construction, because a late value-engineering change to smaller, noisier ductwork undoes the whole design.
How are library community rooms made flexible?
With independent zoning, occupancy-driven ventilation, and generous, reconfigurable power and data. I give each community and meeting room its own HVAC zone with CO2-based demand control so a full house and a small meeting each get the right air without wasting energy. Power and data are distributed for flexibility — floor boxes or perimeter raceways that support any furniture layout — and the lighting controls offer simple preset scenes. The goal is a room the staff can reconfigure for any event without calling an engineer, and systems that automatically match the actual use.
