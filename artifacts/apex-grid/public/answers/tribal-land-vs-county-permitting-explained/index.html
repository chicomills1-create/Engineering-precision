Tribal Land vs. County Permitting: Which Jurisdiction Governs Your Project?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Permitting jurisdiction on reservations follows land status: tribal trust land answers to the tribal government, allotments carry federal trust oversight, and fee land generally answers to the county or state. The allotment-era checkerboard means adjacent parcels can have different AHJs. Engineers confirm land status through title and BIA or tribal land records before design, then permit with each applicable authority.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Checkerboard: How Land Status Splits Jurisdiction

The checkerboard pattern means a single project corridor can cross three or four jurisdictional categories. Tribal trust land is land the United States holds in trust for the tribe; the tribe governs land use and building there as an exercise of sovereignty. Individual trust allotments are held in trust for individual Indian owners; the BIA oversees trust transactions, and the tribe retains governmental interests. Fee land is owned outright; when owned by non-members inside a reservation, state and county governments generally exercise zoning, building-code, and taxing authority. Tribal-member-owned fee land sits in a grayer area where tribal and state claims can overlap. This matters for every discipline. Civil site work on trust land needs tribal land-use authorization and possibly a BIA right-of-way or lease approval before grading begins. Structural design on fee land follows the county-adopted building code and county inspections. Environmental permits can split too: a tribe with federally recognized environmental authority, like the Navajo Nation EPA, administers programs on tribal lands, while the state administers them on fee land. The engineer's jurisdiction memo, prepared at project kickoff, lists each parcel, its status, and the AHJ for building, grading, utilities, and environmental review.

How Engineers Confirm the Right AHJ

Confirmation is a research task, not an assumption. Title reports show the ownership and trust status of each parcel. The BIA agency realty office maintains trust-land records and processes rights-of-way, leases, and easements on trust and allotted land. The tribe's land or planning department confirms tribal land-use requirements and whether the parcel needs a tribal business-site lease, homesite lease, or use authorization. The county assessor and planning department confirm whether they claim the parcel and what their zoning and building requirements would be. When categories straddle a project, sequencing matters. Federal trust approvals, such as BIA rights-of-way or lease consents, can take months and should start before final design, because alignment or building-placement changes late in the process are expensive. Tribal cultural-resource review should run in parallel with early civil design so survey findings can shape the site plan. County permits on fee-land portions proceed on the county's timeline. The project schedule reflects the slowest track, and the design accommodates every AHJ's requirements rather than optimizing for just one.

What else do project teams ask?

If my project is inside a reservation, do I automatically follow tribal code?
Not automatically. Jurisdiction follows land status, not the reservation boundary. Tribal trust land follows tribal authority, but fee land inside the reservation, particularly non-Indian-owned fee land, generally follows county and state codes. Allotments add federal trust oversight. The engineer confirms the status of each parcel through title and BIA or tribal land records before selecting the code and the permitting authority.
What is fee-to-trust conversion and why does it matter for permitting?
Fee-to-trust conversion is the federal process of taking fee land into trust status for a tribe or individual Indian, which moves the parcel under tribal and federal jurisdiction and out of county taxing and regulatory authority. Tribes pursue it to consolidate checkerboarded land bases. For a project, a parcel in conversion limbo needs careful handling, because the AHJ can change mid-project, so the team confirms the current recorded status and designs accordingly.
Who permits a project that straddles trust land and fee land?
Both authorities, each for its portion. The tribal government permits the trust-land portion and the county permits the fee-land portion, with federal trust approvals layered on where BIA action is required. The design generally satisfies the stricter of the overlapping requirements, and the approvals are sequenced so the longest federal or tribal review track does not surprise the schedule. A jurisdiction memo at kickoff keeps the tracks organized.
Can a tribe regulate building on non-member fee land inside the reservation?
Tribal authority over non-member fee land is limited and fact-specific, shaped by a complex body of case law. As a practical matter, counties typically assert zoning and building-code jurisdiction over non-Indian fee parcels, while tribes may still have interests in environmental protection, cultural resources, and activities affecting tribal health and welfare. Project teams coordinate with both and let the land-status analysis, not assumptions, determine the permit path.
