How Is Bridge Scour Protection Designed in Iowa?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Bridge scour protection in Iowa is designed by first computing the expected scour depth at piers and abutments for the design flood, then armoring the channel with riprap sized for the flow velocity, or driving foundations below the total scour elevation, with designs reviewed by the county engineer or the Iowa DOT.

Computing Total Scour Depth

The scour analysis begins with the hydraulics: the engineer builds a model of the stream reach from survey cross-sections, computes water surface profiles for the design flood, and extracts the velocities, depths, and flow distribution through the bridge opening. Long-term degradation is estimated from the channel's history and sediment characteristics. Contraction scour is computed from the ratio of the natural channel width to the constricted bridge opening — the tighter the squeeze, the faster the water and the deeper the scour. Local pier scour depends on pier shape, width, skew to the flow, and the bed material, with wide or skewed piers producing deeper holes. The engineer sums these components with the factors the accepted methods prescribe to get total scour at each pier and abutment, then adds a margin for uncertainty in the bed material data. Iowa's streams often carry sand beds that scour readily, and the engineer verifies the bed gradation with samples rather than assuming it. The superflood check — typically the 500-year event — confirms stability even when the design flood is exceeded: the bridge may sustain damage in such an event, but the foundations must not lose support. These computations, with the survey and the hydraulic model documented, form the engineering record the reviewer approves.

Riprap Armoring and Deep Foundations

Riprap is engineered stone armor, not dumped rock. The engineer sizes the stone by median weight for the design velocity at each location — higher velocities at contracted openings and around pier noses need heavier stone — and specifies the gradation band, layer thickness, and areal extent so the armor cannot be flanked or undermined. A geotextile or granular filter goes beneath the stone to keep the bed soil from washing out through the voids, and the armor is toed into the channel bed below the expected scour depth at its edges so the flood cannot get underneath it. Slopes are graded to stable angles before placement, and the engineer details the transition where the armor meets the natural channel. Where the computed scour exceeds what armoring can practically resist, the foundations go deep. Drilled shafts or driven piles are designed to carry the bridge loads from below the total scour elevation, with the scour depth treated as lost soil — providing no lateral support and no bearing — in the structural analysis. The engineer checks the exposed column length for buckling and lateral load under the scoured condition, and details the pier to tolerate the flow forces at the design flood. Many Iowa county bridges combine both strategies: deep foundations for the piers plus riprap at the abutments where approach embankments constrict the flow.

County and DOT Review Tracks

The review path follows the road system. Bridges on state and federal highways go through the Iowa DOT's bridge and hydraulics review under the DOT's design standards, with formal submittal stages and the DOT as the authority having jurisdiction. County bridges are the county engineer's responsibility: the county engineer sets the design requirements — commonly built on DOT standards adapted for county road loadings and budgets — reviews the hydraulic and scour analysis, and approves the plans. The engineering rigor is equivalent; the standards documents and the reviewers differ. A parallel floodplain track often applies. Where the bridge crosses a regulated floodway, the local floodplain administrator — city or county depending on location — requires a floodplain development permit with hydraulic analysis showing the project does not raise flood levels beyond the allowed threshold. The bridge engineer coordinates this with the scour design from the start, because the opening size that satisfies the floodplain no-rise requirement also sets the contraction scour the foundations must survive. Each authority decides its own approvals, and construction inspection verifies that the riprap gradation, placement, and foundation depths match the approved plans.

What else do project teams ask?

What is bridge scour?
Scour is the erosion of streambed material around bridge piers and abutments by flowing water. Flood flows accelerate through the bridge opening and form vortices around piers that dig holes in the bed; if the erosion reaches the foundation bearing soils, the bridge can settle or collapse. It is the most common cause of bridge failures, which is why the engineer computes scour depths and designs protection for the design flood.
How is riprap sized for scour protection?
By the flow velocity and depth at the protected location. The engineer computes the design velocity from the hydraulic model, selects a median stone weight that resists displacement at that velocity, and specifies the gradation, layer thickness, and extent of coverage. A filter layer goes underneath so bed soil cannot wash through the stone, and the armor edges are toed below expected scour depth.
What happens if scour is deeper than riprap can handle?
The engineer founds the bridge below the problem. Drilled shafts or piles are designed to carry all loads from beneath the total computed scour elevation, with the scoured soil assumed to provide no support. The exposed substructure is checked for the scoured condition, so the bridge remains stable even after the flood removes the bed material around it.
Who reviews bridge scour designs in Iowa?
It follows the road: the Iowa DOT reviews bridges on the state highway system under DOT standards, and the county engineer reviews county bridges under county requirements. Where the crossing is in a regulated floodway, the local floodplain administrator issues a separate floodplain permit. The engineer coordinates these tracks because the hydraulic opening affects both the floodplain analysis and the scour depths.
