How Are Dairy Manure Lagoons Engineered for Safe Storage?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Dairy lagoons are engineered as lined storage impoundments: volume for the full non-application season plus storm and freeboard, liner and embankment design from geotechnical data, pump-out infrastructure, and county-permitted setbacks and monitoring.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Storage Volume and the Water Budget

The storage calculation is unforgiving of optimism. The engineer builds the daily inflow from metered or estimated manure production per cow, adds parlor wash water, flush water, and any other process streams routed to the lagoon, and multiplies over the regulatory storage period — then adds precipitation directly on the lagoon surface, evaporation credits where the rules allow them, and the design-storm volume on top. Freeboard — the empty safety margin between the liquid surface and the embankment crest — is added last and never borrowed for storage. The engineer presents the budget in the permit set as a table the reviewer can audit, because the storage number is the first thing the county checks. The nutrient management plan is the lagoon's operating license. Storage volume means nothing without somewhere for the manure to go: the engineer cross-checks the stored volume against the application acreage, the crop nutrient uptake, the application windows, and the setbacks from waterways — the whole mass balance. If the herd grows, the plan must show the acreage or the export arrangement growing with it. The engineer designs the pump-out and application infrastructure — the pump station capacity, the dragline or tanker access, the field roads — so the plan's application schedule is physically achievable in the windows the weather allows.

Liners, Embankments, and Geotechnical Design

The liner is selected for the site and the rules. Compacted clay liners must meet a permeability standard verified by testing during construction; synthetic geomembranes (typically HDPE) need the subgrade preparation, panel layout, seaming, and testing the manufacturer's engineering requires; composite liners combine both where the regulations or the groundwater sensitivity demand it. The engineer details every penetration — inlet pipes, pump intakes, monitoring wells — with boots and seals, because a perfect liner with a leaking penetration is a leaking lagoon. Construction quality assurance, with seam testing and thickness verification, is specified and observed. Embankments get dam-grade attention. The engineer designs the interior and exterior slopes for stability under full and rapid-drawdown conditions, specifies compaction and materials, protects the crest and exterior from erosion, and sets the crest elevation above the design flood level with the freeboard intact. The geotechnical investigation — borings, permeability testing, groundwater levels — underpins the seepage analysis and the slope stability calculations. The engineer also designs the surface-water diversions that keep clean stormwater from running into the lagoon: every acre of clean runoff diverted is storage volume the farm doesn't have to build.

Dairy Lagoon Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the lagoon design is finalized: • Storage volume covering the full non-application period plus design-storm precipitation and regulatory freeboard • Inflow budget audited: manure, wash water, flush water, rainfall — with high-rainfall scenario tested • Liner system selected for site soils and regulations, with seams, penetrations, and CQA specified • Embankment slopes, compaction, and erosion protection designed from geotechnical investigation • Crest above design flood elevation; clean stormwater diverted away from the lagoon • Inlet, agitation, and pump-out infrastructure with all-weather access and power • Monitoring wells upgradient and downgradient where required, with the sampling plan documented • Setbacks from wells, waterways, and property lines met; nutrient plan balancing herd, storage, and acreage

What else do project teams ask?

How much freeboard does a manure lagoon need?
Typically two feet or more of empty safety margin between the maximum operating level and the embankment crest, as the regulations specify — and it is never available for storage. The engineer designs the operating levels, the pump-out triggers, and the storm response around protecting that freeboard, because a lagoon that overtops in a storm is an environmental violation with the evidence washing downstream. Freeboard markers the operator can read from the bank are part of the design.
Clay or synthetic liner — which is better for a lagoon?
Either can work when designed and built right; the site and the regulations decide. Compacted clay suits sites with suitable local clay and requires permeability testing during construction. HDPE geomembrane suits sites without good clay and gives a very low permeability, but demands meticulous subgrade preparation, seaming, and testing. The engineer selects based on the geotechnical investigation, the groundwater sensitivity, and what the county permit requires — and specifies construction quality assurance for whichever is chosen.
What happens if a dairy outgrows its lagoon?
It faces a compliance crisis: the storage budget no longer covers the non-application season, forcing the choice between illegal application, emergency pumping at ruinous cost, or cutting the herd. The engineer designs with growth headroom and ties the lagoon volume to the permitted herd size in the nutrient management plan, so growth triggers a plan amendment — and a storage check — before it becomes a violation. Expanding a lagoon is a new permit, not a weekend project.
Who inspects a dairy lagoon?
On unincorporated county land, typically the county environmental health or water-quality program under state regulations, sometimes with the regional water board involved. Inspections check freeboard, embankment condition, liner integrity indicators, monitoring-well data, and the records tying pump-outs to the nutrient management plan. The engineer designs the record-keeping touchpoints — staff gauges, pump logs, sampling ports — into the facility so compliance is a routine, not a scramble.
