How Is Holding Cell Ventilation Designed for Courthouses?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design detention-grade supply and exhaust with tamper-resistant, ligature-resistant air devices, code-required air change rates, negative pressure to corridors, and separately zoned temperature control.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Air Change Rates and Room Airflow Patterns

The ventilation calculation starts with occupancy: holding cells concentrate people in small volumes, so the per-person outdoor air requirement drives the design. The engineer applies the mechanical code's rates for the occupancy classification, adds exhaust for odor and contaminant control, and verifies that the air distribution actually reaches the breathing zone. A single ceiling diffuser dumping air that short-circuits to a nearby return does not ventilate the cell no matter what the schedule says. The preferred pattern puts supply and exhaust on opposite sides of the room or at different heights to create a sweeping flow across the occupied space. Transfer air from the corridor is avoided — each cell gets its own ducted supply and exhaust so air, odors, and sound do not travel between cells. For interior cell blocks with no outside walls, the engineer routes dedicated duct risers from the air handling system, keeping runs as short and direct as security construction allows.

Detention-Grade Devices and Duct Security

Everything the occupant can touch must survive the occupant. Supply diffusers, return grilles, and exhaust grilles in holding cells are fabricated from heavy-gauge steel with tamper-resistant screws, small free-area openings, and rounded or flush profiles with no ligature points. The engineer specifies devices listed or rated for detention use and details their anchorage into the cell's concrete or masonry — a strong grille on a weak wall is a weak system. Ductwork serving the cells is designed so it cannot become an escape or hiding path: ducts sized and routed to prevent human entry, security bars or grilles where larger ducts pass through secure partitions, and sealed, smooth interiors with no accessible dampers inside the secure envelope. All balancing dampers and controls live outside the cell in locked service corridors or above secure ceilings. The specification calls out these requirements explicitly, because standard commercial diffusers and exposed ductwork have no place in a holding environment.

Holding Cell Ventilation Checklist

A complete holding ventilation design delivers safe air through indestructible hardware: • Code-required outdoor air and exhaust rates calculated for peak cell occupancy • Supply/exhaust arranged for sweep airflow with no short-circuiting • Detention-grade tamper-resistant, ligature-resistant air devices throughout • Cells held negative to corridors with dedicated ducted supply and exhaust per cell • Separate temperature control zone managed by staff, not occupants • Ductwork secured against entry with no accessible dampers inside cells • Field-measured airflow at every grille verified during commissioning

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't standard commercial diffusers be used in holding cells?
Standard diffusers are light-gauge aluminum or plastic with removable cores — they can be pried off, broken into sharp pieces, or used as ligature anchor points. Detention-grade devices are heavy-gauge steel with tamper-resistant fasteners, tiny openings, and no removable parts an occupant can access. The cost difference is small compared to the liability of an injury or escape facilitated by the wrong grille.
Should holding cells be positive or negative to the corridor?
Negative. Exhausting slightly more air than is supplied keeps odors and airborne contaminants inside the holding area instead of drifting into corridors, courtrooms, and public spaces. The pressure relationship is verified with the doors closed during commissioning, and the design maintains it across the full range of system operation — not just at the test condition.
How is temperature controlled in holding cells?
Holding areas get their own HVAC zone with setpoints controlled by facility staff, since detainees cannot be given thermostat access and the loads differ sharply from courtrooms. The engineer designs for the dense occupancy and limited operating schedule — holding may be heavily used on arraignment days and empty otherwise — with controls that set back when unoccupied but recover before the next session.
Can ductwork become a security risk in holding areas?
Yes, which is why the design treats it as one. Ducts large enough for a person to enter are not routed through secure partitions without security grilles; balancing dampers and access doors are placed outside the secure envelope; and duct interiors are sealed smooth with no loose components. The engineer coordinates duct routing with the security consultant so the HVAC system never creates a path around the physical security.
