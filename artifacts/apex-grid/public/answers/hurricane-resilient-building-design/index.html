How Are Buildings Designed to Survive Hurricanes?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Hurricane-resilient buildings use a continuous roof-to-foundation load path with engineered connectors, impact-protected openings, wind-smart roof geometry, and elevation plus flood-resistant detailing where surge is a threat.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The continuous load path in detail

I build the load path on paper before anyone builds it in the field. Starting at the top: the roof covering is attached to the deck for the component-and-cladding pressures at its zone — and zones at ridges, eaves, and corners carry pressures multiples higher than the field of the roof, so the fastening schedule changes across the roof plane. The deck is nailed or screwed to the framing with a pattern I specify, not the framer's habit. Rafters or trusses connect to the wall top plate with rated hurricane ties sized for the uplift at that location, and I call out the specific connector model rather than writing a generic note. Down the wall, I continue the chain: top plate to studs, studs to bottom plate, bottom plate to the floor or foundation with anchor bolts or straps at spacing I calculate. Each floor level repeats the pattern. At the foundation, hold-downs and anchor bolts carry the accumulated overturning into the concrete. I produce a connector schedule the framer can actually build from, because a load path that exists only as a general note on the structural drawings does not exist at all.

Openings, debris, and the envelope

The building envelope is a pressure boundary, and I design it as one. Every window and glazed door in a wind-borne debris region gets impact-rated assemblies tested for both the large-missile impact and the cyclic pressure that follows — the impact test alone is not the whole story, because a window that survives the 2x4 but fails under the subsequent pressure cycles still breaches. I verify that the specified products carry the approvals the jurisdiction requires: Florida's product approval system is the strictest, and a product approved elsewhere may not satisfy a Florida building department. Garage doors get engineered as structural components. I specify doors tested for the project's wind pressures with reinforced tracks, and I detail the track anchorage into the surrounding wall — a rated door in an unrated opening is theater. Soffits, ridge vents, and gable vents are designed against blowout and water intrusion, because wind-driven rain entering through a failed vent destroys interiors even when the structure survives. After the envelope is designed, I walk the drawings looking for the opening the team forgot: the attic vent, the crawlspace access, the pet door. Wind finds them all.

Hurricane-resilient design checklist

A hurricane-resilient building is ready when the wind has no weak link to exploit and the water has nowhere to do structural harm. • Continuous load path documented connector by connector, from roof covering to foundation anchorage • Roof geometry reviewed for wind performance — hip preferred, gable ends engineered and braced where used • Every glazed opening protected with impact-rated assemblies or engineered shutters; garage and entry doors wind-rated • Component-and-cladding pressures applied by zone — corners, edges, and ridges fastened for their higher demands • In flood zones: elevation above the design flood level, breakaway walls below, utilities above, flood vents where required • Product approvals confirmed with the authority having jurisdiction — Florida approvals, Texas windstorm certification, or local equivalents as applicable

What else do project teams ask?

What is a continuous load path and why does it matter?
It is the unbroken chain of connections that carries wind forces from where they hit the building down to the ground: roof covering to roof deck, deck to walls, walls to floors, floors to foundation. Wind does not push a building over like a hand on a box — it tries to lift the roof off, rack the walls, and slide the whole structure. Every link in that chain must be designed for its share of the load. In post-hurricane damage surveys, the buildings that lose roofs almost always lost them at an under-designed connection, not because the lumber or steel was too weak.
Do I really need impact windows if I have shutters?
You need one or the other on every opening, properly engineered — they are alternative compliance paths, not redundant extras. Impact-rated glazing stays in the frame when struck and keeps the building envelope sealed without any action by the occupants. Shutters work only if they are installed before the storm, which means the owner must be present, able, and willing. I often recommend impact glazing for the openings people forget about — high windows, sidelites — and shutters where the owner wants them, but the design covers every opening either way.
Why are hip roofs better in hurricanes?
Aerodynamics. A hip roof slopes on all four sides, so wind flows over it with lower suction pressures and there is no tall gable-end wall for the wind to push against. Gable roofs can perform adequately, but only with engineered bracing of the gable end and careful detailing of the gable overhang — the exact details that get value-engineered out. When the architecture allows it, I recommend the hip; when it does not, I over-design the gable bracing and document it so the framer builds what was drawn.
How do you design for wind and flood at the same time?
By treating them as simultaneous demands on different parts of the building. The elevated living space gets the full wind design — load path, opening protection, roof geometry. Below the design flood elevation, everything is sacrificial or flood-resistant: breakaway walls that fail cleanly without taking the structure, flood vents that equalize water pressure, and no utilities or finished space. The foundation is designed for scour and wave action as well as the wind overturning from above. The floodplain ordinance sets the elevation; the engineering makes the building survive everything below it and everything above it.
