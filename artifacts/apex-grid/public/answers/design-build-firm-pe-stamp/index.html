What PE Stamp Support Do Design-Build Firms Need Across Five States?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Design-build firms need licensed PE review-and-seal capacity in every project state — one consistent sealing process run firm-wide, with the actual responsible-charge review performed by a PE licensed in each state under that state's board rules. Build it as a program, not a per-project scramble.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The five-state program architecture

I build the program around a standard engagement template the firm reuses everywhere: defined review phases, milestone deliverables, revision and re-review protocol, correction-round handling, seal-block standards per state, and fee structures by project type. The firm's project managers learn this template once and deploy it in Phoenix, Los Angeles, Houston, Las Vegas, and Miami alike. Consistency here is what lets a PM who has only run Texas projects step onto a Florida project and know exactly how the engineering review will work. Under the template, the licensing layer adapts per state. The Texas projects run under TBPELS with the firm's F-number on the seal blocks and Rule 137.33 responsible-charge documentation. California projects get the Title 24 reconciliation step and seismic review emphasis built into the milestones. Florida projects get the threshold analysis at setup — which scopes need sealing under the statutes — and the wind-provision review focus. Arizona and Nevada projects run the standard template with local AHJ playbooks. One process, five licensing configurations. The firm's leadership sees a single engineering program; each state board sees compliant, locally reviewed, properly sealed work.

Staffing the program: employed PEs vs. partners

The strategic question for a growing design-build firm is where the licensed engineers sit. Employed PEs give maximum program consistency — the same reviewers, the same standards, institutional knowledge that accumulates across projects — and they make the firm's engineering capability a marketable asset. The cost is the licensing footprint: each PE needs licensure in every state where they seal, which means managing comity applications, continuing education across states, and renewal calendars. For firms concentrated in two or three states, this is very manageable; for firms touching all five, it is a real administrative function. The partner model — vetted local PE firms under master agreements — trades some consistency for immediate coverage and local AHJ fluency. I recommend it for new markets and for surge capacity: the local partner brings jurisdiction knowledge the firm's employed PEs may lack, and the master agreement keeps the process consistent. Many firms end up hybrid: employed PEs in the core markets, partners in the expansion markets, with a planned transition as licenses land. Whichever model, the non-negotiable is genuine responsible charge — the sealing PE reviews the work for real. A program built on signature arrangements is a liability program, not an engineering program.

Design-build firm sealing checklist

Build the program once and every project in every state runs inside it. • Standard engagement template: review phases, milestones, revision protocol, correction handling, per-state seal blocks • Licensing map: every market covered by an employed PE or a vetted local partner, verified on the state board sites • Texas F-number confirmed on the firm's registration; comity applications filed well ahead of market entry • Fast-track sequencing: PE review phased with design (foundations → structure → MEP), phased submittals confirmed with each AHJ • Jurisdiction playbooks for every metro: submittal standards, amendments, correction patterns, deferred-submittal policy

What else do project teams ask?

Can a design-build firm seal with its own employed PEs?
Yes — and many established design-build firms do exactly this, employing licensed PEs who take responsible charge of the firm's engineering and seal the permit sets. The requirements do not relax because the PE is on payroll: the engineer must be licensed in the project state, must genuinely perform or directly supervise the engineering, and in Texas the firm itself needs the F-number registration. Firms expanding into new states need to get their PEs licensed there (comity takes time) or partner with local PEs until the licenses land. The employed-PE model is excellent for program consistency — the same engineers reviewing to the same standards on every project — once the licensing footprint covers the firm's markets.
How do we handle states where we have no licensed PE yet?
Partner with a local PE or engineering firm for review-and-seal on those projects while your own engineers pursue comity licensure in parallel. The local partner performs the genuine responsible-charge review — verifying calculations, requiring revisions, sealing — under a defined engagement; this is not a signature arrangement, it is real engineering. I structure these partnerships with clear scope boundaries and a transition plan: the local PE handles the immediate projects, your engineers get licensed, and the program consolidates as the footprint grows. Never let a project wait on a comity application — permit schedules do not pause for licensing boards.
Does design-build change what the AHJ requires sealed?
No. The building department's sealed-engineering requirements are identical regardless of delivery method — the same structural, MEP, and civil scopes need PE seals whether the project is design-bid-build or design-build. What changes is who arranges the seals: on design-build, the firm holds the design contract, so the firm owns the entire sealing scope with no architect's engineer to share it. I advise design-build firms to map the full sealing scope at project setup — every discipline, every delegated component — because the AHJ will hold the design-builder accountable for all of it. The delivery method concentrates responsibility; plan for that.
How do we keep sealing from bottlenecking fast-track design-build?
By making the PE's review run parallel to design instead of after it: engage the reviewing engineers at project start, review in phases tied to the fast-track sequence (foundations, then structure, then MEP), and use phased permit submittals where the AHJ allows them. The bottleneck is never the seal itself — it is the review the seal requires, and that review takes the time it takes. Firms that plan the review into the fast-track schedule get seals on time; firms that append "two weeks for stamping" to the end of design discover that a real review of a complex set takes longer. Budget review time honestly and sequence it early.
