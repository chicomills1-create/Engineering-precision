How Is Building in the Chattahoochee Floodplain Handled Near Atlanta?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Building in the Chattahoochee floodplain near Atlanta is handled by mapping the site against FEMA flood maps, elevating the lowest floor above the base flood elevation, using flood-resistant materials and breakaway walls below, proving no-rise in the floodway with hydraulic modeling, and securing floodplain permits through the city or county, and the AHJ makes the final call on every approval.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Floodway, Flood Fringe, and the No-Rise Rule

The floodway is the river's working channel during a flood, and the regulations treat it accordingly. Any proposed fill, structure, or grading inside the floodway must be supported by a hydraulic analysis, typically built with standard river modeling software, demonstrating zero increase in the base flood elevation at every cross-section, upstream and downstream. That no-rise certification is signed and sealed by the engineer, and reviewers check it closely, because a rise that floods a neighbor upstream is exactly what the rule exists to prevent. Most projects simply avoid the floodway entirely. The flood fringe offers more room but no free pass. Buildings here must have their lowest floor elevated to or above the base flood elevation shown on the map, and the engineer documents that elevation with a finished-construction elevation certificate the floodplain administrator keeps on file. Compensatory storage sometimes enters the picture: where fill displaces floodwater storage volume in the fringe, the design excavates an equal volume elsewhere on the site so the floodplain's capacity is unchanged. Freeboard, building a foot or two above the minimum, is the engineer's standard recommendation, since it buys protection against storms bigger than the mapped event and against the map itself being revised upward later.

Elevating and Floodproofing the Building

Elevation is the most reliable flood protection, and the structural design makes it real. Buildings rise on piers, columns, or stem walls engineered for flood loads: hydrostatic pressure, hydrodynamic drag from moving water, buoyancy trying to float the structure, and debris impact. Breakaway walls below the elevated floor are designed to fail cleanly under a defined flood load, sacrificing themselves to protect the main structure. The engineer sizes every element for the flood forces the code assigns, not just gravity and wind, and details connections so the elevated building acts as one unit when water moves against it. Below the elevated floor, flood vents do quiet but critical work. Sized by the enclosed area they serve, they open automatically as water rises, letting floodwater enter and exit so pressure equalizes on both faces of the walls. Without them, a few feet of water outside and dry air inside can push a foundation wall to failure. Materials in the flood zone are chosen for wetting: no standard drywall, no fiberglass batts that trap water, just assemblies that can be flooded, cleaned, and dried. Utilities get the elevation treatment too, with electrical panels, HVAC equipment, and water heaters placed above the design flood level, because replacing systems after every flood is not a design.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between the floodway and the flood fringe?
The floodway is the channel plus the adjacent land needed to discharge the base flood without raising water levels; development there is essentially prohibited unless the engineer proves no rise in flood elevations. The flood fringe is the remainder of the mapped floodplain, where building is allowed if the lowest floor is elevated above the base flood elevation and flood-resistant construction rules are followed. The map panel for the site shows which zone each portion falls in.
What does a no-rise certification require?
A licensed engineer builds a hydraulic model of the river reach with the project in place and demonstrates that the base flood elevation does not increase at any modeled cross-section. The analysis covers existing and proposed conditions, accounts for the cumulative effect of the encroachment, and is submitted with supporting calculations for the floodplain administrator's review. If the model shows any rise, the design must change until it shows none.
Can a building be dry-floodproofed instead of elevated?
For non-residential buildings, yes, where the code allows: walls are sealed, openings get removable shields, and the structure is engineered to resist hydrostatic and hydrodynamic flood loads. Residential buildings must be elevated; dry floodproofing is not permitted for homes under the national floodplain standards most Georgia jurisdictions adopt. Either way, the design must be certified by an engineer, and utilities are still elevated above the flood level.
Which agency permits floodplain work on the Chattahoochee?
The floodplain development permit comes from the local AHJ: the City of Atlanta, Cobb County, Fulton County, or Gwinnett County depending on which bank the site sits on. On top of that, the river corridor protections add a separate review layer for setbacks and land disturbance within the protected corridor. The building permit follows only after the floodplain approvals are in hand, and each jurisdiction runs its own submittal portal and timeline.
