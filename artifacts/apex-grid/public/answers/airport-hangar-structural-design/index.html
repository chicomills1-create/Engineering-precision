How Is an Airport Hangar Structurally Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A hangar is structurally designed as a clear-span frame with a heavily engineered door opening and header, wind-uplift resistance for the big open or closed doorway, integrated foam fire suppression support, and apron drainage with fuel-spill containment.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Long-span structure and door openings

I select the structural system from the clear-span requirement and the door type. Rigid steel frames handle most hangars efficiently; long-span trusses or arches serve the widest buildings. The frame design accounts for the asymmetry the door opening creates — the front wall is mostly opening, so lateral stability has to come from the roof diaphragm, the side walls, and braced frames placed where they do not block aircraft movement. The door foundation and support detailing gets the same care as the frame. Bottom-rolling door rails need a continuous, settlement-resistant foundation with drainage so water does not pond on the rail line; vertical-lift doors need tower or headroom structure designed for the raised leaf's dead load plus wind on the raised surface. I coordinate door power and controls with the electrical design — a hangar door that loses power mid-travel with an aircraft inside is an emergency, so the operating system gets reliable power and a manual or backup operating provision.

Wind loads and uplift

The wind analysis models the hangar in its real operating states: doors closed, doors fully open, and doors partially open, with wind from the governing directions. Internal pressure coefficients for the open-door case drive the uplift design, and I carry those loads through the complete load path — cladding fasteners to purlins, purlins to frames, frames to foundations. Every connection in that path is designed and detailed, because uplift failures are connection failures far more often than member failures. Serviceability matters alongside strength. Long-span frames deflect and drift under wind, and I check that the deflections stay within the door manufacturer's operating tolerances — a door that binds in a windstorm is a door that cannot close when it is needed most. Cladding selection balances wind performance with the maintenance reality of an airfield environment: corrosion-resistant systems with verified fastening schedules, installed per the approved details, not field-improvised.

Fire suppression and apron drainage

The fire-protection design starts with the hazard classification: what aircraft, what fueling operations, what storage. From there I coordinate the suppression system — foam deluge for the higher-hazard hangars, sprinklers elsewhere — with the structural design so piping, concentrate tanks, and discharge drainage all have their place. Foam-water discharge is a large, sudden flow, and the floor drainage and containment must handle it without flooding the hangar or sending foam to the storm system. Apron drainage outside the doors manages stormwater across large paved areas with minimal slope — aircraft pavements cannot have the grades a parking lot uses — so I design trench-drain networks with the hydraulic capacity for the design storm plus oil-water separation on the discharge. Fuel-farm and fueling areas get their own contained drainage. Throughout, I design to the airport authority's standards where they govern, since airfield drainage, fuel handling, and fire protection on airport property typically answer to the authority's engineering review as well as the applicable codes.

What else do project teams ask?

How wide can a hangar door opening be?
Hangar doors routinely span 80 to over 200 feet depending on the aircraft, with the practical limit set by the door type and the structure needed to support it. Bottom-rolling doors handle very wide openings well because each leaf carries its own weight on the rail; vertical-lift doors need the headroom and structure to raise the leaf. I start from the design aircraft — wingspan plus maneuvering clearance — and the door selection follows. The header and door-support structure for a wide opening is a major structural element, not an afterthought, and it is designed early so it does not collide with the mechanical and fire-protection systems.
Why is wind uplift a special concern for hangars?
Because a hangar is a large, lightweight structure with an enormous opening, which is the worst combination for wind. When the big doors are open, wind pressurizes the interior and pushes up on the roof from inside while suction pulls from outside — the net uplift can be far higher than on an enclosed building of the same size. I design the roof deck, its fastening, and the frame-to-foundation connections for that worst case, and I verify the analysis for both doors-open and doors-closed conditions. On an open airfield with full wind exposure, there is no margin for optimistic assumptions.
What fire suppression do aircraft hangars need?
It depends on the hangar size and what it houses. Larger hangars and those with fuel-servicing operations typically require foam-water deluge or similar special suppression designed for flammable-liquid fires, while smaller private hangars may be protected with standard sprinklers per the applicable NFPA guidance for the hazard. I coordinate the suppression design with the structure early — deluge piping, foam concentrate storage, and drainage for foam-water discharge all need space and structural support. The fire marshal or airport authority reviews the system, and its requirements shape the design from the start.
How does apron drainage handle fuel spills?
Apron and hangar-floor drainage is designed as a containment system, not just a stormwater system. Trench drains and sloped paving collect stormwater plus the drips, leaks, and washdown of aircraft operations, and oil-water separators treat the flow before it leaves the site — fuel and oil float and are captured while water passes. Inside the hangar, floor drains in fuel-handling areas connect to containment rather than the storm system. I design the drainage with the environmental rules in mind, because a fuel spill that reaches a waterway is a regulatory event, not just a cleanup.
