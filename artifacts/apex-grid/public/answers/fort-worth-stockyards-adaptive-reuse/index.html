How Are Fort Worth Stockyards Buildings Adaptively Reused?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Fort Worth Stockyards buildings are adaptively reused by assessing the historic brick and timber structures, adding concealed steel or concrete reinforcement for modern loads and codes, upgrading mechanical and electrical systems, and working through the city's historic preservation review for changes to protected facades.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Assessing Century-Old Structure

The engineer reads a Stockyards building like a medical history. Masonry walls are measured for thickness and surveyed for bulging, cracked, or spalled areas; mortar is tested or conservatively assumed to be weak lime mortar; timber framing is graded in place, with the engineer noting checks, splits, notches from long-removed equipment, and any rot at bearing points. Steel, where it exists, is checked for section loss from corrosion. Foundations are exposed at representative points to confirm type and depth. This survey produces the building's true structural biography, which is always more complicated — and more interesting — than the original drawings suggest, if drawings exist at all. The analysis then tests the building against its future. The engineer models the structure under the new occupancy's gravity loads, the code's wind loads, and the seismic demands, using material properties from testing or conservative historic defaults rather than modern values. The output is a deficiency list: the beam that cannot carry the new assembly load, the unanchored masonry wall that needs diaphragm ties, the foundation that is fine for a warehouse but not for a commercial kitchen's equipment. That list becomes the scope of the structural intervention — targeted, minimal, and designed to preserve as much historic fabric as possible.

Concealed Reinforcement and Systems Upgrades

The art of the intervention is invisibility. Steel moment frames slip inside masonry walls to provide the lateral system the unreinforced brick lacks; new concrete or steel beams hide above new ceilings to carry floors the old timber cannot; carbon-fiber wraps strengthen masonry piers without changing their dimensions. Floor diaphragms are stiffened from above before new flooring goes down, tying the whole building together so walls, floors, and roof act as one system. The engineer details every connection for the reality of old materials — through-bolts with large bearing plates on soft brick, for example — so the new strength does not destroy the old fabric it relies on. Meanwhile the building's systems are brought into the modern era. New HVAC must be threaded through timber framing without cutting the members the engineer is counting on; electrical service is upsized for commercial loads; fire sprinklers and alarms go in per the code for the new occupancy; and accessibility — entries, restrooms, paths of travel — is woven into the historic plan. The engineer and architect coordinate constantly, because a duct routed through a beam the structural design needs is a conflict that has to be resolved on paper, not in the field. The result, when it works, is a building that feels authentically 1910 and performs like it was built yesterday.

What else do project teams ask?

What makes adaptive reuse different from ordinary renovation?
Adaptive reuse changes a building's purpose — warehouse to restaurant, barn to event venue — which triggers a full structural re-evaluation for the new occupancy's loads plus current fire, accessibility, and energy codes. Ordinary renovation refreshes finishes within the same use. In a historic district, adaptive reuse also has to satisfy preservation standards that protect the building's character-defining features.
How do you strengthen old brick walls without changing how they look?
By working from the inside: steel frames or reinforced cores hidden within the wall thickness, diaphragm ties and collectors concealed above ceilings, and surface treatments like fiber-reinforced wraps only where they will not be seen. The exterior brick face is preserved or restored with matching materials, and any new exterior work goes through historic review.
What are the Secretary of the Interior's Standards?
They are the national guidelines for rehabilitating historic buildings, covering how to preserve character-defining features while adapting a building for continued use. Fort Worth's Historic and Cultural Landmarks Commission applies them to exterior changes in the Stockyards district. The engineer designs interventions to comply — minimal alteration, compatible materials, and reversibility where feasible.
Who approves a Stockyards adaptive reuse project?
Two tracks run together: Fort Worth's Historic and Cultural Landmarks Commission reviews exterior changes for historic compliance, and the city's development services department reviews the building permit for code compliance. The authority having jurisdiction issues the final approval. The engineer and architect sequence the landmarks review early so preservation feedback shapes the structural design before permit documents are finalized.
