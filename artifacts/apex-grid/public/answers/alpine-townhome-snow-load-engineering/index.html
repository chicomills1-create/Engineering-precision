How Is Snow Load Engineering Done for Alpine Townhomes?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Alpine townhome snow load engineering starts from the jurisdiction's adopted ground snow load, applies balanced, unbalanced, and drift loads at every roof step, valley, and party-wall parapet, details cold roofs and ice barriers against ice dams, and manages sliding snow so it never buries an entry or a neighboring unit.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Drift, Unbalanced, and Sliding Snow Cases

The engineer works the ASCE 7 snow provisions as a set of cases, not a single number. The balanced load covers the uniform snow the roof carries in calm conditions; the unbalanced load covers the wind-redistributed case on sloped roofs, with the windward slope stripped and the leeward slope surcharged; and the drift load covers the triangular piles against parapets, rooftop units, and roof steps. For townhomes, the roof-step drift is usually the governing case: the engineer computes the drift height from the upwind fetch of the higher roof and applies the surcharge to the lower roof's framing, decking, and connections. Parapet drifts get the same treatment along every party wall. Sliding snow from an upper roof onto a lower one is a separate impact the engineer does not ignore — the code's sliding load adds a surcharge to the lower roof, and the engineer sizes the lower framing for the combined drift-plus-slide case where the geometry creates it. Snow retention systems, where used, are engineered for the full sliding force with attachment details into the structure, not just the decking. The engineer documents every snow case on the structural plans so the plan reviewer and the framer see the same loading the design assumed.

Alpine Townhome Snow Engineering Checklist

Use this checklist before the structural set is issued: • Adopted ground snow load confirmed in writing with the authority having jurisdiction for the exact parcel • Site-specific snow study engaged where the elevation exceeds the code's mapped limits • Balanced, unbalanced, and drift loads computed for every roof plane, step, valley, and parapet • Sliding-snow surcharge applied where upper roofs shed onto lower roofs • Cold-roof detailing: full insulation, airtight ceiling plane, eave-to-ridge ventilation, ice barrier at eaves • Roof drains on low-slope areas detailed against freezing; overflow scuppers provided • Snow retention or managed shedding designed above entries, walkways, and neighboring units • Footings below the local frost line on the soils report's bearing values; differential settlement checked at party walls • Jurisdiction confirmed: town-adopted snow load amendments vs county adoption, and the plan-check submittal path for each

What else do project teams ask?

Why do neighboring mountain towns have different snow load requirements?
Because ground snow load is microclimate-driven. Elevation, storm track exposure, and local snowfall records vary dramatically across short distances in the mountains, so jurisdictions amend the mapped values with their own data — one Colorado county may adopt 70 psf while the town across the ridge adopts 100. The engineer uses the adopted value for the parcel's actual jurisdiction, confirmed before design, never an adjacent town's number.
What is a roof-step drift and why does it govern townhome design?
When wind blows snow off a higher roof section, it deposits in a triangular drift against the step where the roof drops to the lower section. That drift surcharge can double the local design load on the lower roof's framing. Townhomes with staggered units create these steps by design, so the engineer computes the drift at every step and sizes the lower roof — framing, deck, and connections — for the combined load.
How do you prevent ice dams on a townhome roof?
By keeping the roof deck cold: full-depth ceiling insulation, an airtight air barrier so warm interior air cannot leak into the attic, and continuous ventilation from eave to ridge. The engineer details an ice barrier membrane at the eaves, keeps heat sources like recessed lights and bath fans out of the attic or sealed and insulated, and avoids dead-level valleys where ice can anchor. The goal is snow that stays frozen until it melts from above, not from below.
Who is liable when roof snow slides onto a neighbor's property?
The building owner, which is why the engineer designs for it. Steep metal roofs shed snow in sudden releases, so the engineer either provides engineered snow retention above entries, walkways, and adjacent units, or designs the site and the HOA documents around managed shedding zones. Where the jurisdiction prohibits shedding across lot lines, the roof is designed to retain the full load — the engineer prices that decision honestly during design.
