How Is Great Lakes Lakefront Erosion Engineered Against?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Great Lakes lakefront erosion is engineered by sizing revetments, seawalls, or breakwaters for design wave forces and high-water cycles, stabilizing bluffs with drainage and retaining systems, and controlling upland runoff - permitted through state coastal programs and the Army Corps of Engineers, which decide approvals.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Reading the Lake and the Bluff

Every shoreline design starts with the water. The engineer assembles the wave climate from historical hindcasts adjusted for the site's exposure and nearshore depth, because a shallow foreshore breaks waves before they arrive while a deep one delivers them at full force. Design water levels combine the lake's multi-year high stands with storm surge and seiche setup, since a revetment sized for average water fails when the lake is three feet high and a November storm arrives on top of it. The bluff gets equal study. Borings and test pits reveal the soil profile: clay tills that fail along saturated planes, sands that ravel grain by grain, and layered profiles that do both. The engineer maps existing failure modes, whether toe undercutting, rotational slides, or surface gullying, because each demands a different fix. A bluff failing from groundwater needs drainage; a bluff failing from wave attack needs armor; most need both, and the investigation tells the engineer the proportion.

Shore Protection Structures

Revetments are the workhorse: armor stone placed on a graded slope over filter layers and geotextile, sized by wave height and period so individual stones stay put in the design storm. The slope dissipates wave energy rather than reflecting it, which protects neighboring shorelines from the reflected energy that vertical walls create. Seawalls suit sites with no room for a slope, but their vertical faces reflect waves downward, scouring the toe, so they need deep toe stone or sheet piling designed for that scour. Offshore breakwaters change the equation by breaking waves before they reach the shore, creating a calm zone where beaches can rebuild naturally. They are the most effective protection for long reaches but also the most expensive and the most heavily permitted, since they sit in public waters. In every case the engineer designs the transitions where the structure meets neighboring unprotected shore, because erosion simply moves to the unprotected edge if the ends are not detailed.

Permitting the Shoreline

Shoreline work on the Great Lakes crosses more jurisdictions than almost any other residential-scale engineering. State coastal programs regulate construction below the ordinary high-water mark and often on the bluff itself, the Army Corps of Engineers authorizes work affecting navigable waters, and local zoning governs setbacks, bluff disturbance, and access. Each state runs its own program with its own standards and timelines, so a solution permitted in Michigan may need redesign in Wisconsin. The permit path shapes the design from the start. Many states favor soft or hybrid approaches, such as stone revetments over vertical walls, and some restrict hard armoring where it would starve neighboring beaches of sediment. The engineer coordinates pre-application meetings with the regulators before finalizing the design, because a design that the agencies will not approve is not a design at all. Construction windows may be limited to protect fish spawning, adding another schedule constraint the project plan must absorb.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between a revetment and a seawall?
A revetment is a sloped layer of armor stone that absorbs and dissipates wave energy, while a seawall is a vertical wall that reflects wave energy. Revetments are generally preferred where space allows because reflected energy from seawalls scours the lakebed at the toe and can worsen erosion on adjacent properties.
Why do bluffs keep failing even after a seawall is built?
Because most bluff failures are driven from above, not from wave attack at the toe. Groundwater seeping through the bluff saturates failure planes, and uncontrolled surface runoff carves gullies. A seawall stops toe erosion but does nothing for groundwater or runoff, so lasting stabilization pairs shore protection with bluff drainage and surface water control.
Can plantings and vegetation stop shoreline erosion?
Vegetation stabilizes surface soils on the bluff face and reduces runoff erosion, but it cannot resist wave attack at the toe or deep rotational slides. Plantings are a valuable complement to structural shore protection and drainage, not a substitute for them on an actively eroding Great Lakes shoreline.
Who permits shoreline work on the Great Lakes?
Typically the state coastal management agency plus the Army Corps of Engineers for work affecting navigable waters, with local zoning approval for bluff disturbance and setbacks. Requirements, favored approaches, and review timelines differ by state, so the engineer confirms every applicable authority having jurisdiction during early design. All of them decide their own approvals.
