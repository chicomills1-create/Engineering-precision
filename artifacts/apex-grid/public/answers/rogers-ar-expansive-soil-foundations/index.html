How Are Foundations Designed for Rogers' Expansive Soils?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Rogers foundations handle Benton County's expansive clay pockets with geotechnical testing that quantifies swell potential, then either drilled piers socketed below the moisture-active zone with voided grade beams, or post-tensioned slabs stiff enough to bridge differential movement. Permanent drainage and moisture control around the foundation are as important as the structure, because wet-dry cycling is what drives the soil's movement.

Pier-and-Beam vs. Post-Tensioned Slab

The choice between deep piers and a stiffened slab follows the geotechnical report's severity assessment. Drilled piers with grade beams are the answer for high swell potential, deep active zones, or sites where the investigation shows the clay will move more than a slab can tolerate. The piers carry the building's weight to stable ground below the moisture fluctuations, the grade beams span pier to pier, and the void beneath the beams and slab gives the swelling soil somewhere to go. Underreamed or belled piers add uplift resistance where the swelling soil grips the shaft and tries to lift it. This system costs more upfront and essentially eliminates expansive-soil distress when designed and built correctly. Post-tensioned slabs serve the moderate range. The slab is a thick, heavily reinforced concrete plate with tensioned cables that put the whole slab into compression, making it stiff enough to span soft spots and resist the bending that differential heave imposes. It bears on prepared subgrade with careful moisture conditioning and often a moisture barrier, and its detailing includes thickened edges and ribs that add stiffness where the loads and the soil demand it. The system is economical and widely used in Northwest Arkansas production housing, but it has limits: it tolerates movement, it does not prevent it, so the geotechnical engineer must confirm the expected differential movement is within what the slab design assumes. Beyond that limit, piers are the answer, and value-engineering a pier recommendation down to a conventional slab is how foundation failures happen.

Drainage and Moisture: The Other Half of the Design

A perfect foundation on poorly drained expansive clay will still move, because the soil's behavior is driven by water. The grading plan establishes positive drainage away from the building on all sides, with the code's minimum slope as the floor and steeper, verified grading where the site allows it. Roof runoff is the largest single water source: gutters discharge through downspout extensions or buried pipes that carry water well beyond the foundation zone, not onto the backfill beside the wall. Irrigation is designed for consistency, keeping soil moisture stable rather than alternating soaking and drought, and planting beds against the foundation use controlled watering instead of daily saturation. Trees and landscaping get engineering attention on expansive sites. A large tree near the foundation withdraws significant moisture through its roots, shrinking the clay on its side and causing the foundation to settle differentially toward the tree, a pattern foundation repair contractors in Rogers see regularly. The landscape plan keeps large trees at distances related to their mature canopy and root zone, or the foundation design accounts for the moisture withdrawal. Utility trenches through clay are backfilled and compacted to the geotechnical specification, because a poorly compacted trench becomes a water conduit that feeds the clay exactly where the foundation bears. These are permanent maintenance obligations: the homeowner's drainage upkeep after construction matters as much as the contractor's grading during it, and the engineer should say so in plain language at project closeout.

What else do project teams ask?

What are the signs of expansive soil foundation problems?
Sticking doors and windows, diagonal cracks in drywall that open and close with the seasons, uneven or bouncing floors, and gaps between the foundation and the soil or between walls and ceilings. The seasonal pattern is the tell: damage that worsens in wet periods and partially recovers in dry ones points to swell-shrink movement. A structural engineer and a geotechnical investigation confirm the diagnosis before any repair is designed.
Why do Rogers homes need gutters and downspout extensions?
Because roof runoff is the largest controllable water source around the foundation, and on expansive clay, concentrated water is what triggers heave. Dumping thousands of gallons of roof water beside the foundation each storm saturates the clay exactly where movement does the most damage. Extensions or buried pipes that carry the water well clear of the building are among the cheapest and most effective foundation protection measures on expansive soil.
Can trees damage a foundation on expansive clay?
Yes, through moisture withdrawal. Large trees near a foundation pull water from the clay through their roots, shrinking the soil unevenly and causing differential settlement toward the tree. The effect is strongest in dry periods and with thirsty species. Landscape plans on expansive sites keep large trees at appropriate distances or the foundation is designed for the anticipated moisture changes, and removing a mature tree can itself cause heave as the soil rehydrates.
Does the building code require special foundations on expansive soil?
The code requires the foundation to be designed for the soil conditions, which on expansive clay means the geotechnical engineer's recommendations effectively govern the design. The code's drainage provisions, including positive slope away from the foundation, apply everywhere and matter most on expansive sites. The AHJ, Rogers for city parcels and Benton County for unincorporated parcels, reviews the design against the adopted code, but the site-specific geotechnical report sets the actual foundation requirements.
