What Are the Design Requirements for a Crematorium?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A crematorium is designed around the retort's exhaust stack and emissions controls, heat and noise isolation between the equipment room and chapel or viewing areas, and air-quality plus health-department permitting that must be secured before operation.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Retort exhaust and stack design

I begin with the retort manufacturer's submittals: flue size, exhaust temperature and volume, combustion-air requirements, and any required emissions-control equipment. The exhaust system is designed from those numbers — flue routing with proper clearances to combustibles, stack height and termination for dispersion, and structural support for the stack itself, which is a significant vertical element on the building. Where the air-quality permit requires monitoring or specific control equipment, those provisions are designed into the system from the start rather than retrofitted. Combustion air gets its own engineered path directly to the retort room, sized so the retorts never compete with the building's HVAC for air. The retort room is mechanically ventilated and cooled for operator safety and equipment longevity, with the room held negative to public spaces. I also design for the failure modes: what happens on power loss, how the system shuts down safely, and how operators access the equipment for the maintenance the manufacturer requires. A retort that cannot be serviced properly becomes an emissions problem.

Heat, noise, and separation from public spaces

The chapel, arrangement offices, and viewing rooms are quiet, dignified spaces, and the engineering keeps them that way. I separate the retort room from public areas with distance, mass, and construction that blocks both airborne noise and vibration — resilient equipment mounting, isolated ductwork, and wall assemblies designed for the actual sound levels involved. The HVAC zoning follows the same logic: public spaces get their own quiet, comfortable systems, completely independent of the equipment room's ventilation. Viewing rooms deserve special attention in the design. Families using them need privacy, calm lighting, comfortable temperature, and controlled sight lines to the retort area — dignified, never industrial. I coordinate the architectural and MEP design so the viewing experience feels intentional: dedicated HVAC zone, acoustic privacy from both the equipment and the lobby, and lighting controls that let staff set the right atmosphere. The families will remember this room; the engineering should be invisible.

Emissions permitting and jurisdiction

The air-quality permit is the schedule driver on most crematorium projects. I identify the permitting authority — state agency, regional air district, or county program — during early design and design the exhaust system to its requirements, including any stack testing, monitoring, or record-keeping provisions. The permit application typically needs equipment specifications, emissions data, and site information, so the retort selection and the permit track run in parallel. The jurisdiction picture is often split: the building permit may come from the city while air quality is governed by a county or regional district, and the health department may be a third agency with its own review. Zoning or conditional-use approval can add public hearings to the timeline. I map every AHJ and its submittal requirements before design is finalized, sequence the applications so the long-lead permits start first, and keep the design flexible enough to absorb permit conditions without rework. No one should promise a permit outcome — but a design that anticipates the permit's requirements moves through review far faster than one that discovers them late.

What else do project teams ask?

How tall does a crematorium exhaust stack need to be?
Stack height is set by the retort manufacturer's requirements, the air-quality permit, and dispersion needs — tall enough that exhaust disperses properly and does not affect neighboring properties or the building's own air intakes. There is no single universal height; it depends on the equipment, the site, surrounding buildings, and the air district's rules. I coordinate the stack design with the retort supplier and the permit requirements together, and I locate building air intakes so they can never draw in stack exhaust regardless of wind direction.
Can a crematorium recover heat from the retort?
Technically yes — retort exhaust carries significant heat that can preheat domestic hot water or building heating — but I design heat recovery as a carefully isolated optional system. The recovery equipment must handle high temperatures and corrosive exhaust constituents, and it must never compromise the retort's operation or the emissions controls. For many facilities, the added complexity and maintenance outweigh the energy savings, so I run the economics honestly with the owner before committing to it. When recovery does pencil out, it is designed with full bypass capability so the retort runs normally with the recovery system offline.
How are odor and visible emissions controlled?
Through proper equipment operation and exhaust design: a correctly operated modern retort with its afterburner or secondary combustion chamber destroys odorous compounds, and the exhaust system is designed for complete combustion air supply and proper stack dispersion. Visible emissions are controlled by operating within the equipment's parameters and the air-quality permit's opacity limits. The building design contributes too — the retort room is negatively pressurized relative to public areas so that if any fugitive emissions occur, they stay in the equipment room. There is no substitute for equipment that is properly sized, properly installed, and properly maintained.
What permits does a crematorium need?
Typically a building permit, an air-quality permit from the state or regional air district (the long-lead item, covering emissions and operating conditions), health-department approval, zoning or conditional-use approval, and fire-marshal review of fuel gas and fire protection. Some jurisdictions add their own layers. The air-quality permit often drives the project schedule, so I recommend starting that application during early design rather than after the drawings are done. Zoning deserves early attention too — crematories are a conditional or special use in many zoning codes, and community acceptance is part of the approval reality.
