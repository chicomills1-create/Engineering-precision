How Is MEP Designed for Boardwalk Retail Buildings?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Boardwalk retail MEP is engineered by elevating every electrical, mechanical, and plumbing component above the design flood elevation plus local freeboard, specifying marine-grade corrosion-resistant materials throughout, protecting rooftop and wall equipment for wind-borne debris regions, and planning emergency power so food and refrigerated tenants survive multi-day outages.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Elevating Equipment Above Floodwater

The equipment elevation plan is the backbone of the MEP design. Electrical panels, switchgear, water heaters, boilers, and air-handling units are placed on the second floor, on the roof, or on elevated platforms above the design flood elevation, and the engineer documents each elevation on the plans relative to the finished floor and the base flood elevation so the floodplain reviewer can verify compliance at a glance. Refrigerant lines, condensate drains, and plumbing that must pass through the below-flood zone are detailed to drain freely and survive wetting — no trapped low points, no equipment that fails when submerged. Below the design flood elevation, the design philosophy flips from protection to sacrifice. ASCE 24 expects enclosures below the elevated floor to be built with flood-damage-resistant materials and, in Zone V, with breakaway walls that collapse under wave action without transmitting the load to the structure above. That means no electrical outlets, no HVAC equipment, and no plumbing fixtures in the wash-through zone except what is specifically designed to flood and recover. The engineer lists exactly what is allowed below the flood line on the drawings so a future tenant improvement does not quietly install a panel where water will find it.

Salt Air, Wind, and Debris Protection

Material selection is driven by the marine atmosphere. The engineer writes specifications calling for epoxy-coated condenser and evaporator coils, NEMA 4X enclosures on all exterior electrical equipment, stainless steel hardware at every exposed connection, and ductwork with corrosion-resistant coatings or stainless construction where it sits in the airstream near the ocean. Lighting is marine-grade with sealed optics, because a boardwalk storefront that goes dark every time a fixture corrodes through is a revenue problem, not just a maintenance problem. Wind protection is engineered into the equipment layout. Rooftop units sit on rated curbs with attachment verified for the design wind pressures, and the engineer checks the overturning and sliding of every piece of rooftop equipment — a loose exhaust fan in a hurricane is a missile. Wall louvers and penetrations in the wind-borne debris region get impact-rated coverings or are positioned behind architectural screens that are themselves engineered for debris impact. Gas piping for restaurant tenants is anchored and valved so it can be shut down quickly ahead of a storm, and the engineer coordinates the shutoff locations with the tenant's emergency procedures.

Boardwalk Retail MEP Checklist

Use this checklist before the permit set is issued: • FEMA flood zone and base flood elevation confirmed; local freeboard requirement identified (city vs county ordinance) • All electrical panels, HVAC equipment, and water heaters elevated above the design flood elevation • Marine-grade coils, NEMA 4X enclosures, and stainless fasteners specified throughout • Rooftop equipment curbs and attachments verified for design wind pressures • Backwater valves on sanitary lines below the flood elevation; sealed cleanouts • Emergency power strategy sized for refrigeration tenants, generator and fuel above flood level • Coastal construction control line permit identified if the parcel is seaward of the line • Elevation certificate workflow confirmed with the floodplain reviewer

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't the electrical room go on the ground floor of a boardwalk shop?
Because flood design rules require electrical equipment to sit above the design flood elevation in high-hazard zones, and the ground floor of a boardwalk building is below it by definition. Equipment placed below the flood line will be submerged in the design storm, and saltwater destroys panels, breakers, and bus work permanently. The electrical room goes upstairs, on the roof, or on an elevated platform, and only breakaway walls and flood-tolerant construction occupy the wash-through zone below.
What does the wind-borne debris region change about the design?
It requires glazing, louvers, and wall penetrations to resist impacts from wind-driven objects, which on a boardwalk means the storefront systems, mechanical louvers, and any wall-mounted equipment need rated protection or engineered screens. Rooftop equipment attachment also gets stricter scrutiny, because anything the wind tears loose becomes debris for the building next door. The engineer verifies equipment anchorage and specifies impact-rated assemblies for the openings.
How do tenants keep refrigerated goods safe through a hurricane outage?
The engineer designs an emergency power strategy sized for the refrigeration load: either a building generator with an automatic transfer switch serving designated tenant panels, or code-compliant connection points where tenants can land portable generators. The generator, fuel storage, and transfer equipment all sit above the design flood elevation, and the design accounts for fuel duration through a multi-day outage so ice cream cases and food coolers stay cold until utility power returns.
Does the permit path differ on a city boardwalk versus a county one?
Yes. A city boardwalk usually runs through the municipal permitting portal with the city's floodplain ordinance, its own freeboard number, and often design review for storefront appearance. A county boardwalk answers to county floodplain rules and may additionally need a state coastal construction control line permit for anything seaward of the line, which controls foundations and dune impacts. The engineer confirms the controlling jurisdiction and its freeboard requirement before equipment locations are finalized.
