Does a Historic Building Renovation Need PE Review?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Historic renovations need PE review for any structural or MEP work — the engineer investigates the actual century-old construction, evaluates its condition and capacity, and designs code-compliant upgrades that preserve historic fabric, sealed for the AHJ and the preservation review.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Investigating the actual building

The investigation phase is the historic PE review's foundation, and I budget it seriously — the engineering that follows is only as good as the understanding of the building. I start with the documentary record: historic drawings, Sanborn maps, prior alteration permits, and any earlier engineering reports, treating each as a lead rather than a fact. Then the field work: selective demolition openings at representative locations to expose the real framing, masonry, and connections; systematic documentation of conditions — deterioration mapped, not just noted; and a hunt for the undocumented modifications that every old building carries, because those modifications, not the original design, usually govern the structural behavior. Material investigation follows where the analysis requires it. Masonry shear testing, concrete compressive strength from cores, timber species and grade identification — each converts a conservative assumption into a known value, and known values let the upgrade design be surgical rather than wholesale. The investigation concludes with the structural evaluation report: the building's systems described as they are, capacities assessed, deficiencies identified, and the upgrade scope the code analysis will require. That report, sealed, is the document the whole renovation's engineering stands on.

Designing upgrades that preserve

The upgrade design works within two masters: the code's life-safety demands and the preservation standards' fabric protections. Structurally, that usually means targeted strengthening rather than replacement: steel strong-backs on masonry walls, fiber-reinforced polymer or steel plate on deficient concrete, new lateral elements tucked into non-historic zones — each designed, detailed, and calculated under the PE's responsible charge. In California's seismic country, the voluntary or triggered seismic retrofit of unreinforced masonry and non-ductile concrete historic buildings is the signature scope: adding the lateral capacity the original construction lacks while the historic elevations remain intact. MEP upgrades follow the same philosophy of minimal intrusion. New systems route through chases, closets, and non-character-defining spaces; where exposure is unavoidable, the exposed work is designed to a finish standard the preservation review accepts. Every penetration of historic fabric gets structural verification — coring a 1920s concrete beam for a duct is an engineered decision with the capacity check to prove it. The sealed drawings show the AHJ a code-compliant building and show the historic commission a preserved one, because the PE's design served both from the first sketch.

Historic renovation PE checklist

A historic renovation submittal is ready for the PE's seal when the investigation is honest, the code path is deliberate, and the upgrades respect the fabric. The investigation report is the deliverable everything else stands on. • Documentary research: historic drawings, prior permits, and earlier reports gathered as investigation leads • Field investigation: exposed structure documented, conditions mapped, undocumented modifications identified • Material testing where capacity analysis needs real values: masonry, concrete, timber as applicable • Sealed structural evaluation report: systems, capacities, deficiencies, and the upgrade scope stated • Code analysis under the existing-building provisions: project level, triggers, and compliance path per element • Upgrade design balancing life safety with preservation: targeted strengthening, minimally intrusive MEP routing, every historic-fabric penetration structurally verified

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't historic buildings just be engineered like new construction?
Because the materials, the construction, and the preservation mandate are all different. Century-old masonry, early reinforced concrete, and old-growth timber have properties and behaviors modern prescriptive design doesn't address — the PE evaluates them from investigation and testing, not from span tables. The construction is often undocumented, with load paths altered by decades of modifications. And the preservation standards prohibit the brute-force solutions new construction would use: you can't just wrap a historic facade in a new lateral system. The engineering has to achieve code compliance within preservation constraints, which is harder and more interesting than new design — and exactly why it needs a PE's judgment rather than a prescriptive detail.
What does the structural investigation of a historic building involve?
Field verification of the actual structural system — exposing framing at representative locations, documenting masonry wythes and conditions, measuring members — correlated with any historic drawings as a starting point, not a conclusion. Condition assessment: deterioration, water damage, insect damage in timber, corrosion in early steel, spalling in early concrete. Material testing where the analysis needs it: masonry testing, concrete cores, wood grading. And documentation of every undocumented modification found: the removed bearing wall, the added mezzanine, the infilled opening. The investigation report becomes the basis of the structural evaluation, and its honesty about unknowns is what makes the subsequent engineering trustworthy.
How do existing-building code provisions help historic projects?
The adopted existing-building provisions recognize that a 1920s building can't reasonably meet every new-construction requirement, and they provide tiered compliance paths: repair, alteration, and change-of-occupancy levels with upgrade triggers tied to the work's extent. For historic buildings specifically, many jurisdictions overlay preservation-conscious provisions that accept alternative compliance where strict application would destroy historic character — provided life safety is achieved. I navigate these provisions as the core of the PE's code analysis: which level the project triggers, which upgrades the triggers require, and where the provisions allow the historic fabric to stand. The sealed code analysis shows the AHJ exactly which path each element follows.
What MEP challenges do historic renovations face?
Routing modern systems through construction that never anticipated them: threading ductwork, plumbing, and electrical through masonry walls and timber framing without destroying structural capacity or historic finishes. The PE's MEP review verifies the new systems' code compliance — ventilation, plumbing, electrical — while the routing is coordinated to minimize impact: chases in non-historic areas, exposed systems designed as features where appropriate, and structural verification everywhere new penetrations cut old framing. Historic buildings also commonly need full electrical service upgrades and new HVAC where none existed. The MEP design respects the building's constraints instead of fighting them, and the sealed set documents both the systems and the coordination.
