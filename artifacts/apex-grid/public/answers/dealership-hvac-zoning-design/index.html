How Is HVAC Zoning Designed for Car Dealership Buildings?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers zone dealerships by space type — showroom, shop, offices, lounge — giving each independent control matched to its loads and schedule, with sequences that manage pressure cascades and energy code compliance.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Zone Mapping: Showroom, Shop, Offices, and Lounge

Zone boundaries follow load and use, never just square footage. The showroom splits into perimeter zones at the glass — where solar gain dominates — and interior zones with steadier loads. The service shop is its own ventilation-driven zone, the parts department and offices form standard commercial zones, and the customer lounge gets hospitality-grade control. Each zone gets equipment sized for its own peak and controls programmed for its own schedule. The mapping also respects pressure. Clean zones — showroom, lounge, offices — hold positive to the shop, and the design achieves this through coordinated supply and exhaust quantities, not dampers fighting each other. The engineer documents the intended pressure relationship for every adjacent zone pair so commissioning can verify it.

Controls Sequencing and Energy Performance

The control sequences are where zoning succeeds or fails. Each zone gets occupancy scheduling matched to dealership hours — the shop ramps before the first appointment, the showroom holds through the evening, back offices set back earlier. Demand-controlled ventilation trims outdoor air in variable-occupancy zones, economizers harvest free cooling where the climate allows, and the sequences prevent the classic failure of simultaneous heating and cooling across adjacent zones. Energy modeling demonstrates compliance with the whole building as designed, with the shop's high ventilation loads represented accurately. In California, that means the 2025 California Energy Code / 2025 Standards, effective January 1, 2026. The engineer also builds in trending and alarming so the facilities team can see when a zone drifts out of its sequence — zoning only saves energy if it keeps operating as designed.

Dealership HVAC Zoning Design Checklist

A complete zoning package gives every space its own thermal destiny: • Zone map with boundaries at load and use changes, not arbitrary lines • Perimeter treatment at showroom glass for solar gain control • Shop designed as a ventilation-driven zone with tempered makeup air • Independent controls and scheduling for each zone's real occupancy • Pressure cascade documented and commissioned from clean spaces to the shop • Demand-controlled ventilation and economizers where they pay back • Energy code compliance demonstrated with honest shop ventilation loads

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't one HVAC system serve a whole dealership?
Because the loads are wildly different and often opposite. The glass showroom needs cooling on a sunny winter day while the shop needs heated makeup air; the lounge wants steady 72 degrees while the parts warehouse barely needs conditioning. A single system averages these into discomfort everywhere — some spaces roast while others freeze. Zoning gives each space equipment and controls matched to its actual load, which is both more comfortable and more efficient.
How do you handle the showroom's glass walls in HVAC design?
With dedicated perimeter zones at the glass line. Floor-to-ceiling glazing creates intense, fast-moving solar loads, so the design puts heating and cooling capacity right at the perimeter — fin-tube, trench convectors, or dedicated perimeter air systems — with controls that respond to solar conditions. The interior showroom zone then handles the steadier internal loads. Treating the glass as its own engineering problem, rather than hoping the general system covers it, is what keeps the showroom comfortable on a bright afternoon.
Should the service shop be air conditioned?
Usually it is ventilated rather than fully conditioned. The shop's huge exhaust rates for fume control make full air conditioning enormously expensive to operate — you would be cooling air and immediately exhausting it. The standard design provides tempered makeup air (heated in winter, sometimes evaporatively cooled in summer depending on climate), spot cooling at workstations where justified, and radiant or unit heating. The engineering targets safe, workable conditions rather than office comfort, and the budget goes to ventilation that protects health.
How does HVAC zoning affect energy code compliance?
Positively, when done honestly. Zoning lets each space use only the energy it needs — setback for unoccupied offices, ventilation-only operation for the shop, solar-responsive showroom control — which the energy model credits. The key is honest modeling: the shop's high ventilation and makeup air loads must appear in the compliance model at their real values. In California, compliance follows the 2025 California Energy Code / 2025 Standards, effective January 1, 2026, demonstrated for the building as actually designed and operated.
