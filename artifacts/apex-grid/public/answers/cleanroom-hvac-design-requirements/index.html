What Are the HVAC Design Requirements for Cleanrooms?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Cleanroom HVAC is designed around the ISO class: HEPA ceiling coverage, air change rates, and pressurization cascades scale with cleanliness, with unidirectional flow for ISO 5, tight temperature and humidity control, continuous monitoring, and formal qualification testing.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

ISO classes and what they demand

The ISO class is the single number that sizes the entire HVAC system. I translate it into three design outputs: the particle limit the room must hold, the HEPA coverage and airflow pattern that achieves it, and the air change rate that sustains it. ISO 5 demands unidirectional flow — a piston of clean air moving steadily downward from a near-full HEPA ceiling to low wall returns, sweeping particles away from the work zone before they can settle. Lower classes use mixed flow, where HEPA-filtered supply dilutes room air through conventional diffusers. Temperature and humidity control tighten as the class rises. Semiconductor and pharmaceutical processes often need temperature held within a degree and humidity within a few percent, which drives the sizing of the air handler's cooling, heating, and humidification sections as much as the particle requirements do. I also design for the room's internal loads honestly — process equipment, lighting, and personnel all add heat and particles, and a cleanroom designed for an empty room fails its first day in production.

Pressurization cascades and airlocks

I map the cascade the way I'd map a hospital's — cleanest room at the highest pressure, stepping down through gowning rooms and airlocks to the general corridor. The differentials are small but they must be constant, which means the supply and exhaust in every room track each other through all operating modes, including unoccupied setback. Door undercuts and transfer grilles are pressure devices in a cleanroom, sized deliberately rather than left to the door supplier. Personnel flow gets the same rigor as airflow. Gowning rooms are classified spaces in their own right — typically one or two classes below the cleanroom they serve — with their own air changes and pressure steps. Material pass-throughs with interlocked doors let supplies enter without opening the main doors, and I separate personnel and material airlocks where the process warrants it. Every pressure relationship is monitored continuously with local display and BAS alarming, because a cascade excursion is a contamination event until proven otherwise.

Filtration, monitoring, and qualification

HEPA filtration is the heart of the system, and I protect it like one. Prefiltration upstream extends HEPA life, filter housings are specified bubble-tight with gel or gasket seals, and every HEPA filter gets scanned for leaks after installation — a pinhole leak in a HEPA filter above a filling line is a batch-loss waiting to happen. Fan-filter units offer modularity and redundancy for many layouts; ducted central systems offer tighter control and easier maintenance access outside the classified space. I choose based on the class, the process, and how the owner maintains equipment. Monitoring never sleeps in a qualified cleanroom. Differential pressure, temperature, and humidity trend continuously, and particle counters sample the critical zones on a schedule the contamination control strategy defines. The building automation system alarms on excursion and keeps the records auditors ask for. Then qualification proves it all: filter integrity, airflow visualization, air changes, particle counts at rest and operational, and recovery testing. A cleanroom that can't produce those records can't defend its product, so I design the documentation deliverables into the project from day one. • Translate the ISO class into HEPA coverage, airflow pattern, and air change rate before sizing equipment • Cascade pressure from cleanest to dirtiest with interlocked airlocks at every transition • Specify bubble-tight HEPA housings with prefiltration and scan every filter after installation • Monitor pressure, temperature, humidity, and particles continuously with BAS alarming • Design test ports and documentation for IQ/OQ/PQ qualification from the start

What else do project teams ask?

What do ISO cleanroom classes mean in practice?
ISO classes rank rooms by allowable particle concentration — ISO 5 allows far fewer particles per cubic meter than ISO 8, with each class roughly a tenfold step. In practice, ISO 5 suits aseptic filling and the most sensitive semiconductor steps, ISO 6 and 7 cover most pharmaceutical and electronics manufacturing, and ISO 8 handles less critical assembly and gowning support. The class dictates HEPA coverage, air changes, gowning requirements, and monitoring rigor, so I lock the class with the process owner before designing anything — upgrading a finished ISO 8 room to ISO 7 is essentially a rebuild.
How much HEPA filter coverage does a cleanroom need?
It scales with class. ISO 5 unidirectional-flow rooms typically need 80 to 100 percent of the ceiling as HEPA coverage to maintain the downward sweeping airflow that defines the class. ISO 7 rooms commonly use 30 to 60 percent coverage with ceiling-mounted fan-filter units or ducted HEPA modules in a mixed-flow pattern. ISO 8 can go lower. I lay out coverage to protect the critical work zones first — the filling line, the lithography tool — then fill in the room, because uniform coverage matters less than clean air where the product is exposed.
Why do cleanrooms need pressure cascades and airlocks?
Because particles ride on air currents, and the cascade makes sure those currents always move from cleaner to dirtier spaces. Each step in the cascade — cleanroom to gowning room to corridor — holds a small positive differential, so opening a door pushes clean air outward instead of pulling dirty air in. Airlocks and gowning rooms give personnel and materials a transition zone where outer garments shed particles before entry. I interlock airlock doors so both can't open at once, which preserves the cascade during the busiest shift changes.
How is a cleanroom proven to meet its class?
Through formal qualification: installation qualification confirms it's built as designed, operational qualification confirms systems perform across their ranges, and performance qualification confirms the room holds class in operation with people and equipment working. Testing includes HEPA filter integrity scans, air change and airflow visualization, particle counting at rest and in operation, and recovery tests measuring how fast the room returns to class after a disturbance. I design in the test ports, monitoring points, and documentation structure for this during design — qualification is not a punch-list item.
