How Is HVAC Designed for Grocery Store Produce Departments?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design the produce zone cooler and more humid than the sales floor with gentle low-velocity air, coordinate misting with the latent load, and ventilate to prevent ethylene buildup.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Temperature, Humidity, and Misting Coordination

The produce department's temperature target balances product needs against shopper comfort — cool enough to slow respiration and extend shelf life, warm enough that customers linger. The engineer zones the department separately from general sales floor HVAC so it can hold its own setpoints, with humidity control that maintains high relative humidity at the product without condensing on cold surfaces or wetting the floor. Misting is coordinated as an HVAC load, not an afterthought. The design accounts for the moisture misting adds, schedules it intermittently to avoid saturating the air, and uses reverse-osmosis or filtered water to prevent mineral deposits on product and fixtures. Drainage under misted displays handles the runoff, and the humidity controls modulate with the misting schedule rather than fighting it.

Air Distribution and Ethylene Control

Air movement in produce is a Goldilocks problem: enough circulation to prevent stagnant ethylene pockets and even out temperatures, gentle enough not to desiccate leafy product. The engineer uses low-velocity displacement or carefully aimed diffusers, keeps supply air off the open displays, and verifies airspeeds at the product level during commissioning. Ethylene control shapes the department layout and ventilation. High-ethylene producers are separated from ethylene-sensitive items, the HVAC provides enough air exchange to dilute what the product emits, and in some designs ethylene scrubbers or ventilation strategies target the worst offenders. The engineer also considers the back room: produce prep and storage need the same temperature and humidity discipline, or product degrades before it ever reaches the floor.

Produce Department HVAC Checklist

A complete produce HVAC design keeps product fresh and the floor dry: • Separate temperature and humidity zoning from the general sales floor • High relative humidity at product level without floor or fixture condensation • Misting schedule coordinated with the latent load and humidity controls • Low-velocity air distribution aimed away from open displays • Ethylene management through layout separation and ventilation • Filtered misting water and drainage under misted displays • Back-room prep and storage held to the same temperature discipline

What else do project teams ask?

What temperature and humidity does a produce department need?
Most produce departments target temperatures a few degrees below the general sales floor with relative humidity of 80 percent or higher at the product level — leafy greens and vegetables thrive cold and humid, while tropical fruits prefer slightly warmer. The exact targets follow the product mix, and the engineer zones the department so it holds its own setpoints. The critical detail is avoiding condensation: high humidity is wanted at the product, not dripping from fixtures onto the floor.
What is ethylene and why does it matter in produce departments?
Ethylene is a natural plant hormone that fruits emit as they ripen — and it accelerates ripening and spoilage in nearby produce. Bananas and avocados are heavy emitters; lettuce, herbs, and broccoli are highly sensitive. In a poorly ventilated department, ethylene concentrates and sensitive items wilt days early. The engineering response is layout separation, enough air circulation to prevent stagnant pockets, and ventilation that dilutes the gas — simple physics applied to a real spoilage problem.
Why does air velocity matter over produce displays?
Moving air strips moisture from product surfaces — the same wind-chill drying that wilts lettuce in a home refrigerator's crisper when the fan runs too hard. High-velocity supply air aimed at open produce displays accelerates moisture loss, wilting, and weight shrink, which is literally product evaporating into profit loss. The engineer specifies low-velocity distribution and verifies airspeeds at the display, trading a little less air movement for measurably longer shelf life.
How does produce misting affect the store's humidity?
Misting adds real moisture that the HVAC system must handle — it is a scheduled latent load like any other. The design sizes dehumidification for the misting schedule, uses intermittent rather than continuous misting to let the air recover, and coordinates the humidity controls with the misting timer. Stores that add misting without telling the HVAC design end up with condensation, sweating cases nearby, and slippery floors; coordinated design gets the crisp product without the side effects.
