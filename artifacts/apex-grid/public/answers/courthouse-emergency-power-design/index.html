How Is Emergency Power Designed for Courthouse Continuity?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers classify loads per code, size generators with sequenced load pickup across multiple transfer switches, provide 24-72 hour fuel storage, and keep emergency distribution fully independent from normal power.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Load Classification and Generator Sizing

The load classification is the design's legal backbone. Emergency systems under the electrical code — egress lighting, exit signs, fire alarm, fire pumps, and elevators used in evacuation — get the fastest restoration and the most rigorous installation requirements. Legally required standby covers systems like security, communications, and smoke control that the code or the authority having jurisdiction mandates. Optional standby is the owner's choice: courtroom AV so proceedings can continue, holding area HVAC, data rooms, and selected receptacles. Sizing uses the sequenced demand, not the connected total. The engineer calculates the load that actually transfers at each step, accounts for motor starting inrush on HVAC and elevator loads, and selects a generator that runs in its efficient band at the expected operating point — typically well above half-load. Power quality matters for the sensitive electronics: the design verifies voltage and frequency dip during the largest motor start stays within the tolerance of the security, AV, and IT systems riding on the generator.

Fuel, Transfer Switches, and Testing

Fuel storage runtime is set by the facility's role: a courthouse that must operate through multi-day outages needs on-site fuel for the full required duration, not just a day tank and a hope. Diesel remains the standard for its energy density and shelf life, with fuel polishing and maintenance to keep stored fuel viable, and the design includes refueling access for tanker trucks during an extended event. Natural gas generators trade unlimited runtime for dependence on the gas utility — the engineer presents that tradeoff honestly for the jurisdiction's risk profile. Transfer switches are the system's decision points, and the design separates them by load class with independent conduits and enclosures. Bypass isolation switches allow maintenance without dropping the load — critical for a building that cannot go dark for switch service. The monthly test requirement is designed as a facility feature: load-bank or building-load testing with automatic logging, so compliance is a routine rather than a scramble. Every transfer, every test, and every fuel level reports to the building management system.

Courthouse Emergency Power Checklist

A complete emergency power design keeps the courts and their protections running: • Full load inventory classified as emergency, legally required standby, or optional standby • Generator sized for sequenced demand with motor-starting and power quality verified • Multiple transfer switches with load pickup sequencing: life safety first • On-site fuel storage for the required runtime with polishing and refueling access • Independent emergency distribution — separate panels, conduits, and enclosures • Bypass isolation switches for maintenance without dropping critical loads • Monthly loaded testing with automatic logging and BMS monitoring of all status

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between emergency and standby power?
Emergency power serves life-safety systems the electrical code mandates — egress lighting, fire alarm, fire pumps — with the fastest automatic restoration, typically within 10 seconds, and the strictest installation rules. Legally required standby covers other code-mandated systems like security and smoke control with slightly longer restoration allowances. Optional standby is everything the owner chooses to back up, like courtroom AV or data rooms. The classification drives the transfer switch arrangement, wiring methods, and testing requirements.
How long should a courthouse generator run on its fuel supply?
The runtime target follows the facility's designated role — courthouses that serve as continuity-of-government or emergency operations facilities commonly target 24 to 72 hours of on-site fuel, sometimes more. The engineer sizes the tank for that duration at the expected operating load, adds fuel maintenance to keep stored diesel viable, and arranges refueling contracts for longer events. The honest answer depends on the jurisdiction's emergency plan, which the design is built to support.
Can one generator serve both life-safety and optional loads?
Yes, with proper separation: the code requires emergency loads to be electrically independent from other loads, which is achieved with separate transfer switches, panels, and raceways — not necessarily separate generators. The design sequences the pickup so life-safety transfers first and optional loads follow, and selective coordination ensures a fault on an optional circuit cannot cascade into the life-safety system. One well-designed generator with disciplined distribution is standard practice.
Why does generator testing need to be under load?
Because an unloaded test proves the engine starts, not that the system works. Wet-stacking, clogged filters, and transfer switch failures only reveal themselves under real load. Monthly testing that transfers actual building load — or a load bank sized to exercise the generator properly — verifies the full chain: start, transfer, carry, retransfer. The design includes the test provisions and logging so the facility can prove compliance without improvising.
