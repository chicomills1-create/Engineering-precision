How Are Texas Roofs Designed to Resist Hail?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Texas roofs are designed to resist hail with impact-rated assemblies — Class 4 shingles or standing-seam metal over solid decking — plus adequate slope, reinforced fastening patterns, and sacrificial details at ridges and edges, meeting the Texas Windstorm (TWIA) requirements in the designated catastrophe area along the coast.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Impact-Rated Assemblies and What the Rating Means

The Class 4 impact rating is earned in a lab, not claimed on a brochure. Test assemblies are struck with steel balls dropped from specified heights, then examined for fractures, cracks, and tears in the weathering surface. A Class 4 shingle survives the heaviest test without damage that would admit water. The engineer relies on this tested performance because hailstone size and energy vary wildly in the field — the rating is the only objective measure that a given product resists the impact energy Texas storms deliver. Material choice follows the rating. Polymer-modified asphalt shingles achieve Class 4 by flexing on impact instead of fracturing like conventional fiberglass shingles. Standing-seam metal earns its reputation differently: the steel dents rather than punctures, the concealed clip system lets panels move without tearing fasteners out, and the continuous vertical seams give hailstones no edge to catch. Concrete and clay tile resist impact well but demand a structure designed for their weight and a fastening system that keeps individual tiles from becoming wind-borne debris. The engineer matches the assembly to the building's structure, slope, and budget — then details the underlayment as the backup waterproofing layer regardless of the outer material.

Wind, Water, and the TWIA Corridor

Hail never arrives alone in Texas — it comes inside severe thunderstorms with damaging straight-line wind, and sometimes with tornadoes. The engineer therefore designs the hail-resistant surface and the wind-resistant attachment as one assembly. Enhanced fastening schedules, ring-shank nails, properly attached decking, and edge metal designed for the code's wind pressures keep the impact-rated surface on the building during the storm that tests it. Parapets and rooftop equipment get the same attention, since a dislodged unit becomes a battering ram against the very roof it sat on. In the TWIA catastrophe area along the coast, the Texas windstorm requirements overlay the building code. The engineer designs to both, selecting assemblies and fastening that satisfy the windstorm program's criteria and preparing the documentation the certification process requires — product approvals, fastening schedules, and inspection records. Outside the catastrophe area, the local authority having jurisdiction governs through standard plan review, but the engineering is the same: an impact-rated surface, a wind-rated attachment, and a waterproofing backup, detailed as a single system and inspected during installation.

What else do project teams ask?

What does a Class 4 impact rating mean for a roof?
Class 4 is the highest impact-resistance rating for roofing, earned by surviving standardized steel-ball drop tests without fracturing the weathering surface. Class 4 shingles — usually polymer-modified asphalt — flex on impact instead of cracking like conventional shingles. The rating gives the engineer an objective, tested measure of hail resistance rather than a marketing claim.
Is a metal roof better than shingles for Texas hail?
Standing-seam metal performs very well: it dents rather than punctures, its concealed fasteners resist wind uplift, and it typically outlasts shingles by decades. It costs more upfront. Class 4 impact-rated shingles are the economical alternative with proven hail performance. The engineer selects based on the building's structure, slope, budget, and the owner's long-term plans.
What are the TWIA requirements for roofs?
The Texas Windstorm Insurance Association program applies in the designated catastrophe area along the Gulf Coast, where certain construction must meet windstorm-resistant standards and be inspected or certified to qualify for windstorm insurance coverage. The engineer designs the roof assembly to satisfy both the building code and the windstorm program's criteria and documents the assembly for certification.
Who approves a hail-resistant roof design?
The local authority having jurisdiction — the city building department or the county permitting office — approves the roof through plan review and field inspection. In the coastal catastrophe area, the Texas windstorm certification process adds its own documentation and inspection requirements on top of the local permit. The engineer confirms which approvals apply before specifying the assembly.
