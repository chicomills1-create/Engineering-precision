How Is Indoor Crop Lighting Designed for Maximum Yield per Watt?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Indoor crop lighting is engineered for the cheapest mole of photons: fixtures specified and compared on micromoles per joule with total cost of ownership modeled, schedules optimized against time-of-use rates and demand charges, uniformity eliminating wasted over-lighting, and maintenance planned around measured depreciation rather than fixture failure.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Fixture Efficacy and Cost per Mole

The engineer treats the fixture schedule as a procurement of photons. Each candidate fixture's efficacy — micromoles of photosynthetic photons per joule of electricity — is compared at realistic operating temperatures, since efficacy falls as fixtures run hot and the grow room is a warm place. The comparison extends to the heat: a less-efficient fixture not only buys fewer photons per kilowatt-hour, it also buys more cooling load per photon, and the engineer carries both penalties in the total-cost model. Driver efficiency, dimming range, and warranty terms join the evaluation, because a high-efficacy fixture with a short driver life is a false economy. Uniformity is where purchased photons become used photons. The engineer lays out fixtures so the canopy's dimmest productive point still receives the target PPFD — every photon above the crop's saturation point in a hotspot is electricity converted to heat for no yield. The layout is verified on the light plan at real mounting heights, with edge and corner corrections, and the engineer resists the temptation to solve uniformity with excess fixtures: the economic optimum balances fixture count against the value of the uniformity gained. Reflective room finishes are coordinated as free photons, recovering stray light back to the canopy.

Scheduling Against Utility Rates

The photoperiod is negotiated with the electric bill. The engineer obtains the site's actual utility tariff — time-of-use periods, demand charge structure, and any agricultural or interruptible rates available — and models the lighting schedule's cost under it. Where the crop's photoperiod response allows, light hours shift toward off-peak windows; where it does not, the design at least avoids stacking every zone's startup into the demand-peak window. Staged startup sequences ramp zones minutes apart, and the controls enforce the stagger so a power restoration does not slam the service with the entire farm at once. Dimming becomes a demand-management tool. DLI-based controls that trim fixture output to land exactly on the daily light target save energy directly, and the same dimming trims the demand peak: the engineer programs the controls to cap total lighting demand during the utility's peak window, dimming zones proportionally while protecting each crop's minimum DLI. The energy model documents the expected demand and consumption under the designed schedule, giving the owner a budget to manage against and the engineer a baseline to verify in the first year's bills.

Indoor Crop Lighting Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the crop lighting design is finalized: • Fixtures compared on micromoles per joule at realistic operating temperatures, with total cost of ownership modeled • Heat rejected per photon carried into the cooling load — efficacy evaluated on photons and cooling together • Layout verified for uniformity at real mounting heights so no energy is wasted on over-lit hotspots • Photoperiod schedule modeled against the site's actual utility tariff: time-of-use, demand charges, available ag rates • Zone startup staging designed so the farm never peaks the electrical service, including after power restoration • DLI-based dimming specified with demand capping during the utility's peak window • Depreciation headroom in the initial light level, with a cleaning schedule and periodic PPFD verification planned • Fixture replacement budgeted by operating hours with accessible mounting for cleaning and service

What else do project teams ask?

What does micromoles per joule actually tell you?
How many usable photons a fixture produces per unit of electricity — the fundamental efficiency of the light as a growing tool. Higher efficacy means more crop-driving light per dollar of power and less waste heat per photon, which also shrinks the cooling load. The engineer compares fixtures on this number at realistic temperatures and uses it as the basis for the total-cost-of-ownership model, not just the purchase price.
How do demand charges change the lighting schedule?
They punish peaks. Many commercial tariffs bill a demand charge on the highest fifteen-minute usage each month, so a farm that starts every zone's lights simultaneously can set a costly peak in one instant. The engineer staggers zone startups, shifts photoperiods toward off-peak rate windows where the crop allows, and uses dimming to cap demand during peak periods — each strategy modeled against the actual tariff before the schedule is set.
When should grow lights be cleaned or replaced?
On a measured schedule, not when they fail. Output depreciates with operating hours and with dust and film accumulation in the humid environment, so the engineer designs depreciation headroom into the initial light level, specifies a cleaning cadence the staff can execute, and plans PPFD verification that triggers cleaning or replacement when output drops below threshold. Budgeting replacement by hours keeps the canopy under full light for the fixtures' entire service life.
Is retrofitting an HPS room to LED worth it?
Usually, and the engineer proves it with numbers rather than assumptions. The comparison models the LED fixtures' higher efficacy against the HPS baseline — electricity savings plus the reduced cooling load from lower heat rejection — against the retrofit cost including any electrical, structural, and controls changes. At production duty cycles the payback is often measured in a few years, but the model has to use the room's real hours, rates, and cooling costs to be trustworthy.
