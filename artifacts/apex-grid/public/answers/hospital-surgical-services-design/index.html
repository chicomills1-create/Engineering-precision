How Are Hospital Surgical Services Engineered for Safe Care?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Surgical services are engineered as a one-way pre-op to OR to PACU platform around a central sterile core, with zoned HVAC and utilities matched to each phase and capacity sized for peak case volume.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Department Flow and Zoning

The floor plan separates three traffic types: patients moving forward through care, staff circulating within the restricted zone, and materials moving between central sterile and the ORs. Pre-op holding sits at the restricted boundary with a check-in that verifies identity and consent before the patient crosses into the surgical zone. Family waiting stays outside the restricted area with visual or electronic status updates. The PACU needs direct access from every OR with wide corridors for beds and monitoring equipment, plus its own nursing station with sightlines to all bays. Discharge splits into same-day and inpatient paths so ambulatory patients never travel through inpatient corridors. Support spaces, anesthesia workrooms, locker rooms, dictation, and on-call rooms, are zoned so staff move efficiently without crossing patient paths.

Sterile Core and Utility Infrastructure

The sterile core's HVAC holds positive pressure to the OR corridor with temperature and humidity controlled for both instrument packaging and staff comfort. Case cart staging, implant storage, and supply rooms cluster around it, and the connection to central sterile is a clean, dedicated path. The core's size is driven by the case schedule: it must hold the instrument sets for the day's cases plus turnover sets without congestion. Medical gas distribution zones the ORs and PACU separately so maintenance in one area does not affect the other, with area alarms at each nursing station. The essential electrical system carries every OR's surgical lighting, booms, and receptacles plus PACU monitoring, and the HVAC design accounts for the department's high internal heat gains. Redundancy in air handling matters here: losing ventilation to the OR suite mid-schedule is a department-wide shutdown.

Surgical Services Engineering Checklist

Use this checklist during design and plan check: • One-way patient flow: pre-op to OR to PACU to discharge or inpatient transfer • Central sterile core at positive pressure with clean dedicated link to central sterile • ORs at the top of the pressure cascade with HEPA supply and high air changes • Separate clean and soiled paths with dedicated soiled return to central sterile • Pre-op bays and PACU positions sized so the ORs are never the bottleneck • Medical gas zones separating ORs and PACU with area alarms at nursing stations • Essential power to surgical lighting, booms, anesthesia, and PACU monitoring • HVAC redundancy so ventilation loss never shuts down the surgical schedule

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between the OR suite and surgical services?
The OR suite is the restricted zone containing the operating rooms and sterile core. Surgical services is the full department: pre-op holding, the OR suite, PACU, family waiting, and all support spaces. Engineering the department means designing how all of these phases connect and flow, not just the rooms where surgery happens.
How many pre-op bays does a surgical department need?
Enough that patient prep never delays the OR schedule. The count comes from the case volume, turnover times, and the clinical program's prep workflow, modeled by the design team with the surgical staff. Undersized pre-op is one of the most common bottlenecks in surgical departments.
Why does the sterile core sit at the center of the OR suite?
To minimize travel distance for instruments and supplies to every OR while keeping the core at positive pressure as the cleanest zone. Central placement also lets a single clean path from central sterile serve the whole suite without crossing soiled or public traffic.
How is PACU different from a regular patient room?
The PACU is an open, highly monitored recovery area where patients emerge from anesthesia. It needs medical gases and monitoring at every bay, direct nurse sightlines across all positions, rapid access from every OR, and the ability to flex into higher-acuity recovery. It is staffed and equipped for immediate post-anesthesia complications.
