Do As-Built Drawings Need PE Certification?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

As-built drawings need PE certification when a permit authority, lender, or insurer requires a licensed engineer's professional statement that the documents accurately represent existing conditions — and that certification is only legitimate after real field verification, never as a desk exercise.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What triggers the requirement

The most common trigger is permit closeout: the AHJ will not issue a certificate of occupancy or close an old permit until the record drawings reflect what was built, and for structural or MEP scope they want those records behind a PE's seal. Unpermitted construction is the second trigger — when a violation surfaces during a sale or a complaint, the path back to compliance usually runs through an engineer's evaluation and certified as-builts. Then there are the transactional triggers: buyers, lenders, and insurers doing diligence on commercial property want certified existing-conditions documents before money moves, particularly for older buildings where the original drawings are long gone. Each trigger shapes the engagement. A closeout certification focuses on code compliance of what was built. A transactional certification focuses on accurate representation of systems and structure for underwriting. I scope the field work to the question being asked, because verifying an entire 50,000-square-foot building to close out a rooftop unit permit is waste, and verifying only the rooftop unit when a lender is underwriting the whole structure is negligence.

How I verify before I certify

Field verification is methodical. I start with whatever records exist — original permit drawings, prior inspection reports, contractor photos — and build a checklist of what needs confirming in the field. On site, I measure structural members, document connection types, photograph equipment nameplates and panel schedules, and trace MEP distribution far enough to confirm the drawings' logic. Discrepancies get investigated, not glossed: if the drawings show a beam that isn't there, I find out what is there and what it's carrying. Everything observed goes into the drawing set as revised, and everything not observable gets a stated limit in the certification letter. The certification letter itself is precise about scope: what was verified, on what dates, by what methods, and what the engineer concludes. It states compliance findings plainly — where the as-built conditions meet code and where they don't — because a certification that hedges everything is useless to the AHJ or lender reading it. Responsible charge applies here exactly as it does to new design: the PE stands behind the statement professionally.

As-built certification checklist

An as-built certification engagement is ready to produce a defensible letter when these pieces are in place. The theme is evidence: the certification is only as strong as the verification behind it. • Reason for certification identified — closeout, violation cure, lender, or insurance — so scope matches the need • All available existing records gathered: original drawings, inspection reports, contractor documentation • Site access arranged for the areas the certification must cover, including roofs and equipment rooms • Field measurements and photographs documenting structural and MEP systems as they stand • Stated limits for concealed conditions, with destructive investigation scoped if the AHJ or lender requires it • Certification letter stating methods, dates, findings, and compliance conclusions — sealed by the PE in responsible charge

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between as-built drawings and a PE certification letter?
As-built drawings are the documents themselves — plans showing the building as it was actually constructed, including every field change from the original design. The PE certification letter is the engineer's professional statement that those drawings accurately represent existing conditions, based on the field verification performed. You can have as-builts drawn by anyone, but only a licensed PE's certification carries professional accountability. Most AHJs and lenders that ask for as-builts of structural or MEP systems want both: the drawings and the engineer's sealed statement behind them.
Will a PE certify as-builts for work that was done without permits?
Sometimes, but with eyes open and conditions stated. The PE's job is to verify what exists and evaluate whether it meets code — not to bless the fact that it was built without permits. I field-verify the construction, compare it against the adopted code, and my certification describes what I observed and whether it complies. If it doesn't comply, the honest deliverable is a letter stating the deficiencies and what remediation the code requires, not a certification of compliance. The AHJ then decides how to handle the violation. No competent engineer certifies non-compliant work as compliant to make a violation disappear.
How does field verification work for concealed conditions?
The engineer verifies what can be observed and states the limits plainly. I measure exposed structure, document equipment and systems I can see, and review any available construction records, photos, or inspection reports to fill gaps. For conditions inside walls, above ceilings, or below slabs, the certification either excludes them, relies on documented evidence with that reliance stated, or the engagement includes selective destructive investigation — small openings to confirm framing, connections, or reinforcement. What I never do is certify concealed conditions on assumption. The letter's credibility comes from its stated limits as much as from its conclusions.
Do lenders really require PE-certified as-builts?
For commercial refinancing, acquisition, and construction-draw verification, yes, routinely. Lenders want to know the collateral is what the borrower says it is — that the square footage, the structural system, and the MEP infrastructure match the underwriting. Insurance carriers ask for the same after major renovations or in high-risk markets like Florida's wind zones. The PE certification gives the lender a licensed professional's statement rather than the borrower's word, which is exactly why it has underwriting value. If your lender's checklist mentions engineer certification of existing conditions, this is the engagement.
