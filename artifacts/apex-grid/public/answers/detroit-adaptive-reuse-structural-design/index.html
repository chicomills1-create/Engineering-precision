How Is a Detroit Factory Converted to Apartments Structurally?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A Detroit factory is converted to apartments by first assessing the existing structure — testing concrete and steel, mapping the lateral system — then designing new floor openings, added shear walls or braced frames for current loads, and upgraded foundations where needed, all permitted through the city's historic and building review processes.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Investigating the Existing Structure

The investigation phase determines everything downstream. The engineer surveys every floor: framing layout, member sizes, connection types, signs of distress such as cracking, corrosion, or past modifications. Original drawings are invaluable when they exist — Detroit's industrial architects often left detailed records — but field verification is mandatory because undocumented alterations, removed columns, and infilled openings are the rule rather than the exception in century-old factories. Exploratory openings expose concealed connections, and the engineer maps the lateral system: which walls or frames actually resist wind and seismic loads, and whether the diaphragms can deliver those loads to them. Material testing quantifies what the survey finds. Concrete cores from columns, beams, and slabs establish the in-place compressive strength, which in early-1900s Detroit factories can be well below modern values. Steel sampling or non-destructive testing establishes the grade of the structural steel or the properties of cast iron in the oldest buildings. Where the lateral system relies on unreinforced masonry infill or party walls, in-place shear testing or conservative default properties set the design values. The engineer compiles all of this into a structural assessment report that becomes the factual basis for the conversion design — and the document the plan reviewer relies on to accept capacities that differ from modern new-construction values.

Engineering the Residential Conversion

The residential program imposes new structural demands. Apartment layouts need stairs, elevators, and vertical shafts that require cutting openings in existing floors — each opening gets engineered edge beams or headers, and the diaphragm is re-analyzed to confirm it still transfers lateral loads around the new holes. Corridor and unit demising walls add dead load the original industrial floors were not necessarily designed for, so the engineer checks every floor for the new gravity loading and strengthens members where the demand exceeds the tested capacity, often with steel plating, fiber-reinforced wraps, or supplemental framing. Lateral upgrades are usually the largest structural scope. A factory designed before modern seismic provisions rarely has a complete, ductile lateral system, so the engineer designs new shear walls or braced frames — commonly placed at stair and elevator cores where they serve the residential layout — and new collectors that drag diaphragm forces into them. Foundations are checked for the added lateral overturning and any net increase in gravity load; where existing footings are inadequate, the design adds underpinning, micropiles, or enlarged footings. Throughout, the engineer details the new elements to connect reliably to the old structure, because the connection between new concrete or steel and century-old materials is where conversions succeed or fail.

Detroit's Building and Historic Review Tracks

The building permit track runs through the Buildings, Safety Engineering and Environmental Department, with structural drawings, calculations based on the investigation report, and a special inspection program for the structural work. Reviewers focus on the load path: how gravity and lateral loads travel from the new residential floors through the modified existing structure to the foundations. Because the design relies on tested material properties rather than code default values for new construction, the investigation report is a formal part of the submittal. The historic track applies where the factory is designated or in a historic district — common for Detroit's landmark industrial buildings. The Historic District Commission reviews proposed changes for compatibility with the building's historic character, which can affect structural choices: rooftop additions for residential amenity space, new window openings, or exterior bracing may be restricted or require sensitive detailing. The engineer and architect develop the structural scheme with both reviewers in mind from the start, since a lateral system designed without regard for historic constraints risks a redesign late in the process. Each authority decides its own approvals.

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't the original factory drawings be used directly for the conversion design?
Because a century-old building never matches its drawings. Undocumented alterations, removed or added members, infilled openings, material deterioration, and construction tolerances all deviate from the original plans. The engineer field-verifies the actual structure and tests the actual materials — the design must be based on what is really there, which is also what the plan reviewer requires.
What is the biggest structural challenge in a factory-to-apartment conversion?
Usually the lateral system. Pre-war factories were not designed for modern wind and seismic requirements, so the engineer must add new shear walls or braced frames and the collectors that feed them — all threaded through an existing building without destroying the historic character or the residential floor plans. Floor openings for stairs and elevators, and foundation capacity for the new loads, are the other major scopes.
How are new floor openings cut in an existing concrete factory floor?
The engineer designs edge beams or headers around each opening to carry the loads the removed slab used to carry, then details the cutting sequence so the structure stays stable during construction. The remaining floor diaphragm is re-analyzed to verify it can still transfer lateral loads around the openings to the shear walls or frames. Temporary shoring is typically required during the work.
Does a Detroit factory conversion need historic approval?
If the building is a designated historic resource or lies within a historic district, yes — the Historic District Commission reviews changes for compatibility with historic character, on a separate track from the building permit. The structural engineer coordinates both reviews, because lateral upgrades, rooftop additions, and new openings can all be affected by historic requirements.
