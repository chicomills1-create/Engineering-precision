How Are Norman Homes Built on Expansive Soil?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Norman homes on expansive clay start with a lot-specific geotechnical investigation (borings, lab testing, swell analysis) that sets the foundation — post-tensioned stiffened slab or drilled piers below the active zone — plus moisture-controlled earthwork and a homeowner maintenance program. The project permits through the City of Norman or the county AHJ.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Geotechnical Investigation: Borings, Lab Work, and Parameters

A residential geotechnical study for a Norman lot typically includes at least two undisturbed-sample borings to 15 to 20 feet, deeper where trees or fill are involved, with laboratory testing for Atterberg limits, moisture content, and swell potential. The report that follows is the foundation's birth certificate: it characterizes the subsoils, recommends site preparation and earthwork, selects the foundation type and bearing depth, sets allowable pressures, and includes a homeowner maintenance program. Design parameters are developed from the weaker boring conditions, because subsoils within a single lot can vary significantly and the foundation must work on the worst soil it actually bears on. The report also answers the questions the structural engineer cannot: the depth of the active zone, the expected differential movement for edge-lift and center-lift cases, the fill type permitted under floor slabs, whether void boxes are needed beneath grade beams, and how trees near the foundation affect the design. For production builders, the same investigation discipline applies at the subdivision scale, with the geotechnical engineer establishing the earthwork and foundation standards for the whole development. Skipping this step and pouring a conventional slab on untested Norman clay is how the metro's cracked-slab inventory was built.

Earthwork, Drainage, and the Homeowner's Role

Site preparation follows the report: clear and grub, proofroll with a loaded truck or sheepsfoot roller to expose soft areas, remove and replace unsuitable soils, and place structural fill in controlled lifts compacted to the specified density at the right moisture content. Fill under floor slabs must be non-expansive, verified by Atterberg limit testing of the borrow source — bank sand and random site soils do not qualify. Six to twelve inches of compacted, impervious, non-swelling soil placed before foundation construction adds uniform surcharge that reduces uneven swelling, and the pad is graded so water can never pond against the structure. After construction, the homeowner inherits the moisture regime. The maintenance program typically requires maintaining positive slopes away from the house, keeping gutters and downspouts functional and discharging away from the foundation, avoiding constant watering of beds against the slab, and managing trees — not planting large species at the foundation line and not removing mature trees without considering the rebound swelling that follows. Crawl-space homes need positive drainage and ventilation beneath the floor. None of this is optional detailing; on expansive soil, the foundation and the water management are one system, and the AHJ's inspection ends while the homeowner's maintenance runs for the life of the house.

What else do project teams ask?

Why do Norman foundations need a geotechnical report?
Norman sits on Permian clays and claystones with significant and highly variable swell potential. The report's borings and lab testing determine the active zone depth, expected movement, foundation type, and earthwork requirements for the specific lot. Without it, the structural engineer is guessing at the soil parameters the entire design depends on.
How many borings does a residential lot need?
Standard practice is a minimum of two borings per site, typically 15 to 20 feet deep, with deeper borings where trees are present or were recently removed. Subsoils can vary significantly within one lot, so design parameters are based on the weaker boring conditions.
What is the active zone in expansive soil?
The active zone is the depth of soil affected by seasonal moisture change — the layer that swells and shrinks. Foundations must either be stiff enough to bridge movement within it or be carried on piers that extend below it to stable strata. The geotechnical investigation determines its depth on each site.
Do trees really damage foundations on expansive clay?
Yes. Large trees draw substantial moisture from clay in summer, causing localized shrinkage and settlement near the foundation, and removing a mature tree can trigger rebound swelling. Borings are deepened near trees, and the geotechnical report addresses tree effects, setbacks, and root barriers as part of the foundation design.
