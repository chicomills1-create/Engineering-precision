Food Plant Ammonia Refrigeration Design (IIAR 2 / ASHRAE 15)

Ammonia refrigeration is the workhorse of food processing — the most thermodynamically efficient common refrigerant, cheap as a commodity, and proven over a century of industrial use. It's also toxic at high concentrations, which is why ammonia system design is one of the most code-intensive engineering disciplines in a food plant: ASHRAE 15, IIAR 2, ASME B31.5, and the International Mechanical Code all converge on the machinery room. Done right, ammonia delivers the lowest operating cost in the industry. Done casually, it creates the plant's single largest life-safety exposure.

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran

System design: loads, architecture, efficiency

An ammonia system starts with the load: process cooling (blast freezing, chill rooms, tempering), cold storage, and the pulldown and product loads that define compressor and evaporator sizing. Engineers calculate each load — transmission, product, infiltration, internal — and select saturated suction temperatures that balance efficiency against equipment cost. This is where operating cost is won or lost for the next 25 years: a system designed at the right suction pressures uses measurably less energy than one sized by rule of thumb.

System architecture follows the loads: direct-expansion or recirculated (overfeed) ammonia, single or two-stage compression, economized screw compressors, evaporative condensers sized for the local design wet-bulb. For food plants, recirculated overfeed systems dominate — they handle the large, variable loads of processing and storage efficiently, and they keep liquid ammonia management in the machinery room where it belongs.

The machinery room: where the codes converge

The machinery room is where the codes converge. IIAR 2 (Safe Design of Closed-Circuit Ammonia Refrigeration Systems) governs: room construction and tightness, emergency ventilation sized to the refrigerant charge, gas detection with staged alarms, emergency shutdown of the system, and electrical classification. ASHRAE 15 adds occupancy and refrigerant concentration limits; ASME B31.5 governs the piping; the mechanical code adopts IIAR by reference in most jurisdictions.

Ventilation design deserves emphasis: normal ventilation controls heat and minor leaks; emergency ventilation — sized in the hundreds of air changes per hour equivalent — must clear a major release fast enough to keep concentrations below dangerous levels. Detection placement follows the physics: ammonia vapor is lighter than air and rises, so sensors go high — but machinery room geometry, airflow patterns, and potential liquid pooling all shape the final layout. This is engineered, permitted, and inspected. It is not a contractor's field call.

Pressure relief is the other life-safety system: relief valves on vessels and piping sections that can be isolated, piped to a safe exterior termination — never into the room. Relief discharge calculations and termination locations are plan-review items. Your engineer should show you the relief routing on the drawings before the permit reviewer asks.

Piping, process spaces, and commissioning

Piping design for ammonia is unforgiving: proper material (steel, never copper alloys), welded joints, adequate supports for the weight of liquid-filled pipe, and thermal movement handled without stressing joints. Oil management in screw compressor systems, proper suction trapping and riser design, and liquid-line subcooling all separate reliable plants from troublesome ones.

Refrigeration and food safety intersect in the process spaces: evaporator placement must avoid drip onto exposed product, defrost water needs drainage that can't contaminate, and room construction around coils must be cleanable. In USDA-inspected plants, FSIS evaluates the refrigeration installation as part of sanitary design — coil placement, drain routing, and condensation control are inspection items, not just comfort items.

Commissioning closes the loop: pressure testing, evacuation, charge verification, controls checkout, alarm and ventilation testing, and operator training. An ammonia plant's first charge is a milestone that should be boring — every test passed, every alarm verified, every operator trained. Engineer the commissioning plan into the project, not around it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is ammonia cheaper than synthetic refrigerants?

For large cold storage and process cooling loads, ammonia typically wins on operating cost — its thermodynamic efficiency beats synthetics, and refrigerant cost is negligible. The tradeoff is the safety and code compliance layer: machinery room design, ventilation, detection, and relief systems per IIAR 2 and ASHRAE 15. A good engineer prices both sides honestly.

Does an ammonia plant need gas detection?

Yes. IIAR 2 requires emergency ventilation, gas detection with alarms, and often emergency shutdown interlocks in machinery rooms. Detection placement, alarm thresholds, and ventilation rates are engineered — not guesswork.

What are low-charge ammonia systems?

Low-charge ammonia systems, ammonia/CO₂ cascade, and packaged systems reduce the charge in occupied spaces and can simplify code compliance. They're worth evaluating for plants where a traditional large-charge system creates siting problems.

Should I consider CO₂ instead of ammonia?

CO₂ (transcritical or cascade) is increasingly competitive, especially where ammonia's toxicity creates permitting or insurance friction. Many new plants use ammonia/CO₂ cascade: ammonia stays in the machinery room, CO₂ serves the process spaces. Your engineer should model both.
