How Is Lighting Designed for Supermarket Sales Floors and Cases?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers layer high-CRI merchandising light by department with efficient general lighting, specify low-heat LED case lighting with dimming, and use daylight and occupancy controls to meet energy codes.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Merchandising Light Quality by Department

Different departments need different light. Produce wants high color rendering in a neutral to slightly cool temperature that makes greens vivid; meat needs warm, high-CRI light that renders reds appetizingly without the old heat-lamp trick; bakery glows under warm light that suggests fresh from the oven. The engineer writes a lighting narrative by department with target color temperatures, CRI minimums, and illuminance levels, then selects fixtures and optics to deliver it. Glare control matters as much as color. Shoppers should see product, not fixtures — so the design uses shielded downlights, asymmetric optics that wash shelving vertically, and careful aiming at end caps. Vertical illuminance on the shelf face is the real metric in a grocery store, not just horizontal foot-candles on the floor, and the engineer models it before fixtures are ordered.

Case Lighting, Controls, and Energy Code

Refrigerated case lighting lives inside the cold envelope, so heat is the enemy. Modern LED case strips produce far less heat than the fluorescents they replaced while rendering product better, and adding door-open or occupancy dimming cuts the remaining heat during low-traffic hours. The engineer coordinates case lighting with the refrigeration design because the wattage shows up directly in the case load. Controls compliance is documented against the energy code: daylight zones near skylights and glazing with automatic dimming, occupancy or vacancy sensors in back-of-house spaces, scheduled shutoff, and lighting power density calculations by space type. Commissioning includes a night walk — every scene, sensor, and daylight response verified with a light meter, not just assumed from the submittal.

Supermarket Lighting Design Checklist

A complete lighting design sells product and satisfies the energy code: • Department-by-department lighting narrative: color temperature, CRI, and illuminance • Vertical shelf-face illuminance modeled, not just horizontal foot-candles • Low-heat LED case lighting with occupancy dimming inside the cold envelope • Glare control through shielded fixtures and aimed accent lighting • Daylight harvesting at skylights and entrance glazing with dimming zones • Occupancy sensors and scheduled scenes for back-of-house and overnight stocking • Lighting power density calculations and full controls commissioning

What else do project teams ask?

What color temperature works best for grocery store departments?
There is no single answer — that is the point of departmental design. Bakery and prepared foods read best warm, around 2700 to 3000K, suggesting fresh-baked warmth. Produce and seafood look freshest under neutral 3500 to 4000K with very high color rendering. Meat needs warm high-CRI light tuned to render reds. Dry goods aisles can run neutral for efficient general lighting. The engineer documents the palette per department so the store feels cohesive while each product looks its best.
How does case lighting affect refrigeration energy?
Directly: every watt of lighting inside a refrigerated case becomes heat the refrigeration system must remove, plus the lighting energy itself. Switching fluorescent case lights to LED typically cuts case lighting power by half or more and removes the associated refrigeration load. Adding occupancy dimming — bright when shoppers approach, dim when aisles are empty — cuts it further. In a store with hundreds of linear feet of cases, the combined savings are significant.
Do supermarkets benefit from skylights and daylighting?
Yes, when designed carefully. Skylights with daylight harvesting dim the electric lighting during the day, cutting both lighting and cooling energy. The cautions are glare, UV fading of packaging, and heat gain — the engineer specifies diffusing skylights with UV filtering, models the daylight zones for the controls, and accounts for the added cooling load. Done right, daylight makes the store feel better and the lighting bill drops.
What lighting controls does the energy code require in grocery stores?
Typical requirements include automatic daylight dimming in daylit zones, occupancy or vacancy sensors in offices, restrooms, and stockrooms, scheduled automatic shutoff, and lighting power density limits by space type — with acceptance testing to prove the controls work. Grocery-specific nuances like case lighting controls and sales-floor scheduling scenes are documented in the compliance forms. The engineer sizes the control system to the code and commissions it so the store passes inspection the first time.
