How Is Feedlot Manure Management Engineered Responsibly?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Manure is managed as a material stream: scheduled pen scraping, contained stockpiling pads with runoff capture, composting or direct haul matched to the land base, and a nutrient management plan with documented agronomic application rates that satisfies the permit.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Collection Schedules and Stockpile Design

The engineer sets the scraping program from pen conditions and equipment reality: pens scraped on a rhythm that keeps surfaces firm and dry, with the frequency tuned to season — wetter months need more passes — and to pen density. The haul route from pens to stockpile is drawn on the site plan with all-weather surfacing, because a stockpile the loader cannot reach in mud is a stockpile that does not get used. The stockpile pad is sized for the longest interval between haul-outs plus a weather contingency, and the engineer details it as containment: sloped to a collection point tied into the runoff system, with curbs or berms where the state requires them. Access ramps and turning areas are designed for the actual loader and truck combination, and the engineer separates the stockpile's drainage from clean areas completely. Where composting is chosen, the design adds windrow pads with turning lanes, water for moisture control, and space for the curing and finished-product piles.

Nutrient Planning and Land Application

The nutrient management plan is the engineering document that ties manure to land. The engineer compiles manure nutrient analyses, soil tests, and crop uptake data into field-by-field application rates — typically expressed as tons per acre for a given crop — that supply nutrients without exceeding what the crop removes. Fields that test high in phosphorus get reduced or zero rates; the plan rotates applications so no field accumulates excess. Setbacks and timing rules come from the permit and the county: distances from wells, streams, and property lines, plus restrictions on applying before forecasted rain or on frozen ground. The engineer maps every setback as a no-apply zone on the field maps the applicator actually uses. Calibration of spreaders, scale tickets or load counts, weather notes, and field records are designed into the workflow — the engineer provides the record forms or digital fields alongside the plan, because a plan nobody can document is a plan that fails its first inspection.

Feedlot Manure Management Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the manure system is finalized: • Pen scraping frequency set by season, pen density, and the operator's equipment • All-weather haul route from pens to stockpile drawn on the site plan • Stockpile pad sized for the longest haul-out interval plus weather contingency • Pad sloped to the runoff collection system; curbs or berms per state requirements • Composting windrow area designed with turning access and moisture control, if used • Nutrient management plan with field-by-field agronomic application rates • Setbacks from wells, waterways, and neighbors mapped as no-apply zones • Spreader calibration, load records, and weather documentation built into the workflow • State permit conditions and county rules reconciled in the plan

What else do project teams ask?

How often should feedlot pens be scraped?
Often enough to keep pen surfaces firm and dry — the exact frequency depends on pen density, rainfall, and season, and the engineer sets it with the operator's equipment in mind. The goal is removing wet, loose material while preserving the firm pack underneath; scraping too aggressively exposes loose soil and makes pens worse.
Does manure stockpiled outdoors pollute groundwater?
It can, which is why stockpiles are engineered. The pad is sloped to capture leachate into the runoff system, setbacks separate piles from wells and waterways, and the nutrient plan controls where the material ultimately goes. An uncontained pile on bare ground is a pollution source; a designed stockpile is a managed inventory.
Is composting feedlot manure worth it?
It depends on the operation's land base, markets, and odor situation. Composting reduces volume, kills weed seeds and pathogens with heat, and produces a more marketable, less odorous product — at the cost of pad space, turning equipment time, and moisture management. The engineer sizes the composting area and weighs it against direct haul-and-spread for the site's economics.
Who regulates manure application — the county or the state?
The state discharge permit sets the nutrient management requirements — rates, setbacks, timing, records — for permitted operations, while the county may add its own setbacks or hauling rules through zoning. The engineer writes the plan to satisfy both and keeps the documentation organized for whichever inspector arrives.
