How Are Feedlot Feed Bunks Designed for Efficient Rations?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Feed bunks are engineered for the ration program: enough linear bunk for every animal to eat at once, a reachable throat height with a self-draining floor, a wide concrete apron on the cattle side, and a feed alley laid out for the delivery equipment.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Bunk Space and Cross-Section Geometry

The engineer starts with the nutritionist's program, because bunk requirements follow the ration. Full-fed finishing cattle typically need nine to twelve inches of bunk per head; limit-fed, backgrounding, or growing programs — where every animal must get its share in a short window — need more, sometimes twice as much. The engineer converts head count and program into total linear footage per pen and checks it against the pen's fence line, since the bunk has to fit the pen it serves. Cross-section geometry is drawn to the animal. The throat — the opening cattle reach through — sits at a height mature cattle can eat from without strain, roughly 18 to 24 inches, and the bunk floor is deep and wide enough to hold the full delivery without feed piling against the back and spoiling. The engineer keeps the design simple and smooth inside: no ledges, pockets, or rough joints where old feed lodges and molds, because yesterday's spoiled feed in the bunk is today's intake problem.

Aprons, Drainage, and Feed Delivery Access

The concrete apron is the bunk's foundation in every sense. Ten to fourteen feet of concrete on the cattle side gives firm footing in all weather, a surface the crew can scrape and wash, and a clean zone where spilled feed can be recovered instead of lost to mud. The engineer slopes the apron away from the bunk at about one to two percent and ties it into the pen's overall grading, so water never stands against the bunk line. The feed alley is designed as a working roadway. The engineer sizes its width and turning areas for the actual delivery equipment — mixer trucks need more room than tractor-drawn wagons — and surfaces it for all-weather use with drainage that keeps the alley passable in rain. Overhead clearance is checked for the truck's raised body, and the engineer coordinates the alley layout with the pen gates and the loadout area so feed trucks, cattle trucks, and pen riders are not fighting for the same lanes at the same hour.

Feedlot Feed Bunk Design Checklist

Use this checklist before bunk construction: • Linear bunk footage confirmed against head count and the nutritionist's feeding program • Bunk throat height set for comfortable cattle reach, roughly 18 to 24 inches • Bunk floor sloped for washout and rain drainage; interior smooth with no feed-trapping pockets • Concrete apron 10 to 14 feet wide on the cattle side, sloped away from the bunk • Apron-to-bunk joint detailed against cracking and spoiled-feed buildup • Feed alley sized and surfaced for the actual delivery equipment, with drainage and turning room • Bunk type (precast concrete, steel, J-bunk, fence-line) matched to equipment and pen layout • Cleaning and inspection access designed in, so bunks stay clean in daily practice

What else do project teams ask?

How much bunk space does each animal need?
Nine to twelve inches per head is typical for full-fed finishing cattle, with more — sometimes double — for limit-fed or backgrounding programs where every animal must eat its share quickly. The engineer confirms the number with the nutritionist, since the feeding program, not a rule of thumb, sets the requirement.
Are concrete or steel feed bunks better?
Precast concrete dominates feedlots for good reason: it is heavy, durable, smooth, and easy to clean, and it stands up to cattle pressure and weather. Steel bunks cost less up front but need corrosion protection where manure and moisture attack them. The engineer weighs installed cost, expected life, and cleanability for the site's conditions.
Why do feed bunks need concrete aprons?
Because the ground at the bunk is the highest-traffic spot in the pen. Without concrete, hoof action turns it into mud, spilled feed is lost into the muck, and cattle stand uncomfortably while eating. The apron gives firm footing, a cleanable surface, and a zone where spilled feed can be recovered.
How do you keep feed bunks clean?
By designing for cleaning: smooth interiors with no pockets, sloped floors that wash out, aprons the crew can scrape, and a bunk line the operator can walk and inspect quickly. The engineer also confirms the feeding program leaves bunks slick — cleaned out — on schedule, since stale feed in the bunk depresses intake.
