How Are Bridges Designed Across the Tennessee River?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Tennessee River bridges are designed around the waterway: deep foundations like drilled shafts or piles socketed into rock below the riverbed, piers shaped to shed flood debris, spans sized for navigation channels and barge traffic, and corrosion protection for submerged steel and concrete, with hydraulic modeling of the river and permits from the transportation agencies that own the crossing.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Scour, Rock Sockets, and Building in the River

Scour analysis drives the foundation depth on every Tennessee River pier. The engineer models the design floods, the 100-year event and the more extreme check flood, computing how deep the current will excavate around each pier considering the pier's shape, the flow angle, and the bed material. That computed scour depth is not where the foundation stops: the deep elements extend well below it into competent material, so that even if the analysis proves optimistic, the pier still stands on rock or on piles with adequate remaining embedment. Streamlined pier noses reduce both the scour and the debris snagging that worsens it. Building the substructure in a working river is its own discipline. Cofferdams, interlocking steel sheet-pile enclosures, are driven around each pier location, sealed, and dewatered so the footing excavation and concrete placement happen in the dry. The design accounts for unbalanced water pressure on the cofferdam, flood events during construction, and environmental containment so concrete wash water and sediments never reach the river. Drilled shafts socketed into rock are verified by inspection of the socket and cleaning of the shaft bottom before concrete placement, because a shaft bearing on cuttings instead of rock is a foundation failure cast in concrete.

Navigation Spans, Barge Impact, and Corrosion

The navigation span is sized for the waterway's commerce. The Coast Guard sets the required horizontal and vertical clearances for the channel based on the design vessel, typically multi-barge tows on the Tennessee, and the engineer spans that opening with a structure type that clears it economically. Piers adjacent to the channel get vessel collision design: the analysis assigns an impact force from the design barge at the design speed, and the pier is either engineered to survive it or shielded by fender systems and dolphin clusters that absorb the energy before it reaches the concrete. Navigation lighting marks the channel span for towboat pilots around the clock. Corrosion protection is a service-life calculation. The splash zone, where wetting and drying concentrate chlorides and oxygen, is the most aggressive exposure on the bridge, and the engineer answers with layered defense: low-permeability concrete, corrosion-resistant or coated reinforcement, high-performance coating systems on structural steel, and cathodic protection for critical submerged elements where justified. Bearings and expansion joints, the moving parts that accommodate thermal and seismic movement, are detailed for inspection access and future replacement, because a bridge designed for a 75- to 100-year life must have its wearable components renewable. Seismic detailing for the New Madrid zone completes the picture in West Tennessee, with ductile piers and restrained bearings keeping the spans on their supports in the design earthquake.

What else do project teams ask?

What is bridge scour and how is it designed for?
Scour is the excavation of riverbed material around piers and abutments by flood currents, and it has caused more bridge failures than any other single hazard. The engineer computes expected scour depths for the design floods using established hydraulic methods, shapes piers to minimize it, and founds every pier on deep elements extending well below the total scour depth into rock or deep competent material. The foundation is designed so the bridge stands even if the riverbed around it washes away to the computed depth.
Why do river piers get special shapes?
A blunt pier facing the current creates turbulence that digs the riverbed out around it and catches floating debris, which worsens the scour and adds lateral load. Streamlined pier noses, pointed or rounded cutwaters aligned with the flow, smooth the water's passage, reduce the scour hole, and shed logs and debris instead of collecting them. On the Tennessee River, where flood debris can include entire trees, that shedding shape is a structural safety feature, not an aesthetic choice.
How does barge traffic affect bridge design?
The navigation channel span must clear the design tow's width and height as set by the Coast Guard, which often makes it the longest and most expensive span on the bridge. Piers near the channel are designed for vessel collision: either the pier itself resists the barge impact force, or fender and dolphin systems absorb the energy first. Navigation lighting, channel markers, and pier protection are all part of the permitted design the Coast Guard reviews.
Who approves a bridge over the Tennessee River?
Several agencies, each with its own authority: the state department of transportation owns the crossing and approves the structural design, the Coast Guard issues the bridge permit governing navigation clearances, and the Army Corps of Engineers reviews dredge and fill work in the river. Environmental reviews run alongside. The owning transportation agency is the AHJ for the bridge design itself, and construction cannot proceed until every agency with jurisdiction has issued its approval.
