How Are Baltimore Alley Houses Rehabilitated?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Baltimore alley houses are rehabilitated by stabilizing narrow masonry walls, underpinning or rebuilding settled foundations, reframing floors for residential loads, adding damp-proofing against rising moisture, and upgrading mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems - permitted through Baltimore City's plan review process.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Narrow Walls, Shared Walls, Settlement

An alley house is essentially two party walls with a building between them, so the structural assessment starts with the masonry. The engineer documents wall thickness - often a single wythe - mortar condition, bulging, and separation at the front and rear, working from inside both the subject house and, with permission, the neighbors'. Crack gauges distinguish active settlement from movement that stopped decades ago. Foundations are typically shallow brick or stone footings on variable urban fill, and the engineer probes bearing conditions where settlement is evident, designing underpinning to a competent stratum in sequenced sections that never leave the wall unsupported. The floor system is the second structural scope. Original joists in these houses are frequently undersized, over-spanned, and damaged at the bearing ends by the same moisture that attacks the walls. The engineer designs reframing with modern joists sized for residential loads, new sill plates on the repaired masonry, and solid blocking and bridging for stability. Where the narrow span still cannot deliver a stiff floor, a mid-span bearing wall or beam on new footings is added. Stair openings - often brutally cut through the original framing - are reframed with proper headers and trimmers so the stair does not hang from hope.

Moisture and Systems in Tight Footprints

Water management is structural work in an alley house. The engineer specifies damp-proofing or waterproofing at the foundation walls, interior or exterior depending on access, and details the drainage path: regraded alley surfaces, gutters and downspouts that discharge away from the building, and sump systems where the water table demands them. Rotted sill plates and joist ends are replaced, and the new wood is isolated from the masonry with capillary breaks so the cycle does not restart. Ventilation of crawl spaces and wall cavities is designed to keep the repaired assembly dry. MEP design works within inches. High-efficiency compact furnaces or heat pumps, tankless water heaters, and carefully stacked plumbing keep chases small; the engineer reviews every framing penetration so pipes and ducts do not compromise the new joists. Electrical service is brought up to modern capacity with proper grounding, and the panel location is coordinated with the structural layout. Fire separation at the party walls is maintained or restored - a shared wall that has been pierced by generations of improvised utilities gets its rating back through listed firestopping at every penetration.

What else do project teams ask?

What is an alley house?
A small rowhouse - typically twelve to fourteen feet wide and two stories - built on the alleys behind Baltimore's main streets, originally as workers' housing. They share party walls with neighbors on both sides, sit close to grade, and are among the city's oldest residential buildings. Their narrow plans and soft-brick construction make structural rehabilitation a specialized task.
How are settled alley-house foundations fixed?
With underpinning in short sequenced sections. The engineer excavates beneath the existing brick or stone footing a few feet at a time, pours new concrete down to competent soil, and packs the joint before moving to the next section - so the wall above is never left unsupported. Where footings are too deteriorated to underpin, partial foundation replacement is designed instead.
Why do alley houses have so much moisture damage?
They sit near grade with little or no original damp-proofing, so groundwater wicks up through the brick by capillary action and rots every piece of wood it reaches - sills, joist ends, and stair framing. Tight alleys trap water against the walls, and generations of failed gutters add roof water to the mix. The rehab breaks the cycle with damp-proofing, drainage, capillary breaks, and replacement of decayed wood.
Does historic district review apply to alley houses?
It can. Many alley houses sit within Baltimore's historic districts, where the Commission for Historical and Architectural Preservation reviews exterior changes - windows, doors, and rear additions. The review does not change the structural requirements, but it affects the timeline and constrains exterior alterations. The engineer confirms district status with the city early so the preservation review runs in parallel with structural design.
