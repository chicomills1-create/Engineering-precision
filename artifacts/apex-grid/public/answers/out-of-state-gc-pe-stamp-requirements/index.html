Can an Out-of-State GC Get a PE Stamp in Arizona, California, Texas, Nevada, or Florida?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

An out-of-state GC can build in AZ, CA, TX, NV, or FL, but the permit set must be sealed by a PE licensed in that state after a genuine review under responsible charge. Engage the in-state reviewing PE during design — comity licensure takes too long to be a permit strategy.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The five-state licensing picture

Each state runs its own board and its own rules, and the GC needs to know the shape of each. Arizona's Board of Technical Registration licenses engineers and enforces sealing standards; firms practicing there need to be properly registered. California licenses professional engineers by discipline and expects seismic and Title 24 competence baked into the sealed design — an out-of-state engineer who has never detailed for high seismic gets an education from plan check. Texas combines individual PE licensure with firm registration (the F-number) and the responsible-charge framework of Rule 137.33. Nevada's board mirrors the standard model with its own sealing rules. Florida's Board of Professional Engineers administers some of the most explicit thresholds in the country for when a PE seal is required, and Florida reviewers enforce them literally. The practical upshot for the out-of-state GC: there is no reciprocity shortcut that puts a seal on your drawings. Every state's board requires its own license for the sealing engineer, and every AHJ checks. Build the licensing verification into your preconstruction checklist for each new state — confirm the PE's license on the board's website, confirm firm registration where required — and you will never have a submittal bounced on credentials.

Sequencing the in-state review without losing the schedule

The mistake is treating the in-state PE as a final checkpoint. The winning sequence brings the reviewing engineer in while design is still fluid — ideally at schematic or design development — so the local-code translation happens when changes are cheap. The PE reviews the structural concept against local seismic, wind, or soils practice; reviews the MEP concept against the local energy code; and flags the AHJ-specific submittal requirements the home-state team has never seen. By the time the permit set is drawn, the design already reflects what the local reviewers expect, and the final review-and-seal is verification rather than discovery. I also recommend the GC get a pre-submittal read from the in-state PE on the AHJ itself: which jurisdiction, what their current checklist requires, how they handle phased or deferred submittals, and what their correction patterns look like. A Houston ProjectDox submittal, a Phoenix electronic plan review, and a Miami-Dade review are different processes with different expectations, and the in-state engineer knows all three. That intelligence, delivered during design, is worth more than any expediting service after submittal.

Out-of-state GC market-entry checklist

Do these before you promise the owner a permit date in a new state. • Engage a PE licensed in the project state during design — not at permit intake — for review-and-seal • Verify the PE's license on the state board site; in Texas, verify the firm's F-number as well • Have the in-state PE review the design concept early for local code translation (seismic, wind, soils, energy) • Confirm the AHJ's submittal checklist, phased-submittal policy, and digital seal acceptance with local knowledge • Do not plan around comity licensure timelines — pursue comity in parallel, but permit off the in-state engagement

What else do project teams ask?

Can my home-state PE get licensed in the project state in time for my permit?
Sometimes, but do not bet the schedule on it. Comity (licensure by endorsement) typically takes several weeks to several months depending on the state, the completeness of the application, and whether the engineer already holds an NCEES record — and the engineer cannot lawfully seal until the license is issued. Meanwhile the design still needs a full review under responsible charge before sealing. The reliable path is to engage a PE already licensed in the project state for the review-and-seal, and let your home-state engineer pursue comity in parallel for future work. I have seen GCs lose a month waiting on a comity application that was never going to beat the permit deadline.
Does my out-of-state GC license affect the engineering requirements?
No — contractor licensing and engineering licensure are separate tracks, and being licensed as a GC in the project state (or working under the right exemptions) does not change what the building department requires for sealed engineering. The AHJ checks the seal block for a valid in-state PE license regardless of who holds the contractor's license. Some GCs assume that qualifying the company to build in the new state covers the engineering; it does not. Treat the in-state PE engagement as its own workstream with its own timeline.
What local code differences trip up out-of-state design teams most?
The big ones are seismic detailing in California, wind and flood provisions in Florida and the Gulf Coast, expansive-soil foundation practice in Texas and Arizona, and Title 24 energy compliance in California — each one is a domain where the local AHJ's expectations are specific and non-negotiable. An in-state reviewing PE catches these during review-and-seal: the lateral detailing gets brought up to what California plan check expects, the energy documentation gets reconciled to Title 24, the Florida wind provisions get verified. This local translation is half the value of the in-state engagement; the seal is the other half.
Can one in-state PE seal a multi-state GC's entire portfolio?
Only for projects in the state where that PE is licensed. A Texas-licensed PE seals your Texas work; your Arizona projects need an Arizona-licensed PE, your Florida projects a Florida-licensed PE, and so on. Multi-state GCs should build a bench — either a firm licensed in all five states with PEs in responsible charge in each, or a vetted PE relationship per state. I work with GCs running AZ/TX/FL pipelines who keep one engineering partner across all three states precisely so the review process, the deliverables, and the schedule expectations stay consistent even as the license behind the seal changes.
