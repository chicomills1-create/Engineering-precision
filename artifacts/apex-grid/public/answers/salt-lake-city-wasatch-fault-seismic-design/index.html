How Are Buildings Designed for the Wasatch Fault in Salt Lake City?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Salt Lake City buildings are designed for Wasatch Fault shaking under ASCE 7, with ductile lateral systems such as special moment frames or shear walls, diaphragms and foundations detailed for seismic forces, liquefaction mitigation for soft lakebed soils, and bracing of nonstructural components - all reviewed by the city or county AHJ.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Wasatch Fault Hazard

The Wasatch Fault runs along the base of the Wasatch Range in segments, with the Salt Lake City segment passing just east of downtown. It is a normal fault - the valley side drops relative to the mountain side - and geologic studies show it has produced large surface-rupturing earthquakes in the past, which is why the building code treats the corridor as a high seismic hazard. A rupture on the Salt Lake City segment would shake the entire valley, and the soft lakebed soils would amplify that shaking beyond what the same earthquake would produce on rock. The secondary hazard is liquefaction. Much of the valley floor is saturated sand and silt deposited by ancient Lake Bonneville, and strong shaking can turn those layers temporarily liquid - buildings settle, tilt, or shift, and buried utilities float upward. The Utah Geological Survey publishes liquefaction-susceptibility mapping that the geotechnical engineer uses as a starting point, but the design always rests on site-specific borings and analysis. Areas near the Jordan River and the valley's low-lying west side deserve the closest scrutiny.

Seismic Design of the Structure

The engineer begins by establishing the seismic design category, which flows from the mapped spectral accelerations, the site class, and the risk category of the occupancy. That category then dictates the permitted lateral systems, the required level of detailing, and the analysis procedure. For a typical mid-rise in the valley, the design will use a ductile system - special moment frames or special shear walls - with reinforcement detailed to confine the concrete and let the steel yield in a controlled way. The analysis checks both strength and drift: the building must resist the code's seismic forces and must not sway beyond the code's drift limits. Diaphragm design gets special attention in concrete and wood-frame buildings alike, because the diaphragm is what collects the earthquake forces from each floor and delivers them to the walls and frames. The engineer sizes the diaphragm, its chords, and its collectors, then traces the complete load path down through the lateral system and into the foundation. Connections are designed for overstrength forces where the code requires it, so that a connection does not fail before the ductile element it serves has a chance to yield. Structural observation during construction verifies that the as-built detailing matches the drawings, because seismic performance lives in the details.

Liquefaction and Foundation Design

When the geotechnical report identifies liquefiable layers, the foundation design has to answer two questions: will the building settle or tilt unacceptably, and will lateral spreading pull the foundation apart. The answers come from ground improvement, deep foundations, or both. Stone columns densify the soil and give pore water a drainage path; deep foundations carry the building's weight past the liquefiable layer into competent material below. The structural engineer then designs the pile caps, grade beams, and connections for the downdrag and lateral forces the code assigns to liquefaction effects. Shallow foundations are still possible on some valley sites where the liquefiable layer is thin, deep, or absent - the geotechnical engineer makes that call from the borings. Mat foundations can also work, spreading the building's weight so that modest settlement stays uniform rather than differential. Whatever the system, the permit submittal includes the geotechnical report, the liquefaction analysis, and the foundation design as one package, and the AHJ - Salt Lake City for sites inside city limits, Salt Lake County for unincorporated areas - reviews them together before deciding on approval.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the Wasatch Fault and how large an earthquake can it produce?
The Wasatch Fault is an active normal fault system running along the base of the Wasatch Range, with the Salt Lake City segment passing just east of downtown. Geologic evidence shows it has produced large surface-rupturing earthquakes in the past, and the building code treats the corridor as a high seismic hazard. Engineers design to the code's ground-motion values rather than to any single scenario earthquake.
What seismic design category applies in Salt Lake City?
Most of the valley falls in the higher seismic design categories because of the fault's proximity and the soft lakebed soils, but the exact category depends on the mapped shaking at the site, the soil site class from the geotechnical report, and the building's occupancy and risk category. The engineer establishes it early because it controls which lateral systems are permitted and how rigorous the detailing must be.
How is liquefaction handled in the Salt Lake Valley?
The geotechnical engineer evaluates liquefaction potential from borings and groundwater data. Where the hazard is confirmed, mitigation typically means ground improvement such as stone columns, deep foundations that bypass the liquefiable layer, or mat foundations designed for uniform settlement - with the structural details accounting for downdrag and lateral spreading. The AHJ reviews the geotechnical recommendations and the structural design together.
Does the permit process differ between Salt Lake City and unincorporated Salt Lake County?
Yes. Sites inside Salt Lake City go through the city's building services division, while unincorporated county sites go through Salt Lake County planning and development services. Both enforce the state-adopted building code with the IBC's seismic provisions, but submittal portals, review timelines, fees, and inspection scheduling differ. The engineer confirms the authority having jurisdiction before the design begins.
