How Does Electrical Area Classification Work at a Texas Chemical Plant?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Electrical area classification at a Texas chemical plant maps every location where flammable gases, vapors, or dusts may be present into classified zones under the National Electrical Code, then requires explosion-proof or intrinsically safe equipment and wiring methods in those zones - documented on area classification drawings reviewed by the AHJ.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

How Classified Boundaries Are Drawn

The classification study begins with the process: what chemicals are handled, at what temperatures and pressures, and where they can escape. For each release source, the engineer evaluates the quantity and dispersion of a potential release, the ventilation - natural or mechanical - that dilutes it, and the topography and drainage that determine where heavier-than-air vapors travel. These factors set the extent of the classified area around each source, often drawn as graded boundaries on plan drawings with Division 1 closest to the source and Division 2 beyond it. Gas detection and ventilation are the levers that shrink classified areas. A well-ventilated compressor building with continuous gas detection can justify smaller Division 1 extents than the same building with stagnant air, because the engineering analysis shows ignitable concentrations cannot persist. The classification drawings also cover outdoor areas, tank farms, loading racks, and trenches where vapors collect. Because plants change - new units, different feedstocks - the classification is a living document, revalidated whenever the process changes.

What Classification Changes About the Design

Inside classified areas, ordinary electrical construction is prohibited. Enclosures must contain an internal explosion without igniting the surrounding atmosphere - the meaning of explosion-proof - or the circuit must be intrinsically safe, limited to energy levels too low to spark ignition. Conduit seals at boundary crossings stop vapor migration through the raceway system into unclassified areas, a failure mode that has caused real incidents. Lighting, heat trace, instrumentation, and communications all get rated equipment, and the engineer verifies temperature classes so no device surface runs hotter than the autoignition temperature of the chemicals present. The coordination burden is significant. Structural supports in classified areas need rated fastening approaches, cable tray needs covers or routing outside the classified envelope where practical, and large motors may need purged enclosures with interlocked ventilation. The electrical engineer issues the classification drawings early so every discipline designs to them, and the final installation is documented in as-built classification drawings that the plant keeps for the life of the facility - future projects start from them.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between Division 1 and Division 2?
Division 1 locations are areas where ignitable concentrations of flammable gas or vapor can exist under normal operating conditions - near open process equipment or frequent release sources. Division 2 locations are areas where ignitable concentrations exist only under abnormal conditions, such as a seal failure or spill. Division 1 demands the strictest equipment and wiring methods; Division 2 allows somewhat less restrictive - but still rated - installations.
What does explosion-proof actually mean?
An explosion-proof enclosure is built strong enough to contain an explosion of the specified gas inside it and cool the escaping gases through tight flame paths so they cannot ignite the atmosphere outside. It does not mean the device cannot spark internally - it means an internal spark cannot cause an external explosion. Every explosion-proof device is rated for specific gas groups and temperature classes.
Who draws the area classification drawings?
The electrical engineer of record, working from process data supplied by the plant's process engineers - chemical properties, release sources, operating conditions - and applying the National Electrical Code with API recommended practices. It is a licensed engineering deliverable, not a vendor sketch, because every downstream electrical decision depends on it.
Who inspects electrical work at a plant outside city limits?
In unincorporated Texas counties without a building department, there may be no municipal electrical inspector - the county fire marshal, the owner's insurance carrier, and OSHA process safety audits become the reviewers. The design still follows the NEC, and many owners hire third-party inspection to verify it. Inside cities like Houston or Beaumont, the city electrical inspector reviews and inspects through the permit process.
