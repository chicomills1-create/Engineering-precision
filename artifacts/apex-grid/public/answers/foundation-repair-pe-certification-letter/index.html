Do I Need a PE Letter for Foundation Repair?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Foundation repair needs a PE certification letter for permits, real-estate transactions, and insurance — the engineer evaluates the cause of distress, certifies the pier or underpinning design, and states any conditions like drainage corrections the repair depends on.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Diagnosing before designing

Every foundation letter I write starts with the evaluation, because the repair must answer the cause. I document the distress: crack locations, widths, and patterns — diagonal cracks at openings tell a different story than horizontal wall cracks — floor elevation surveys showing where the foundation has moved and by how much, and the site conditions driving it: drainage patterns, plumbing leak history, vegetation near the foundation, and the soil profile. In expansive clay markets I correlate the movement pattern with moisture behavior, because clay-driven distress has a signature — seasonal, differential, worst where drainage concentrates water or where large trees dry the soil. The evaluation concludes with a plain-statement diagnosis: what moved, why it moved, and how far the distress has progressed. That diagnosis then scopes the repair: pier or underpinning type, depths keyed to the soil's active zone, the load path from the existing foundation to the new supports, and the site corrections — drainage, moisture barriers, plumbing repairs — without which the soil will simply move the repaired foundation again. A repair design without a diagnosis is a guess with piers.

Certifying the repair design

The certification addresses the repair as an engineered system. I verify the pier or underpinning design: the pier capacities against the foundation loads they'll carry, the depths relative to the active moisture zone or bearing stratum, the spacing and layout covering the distressed areas, and the connection details transferring load from the existing foundation to the piers. The design calculations or the manufacturer's engineered data get reviewed — not accepted on faith — and the installation criteria are specified: depth verification, load testing where the design relies on it, and the lift sequence that brings the foundation back without overstressing it. The letter states the certification's conditions honestly. If the repair's long-term performance depends on correcting the drainage that caused the movement, the letter says so — the certification covers the structural repair, and the site work is its stated prerequisite. Post-repair, many engagements include a follow-up elevation survey to confirm the foundation stabilized, and the letter can reference that verification plan. Precision here is what makes the letter useful to the AHJ, the lender, and the owner alike: everyone knows exactly what the engineer stands behind.

Foundation repair letter checklist

A defensible foundation certification rests on the evaluation first and the repair design second. Letters that skip the diagnosis certify nothing meaningful. • Field evaluation: crack mapping, elevation survey, site drainage and soil conditions documented • Plain-statement diagnosis: what moved, the cause identified — expansive clay, drainage, plumbing, or original design • Repair design verified: pier type, depths below the active zone, capacities, spacing, and load-transfer details • Site corrections specified: drainage, moisture control, and plumbing repairs the long-term performance depends on • Lender and insurer questions answered specifically: post-repair performance stated, not implied • PE licensed in the property's state, with the letter sealed under responsible charge

What else do project teams ask?

What's the difference between a foundation evaluation and a repair certification?
The evaluation diagnoses: what type of distress, what caused it, how severe it is, and what repair approach the engineering supports. The certification states the PE's professional conclusion — that the specified repair design is adequate for the conditions found, based on the evaluation. Many engagements produce both in sequence: I evaluate first, then certify the repair design, whether it's the contractor's proposed system verified against my findings or a design I specify. Lenders usually want the certification; the evaluation is the engineering behind it. Be wary of letters that certify without evaluating — a certification with no stated basis is a signature without the engineering behind it.
Why is expansive clay such a focus in Texas and Arizona foundation letters?
Because it's the region's defining foundation hazard. The clay soils across much of Texas and parts of Arizona swell dramatically when wet and shrink when dry, and that seasonal movement cracks slabs, heaves pier-and-beam foundations, and distresses everything tied to the foundation. A PE letter in these markets has to address the soil: the evaluation identifies clay-driven movement from the crack patterns and elevation data, and the repair design — pier depths below the active moisture zone, plus moisture control — answers it. Letters that specify piers without addressing drainage and moisture management certify half a repair. Texas AHJs and the state's engineering board expect the soil addressed, not just the structure.
Will a PE certify a contractor's proposed pier system?
Only after verifying it's the right system for the conditions. I review the contractor's proposal — pier type, depths, spacing, load ratings — against my evaluation findings and the site's soils. If the system suits the cause and the design is adequate, I certify it, sometimes with specified modifications like deeper piers or added drainage work. If the proposal doesn't match the diagnosis — say, shallow piers specified where the active clay zone runs deep — I say so and specify what the engineering requires. The PE's certification follows the engineering, not the proposal; certifying an inadequate system to keep a job moving would violate the responsible charge behind every seal I place.
Do lenders accept foundation letters from any engineer?
Lenders want a letter from a licensed PE in the property's state, and increasingly they want specificity: the cause of distress stated, the repair design described, and the engineer's conclusion about post-repair performance clearly given. Generic 'foundation appears serviceable' letters get kicked back by underwriters who've seen too many of them. I write lender letters to the transaction's question — is this foundation a sound risk after the specified repair — with the evaluation basis shown. If the distress is beyond economical repair, the honest letter says that too; a lender letter that hides a failing foundation helps no one, least of all the buyer.
