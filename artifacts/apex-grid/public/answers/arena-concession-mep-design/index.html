What Does Concession Stand MEP Design Involve?

The concession stand is a commercial kitchen wearing a venue uniform: Type I cooking exhaust with fire suppression, grease waste plumbing, surge-capable water and drainage, dedicated electrical for cooking and refrigeration loads — all packed into a concourse footprint, coordinated with the building's HVAC, and designed for the halftime rush.

The concise answer

Mechanical: Type I (grease) exhaust hoods with fire-suppression systems over cooking equipment, make-up air that doesn't unbalance the concourse HVAC, and refrigeration heat rejection. Plumbing: grease interceptors sized to the menu, hot water for the warewashing surge, and drainage that handles the real flows. Electrical: dedicated circuits for cooking, refrigeration, and point-of-sale, on the venue's emergency system where the operations plan requires it. Fire protection coordinates the suppression, alarm, and egress for each stand.

The concourse coordination problem

Twenty concession stands each exhausting kitchen air will unbalance the concourse HVAC if the make-up air isn't designed as a system. The mechanical design treats concessions and concourse as one air balance: dedicated make-up air units for the kitchen exhaust, concourse pressurization that keeps cooking odors out of the seating areas, and controls that ramp with the event schedule. This is the MEP detail that separates venues that smell like popcorn (good) from venues that smell like fryer exhaust in the club level (bad).

Health code and operations

Concession design must satisfy the local health authority: hand sinks, warewashing, food-contact surfaces, and grease management per the food code — plus the venue operator's reality of high-volume, low-skill staffing. Durable finishes, hose-down capability, and equipment layouts the health inspector and the operations manager both approve.

Frequently asked questions

What exhaust does a concession stand need?
Cooking equipment producing grease-laden vapors requires Type I exhaust hoods with fire-suppression systems, plus dedicated make-up air. Non-cooking stands (prepackaged food, beverages) have much simpler requirements.
What is a grease interceptor?
A plumbing device that separates fats, oils, and grease from wastewater before it enters the sanitary system — sized to the menu and the fixture count, and required by most jurisdictions for food-service venues.
How do concessions affect concourse HVAC?
Kitchen exhaust removes large air volumes; without designed make-up air, the concourse goes negative — pulling in unconditioned air at entries and spreading cooking odors. The mechanical design balances the whole concourse as a system.
Do concession stands need fire suppression?
Cooking operations under Type I hoods require automatic fire-suppression systems tied to the fuel supply, plus portable extinguishers and coordination with the building fire alarm. The fire-protection engineer designs this with the kitchen layout.
