How Is MEP Designed for a Lodge on the Olympic Peninsula?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

An Olympic Peninsula lodge's MEP is designed for self-sufficiency in a rainforest: on-site well water and county-approved wastewater treatment, dedicated dehumidification with moisture-managed ventilation, heat-pump heating backed by grid-independent propane, and emergency power sized for multi-day outages.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

On-Site Water and Wastewater

The water system is engineered from source to tap. The engineer sizes the well pump and pressure system for the lodge's peak-hour demand — morning showers across full occupancy — plus the fire protection storage or flow the fire district requires, with treatment (filtration, disinfection, and any required contaminant removal) validated by water-quality testing. Storage tanks bridge the gap between well yield and peak demand, and the engineer designs the controls, alarms, and backup power for the pump so a power failure does not become a water failure. Cross-connection control protects the well from any contamination source on the site. Wastewater design follows the county health code path. The engineer performs the soil and site evaluation the county requires, sizes the septic tank and drainfield — or the advanced treatment unit where soils or setbacks demand it — for the lodge's design flows with the commercial-use factors the code applies to lodging. The system is sited above seasonal high groundwater with the separations the code mandates, stormwater is graded away from every component, and the engineer provides the reserve drainfield area the county requires. Monitoring ports and access risers are designed in so the system can be inspected and pumped without excavation.

Dehumidification, Heating Resilience, and Power

The moisture design treats the building as a system. The engineer sizes dehumidification for the latent load of full occupancy plus the infiltration the envelope allows, selects equipment rated for continuous damp-environment duty, and designs the condensate drainage with the same care as plumbing — failed condensate drains are a top source of lodge water damage. Ventilation air is preconditioned: incoming outdoor air passes through the dehumidification before reaching guest rooms, and the engineer balances the building slightly positive to keep humid air out of the envelope cavities. Materials in the mechanical design — stainless or coated drain pans, corrosion-resistant coils — are specified for the environment. Heating resilience means heat without the grid. The engineer designs the primary heat-pump system for efficiency across the peninsula's temperature range, then provides the propane backup with distribution that works during outages — gravity-friendly hydronics or battery-backed controls — sized to keep the building above freezing and the occupied zones comfortable. The emergency power system is tiered: life safety and freeze protection first, then water, wastewater, kitchen, and guest comfort, with fuel storage for the outage duration the site's utility history justifies. The engineer commissions the outage sequence — transfer, load pickup, and the manual procedures the caretaker follows — because a backup system nobody has tested is a hope, not a plan.

What else do project teams ask?

How does a remote lodge get water and sewer?
From on-site systems engineered for the site: a well with treatment, storage, and backup-powered pumping for water, and a county-health-department-approved septic or advanced-treatment system for wastewater. The engineer sizes both for peak lodge occupancy plus fire protection demands, sites them for the high water table and setbacks, and provides the operations manuals the county requires.
Why is dehumidification so critical on the Olympic Peninsula?
Because the outdoor air is near saturation much of the year, and ventilating with it — plus the moisture from dozens of showering guests — drives indoor humidity to mold-growing levels without active removal. The engineer designs dedicated dehumidification with preconditioned ventilation air, manages building pressurization to protect wall cavities, and specifies corrosion-resistant mechanical components for the damp duty.
What heats a lodge through a multi-day power outage?
A grid-independent backup: typically propane boilers or furnaces with distribution designed to work on battery-backed controls or gravity circulation, sized to keep pipes from freezing and occupied zones habitable. The engineer pairs this with emergency power for the well, wastewater, refrigeration, and controls, with fuel storage matched to the site's outage history.
How is equipment chosen for such a remote site?
For robustness and serviceability over sophistication: the engineer selects systems with local parts availability, simple diagnostics, and remote monitoring with alarms on the critical functions — well, septic, power, and heating — so the on-site caretaker gets early warning of failures and a traveling technician is rarely an emergency.
