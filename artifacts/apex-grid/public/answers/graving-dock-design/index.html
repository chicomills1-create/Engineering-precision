How Are Graving Docks Engineered for Major Ship Repair Work?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A graving dock is an excavated watertight concrete basin with a movable dock gate, engineered to be pumped dry against hydrostatic uplift. The floor carries concentrated keel-block ship loads, the gate seals against the sea, dewatering pumps handle the basin volume, and crane rails serve the repair operation — all in a marine corrosion environment.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The concise answer

A graving dock is a concrete basin engineered to be watertight, pumped dry, and loaded with a ship — in that order of structural priority. Uplift governs the floor, the gate governs operability, and the keel-block loads govern the floor's local design. Everything about a graving dock is permanent and unforgiving. The basin can't be easily modified after construction, so the design has to anticipate the largest vessel the dock will ever serve.

Where the engineering actually lives

The basin structure is mass concrete engineering: floor slabs several feet thick, walls designed for earth and water pressure in both the full and dewatered states, and waterstops at every construction joint. Uplift is resisted by the structure's weight, tension piles, or a combination — I verify the uplift case with the same rigor as any gravity design because it's the load that can destroy the dock. The dock gate and dewatering systems are mechanical-marine engineering: gate seating and sealing, pump capacity and redundancy, and the flooding and dewatering sequence. Crane rails along both copings need pile-supported foundations for the repair cranes' wheel loads.

Durability in constant marine exposure

The dock lives in seawater and gets pumped dry repeatedly — wet-dry cycling that accelerates corrosion. I specify marine-grade concrete, coated reinforcement, and protected steel, plus a maintenance plan for the gate seals and coatings. A graving dock is a fifty-year asset; the durability design is what gets it there. Graving dock design priorities.

  • Floor and walls engineered for full hydrostatic uplift in the dewatered state
  • Dock gate designed as a reliable movable marine structure
  • Dewatering pumps sized for the operational schedule with redundancy
  • Floor local design for concentrated keel-block and bilge-block loads
  • Crane rail foundations on piles along both copings
  • Marine durability: concrete, reinforcement, and coatings for wet-dry cycling

What else do project teams ask?

What is the controlling structural load on a dry dock floor?
Hydrostatic uplift when the dock is pumped dry — water pressure pushing up across the entire floor area, often exceeding the weight of the empty structure. I design the floor as a thick reinforced slab, sometimes with tension piles or a pressure-relief system, because uplift failure of a dock floor is catastrophic.
How does a dock gate work?
A caisson gate floats into position and seats against the dock entrance, sealing with the help of water pressure; flap gates hinge up from the sill. Either way, the gate is a marine structure engineered for hydrostatic load, ship impact protection, and reliable sealing — and it has to operate on schedule, because the dock earns nothing while it waits for the gate.
What loads do keel blocks impose?
A large vessel's weight concentrates on keel blocks along the centerline and bilge blocks at the turn of the bilge — thousands of tons on discrete bearing points. The dock floor is designed for these concentrated loads plus crane and staging loads around the vessel. I also check the floor for the empty-dock case, where uplift governs instead.
How long does it take to pump a dock dry?
It depends on basin volume and pump capacity — typically several hours for a large dock. The dewatering system is sized for the operational schedule the dock needs, with redundancy so a pump failure doesn't strand a docking. I also design for the reverse: controlled flooding to float the ship out.
