How Does Heat Recovery Ventilation Cut Building Energy Use?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Heat recovery ventilation passes exhaust and intake airstreams through a heat exchanger that transfers heat (HRV) or heat plus moisture (ERV) from exhaust to incoming air, dramatically reducing the energy needed to condition ventilation air.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The concise answer

Heat recovery ventilation passes exhaust and intake airstreams through a heat exchanger that transfers heat (HRV) or heat plus moisture (ERV) from exhaust to incoming air, dramatically reducing the energy needed to condition ventilation air. The device types each have their place: fixed-plate exchangers are simple with no moving parts; enthalpy wheels offer the highest effectiveness but need rotation energy and maintenance; heat pipes and run-around loops keep airstreams fully separate for sensitive applications. Selection depends on climate, the exhaust air's contaminant profile, and how much effectiveness the energy model needs.

Where the savings actually come from

The savings scale with three things: how much outdoor air the building needs, how extreme the climate is, and how many hours the system runs. A densely occupied building in a cold climate with long operating hours — a school, for example — is the textbook case: huge ventilation loads, big temperature differences, and the system running all day. The energy model quantifies it, but the intuition is simple — every degree of preconditioning is a degree the heating or cooling plant doesn't have to provide. There's a second-order benefit people miss: heat recovery downsizes the heating and cooling plant. When ventilation loads drop by half or more, the boilers, chillers, and heat pumps get smaller, which saves first cost as well as energy. I always resize equipment after adding recovery rather than leaving the original sizes in place.

Designing recovery that performs as rated

The gap between rated and delivered effectiveness is a design and controls problem. Balanced airflows, clean exchangers, proper bypass control, and frost protection in cold climates are what separate performing installations from disappointing ones. The design details that matter.

  • Balance exhaust and supply airflows — effectiveness ratings assume balanced flow, and imbalance kills performance
  • Provide bypass or frost control so the device doesn't freeze up or fight economizer operation
  • Keep airstreams separate with heat pipes or run-around loops where exhaust contaminants are a concern
  • Downsize heating and cooling equipment to reflect the reduced ventilation loads
  • Maintain access for wheel and filter servicing — neglected exchangers quietly lose effectiveness

What else do project teams ask?

What's the difference between HRV and ERV?
An HRV — heat recovery ventilator — transfers sensible heat only, using a plate or heat-pipe exchanger. An ERV — energy recovery ventilator — transfers both heat and moisture, typically with an enthalpy wheel or permeable membrane. ERVs suit humid climates where recovering moisture reduces latent cooling loads; HRVs suit dry or cold climates. In very cold climates, ERVs also avoid the frost issues that plague HRVs.
How efficient are modern recovery devices?
Sensible effectiveness of 70-85% is typical for good commercial units, with some designs higher. But rated effectiveness assumes balanced airflows and clean exchangers — real installations with unbalanced flows, dirty wheels, or poor controls deliver less. I treat the rating as the ceiling and design the air distribution and controls to get as close to it as possible.
Do energy codes require heat recovery ventilation?
ASHRAE 90.1 and the IECC require energy recovery on ventilation systems above certain airflow thresholds, with the threshold depending on climate zone and the percentage of outdoor air. Many designs trigger the requirement easily. Even where not strictly required, the economics usually justify it — it's one of the fastest-payback efficiency measures in commercial HVAC.
Can heat recovery cause indoor air quality problems?
Only if misapplied. Enthalpy wheels can carry over a small fraction of exhaust air into the supply — a concern for spaces with contaminants like labs or kitchens, where run-around loops or heat pipes that keep airstreams separate are the right choice. For offices, schools, and retail, ERV cross-contamination is negligible and well within standards.
