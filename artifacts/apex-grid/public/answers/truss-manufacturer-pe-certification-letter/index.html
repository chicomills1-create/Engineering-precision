What Is a Truss Manufacturer PE Certification Letter?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A truss manufacturer PE certification letter states the trusses were designed for the specified loads and code — it covers the trusses only, as delegated design. The engineer of record must still review the submittal, verify loads and bearing conditions, and confirm bracing before installation.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

How delegated truss design works

Delegated design is the code's formal way of splitting engineering responsibility. The engineer of record owns the building's structural system and specifies the criteria the delegated components must meet: the design loads, the spans, the deflection limits, the bearing conditions, and the adopted code. The truss manufacturer's engineer — a licensed PE in the project's state — then designs the trusses to those criteria and seals the truss drawings and calculations. The manufacturer's PE certification letter is the cover statement for that sealed package. This division works because each engineer stays in responsible charge of their own scope: the EOR never seals the truss design, and the truss engineer never seals the building. The handoff between the two is the truss submittal review. The EOR checks the manufacturer's work against the specified criteria and either approves it, approves it with noted corrections, or rejects it with marked revisions. That review is itself an engineering service under the EOR's responsible charge — a real check of loads, reactions, and bracing, not a cursory sign-off on the manufacturer's letter.

The review items that catch real problems

The highest-value checks in a truss submittal review are the interfaces. Bearing reactions get compared against the supporting structure's design — I have caught trusses whose reactions exceeded the girder capacity they were supposed to sit on, a failure that would have shown up only after loading. Specified loads get compared against the EOR's criteria, with special attention to drifted snow, unbalanced loads, and concentrated equipment loads that the manufacturer's standard assumptions may not include. In high-wind markets like Florida and Texas, uplift reactions and the associated holdown connectors get their own check, because truss-to-wall anchorage is the load path's most failure-prone link. Bracing gets the last and hardest look. I confirm the permanent bracing design is present, that it connects into the building's lateral system at points designed to receive it, and that the erection bracing guidance is adequate for the truss spans involved. A truss package with perfect member design and no bracing plan is an incomplete submittal, and I return it as one.

Truss submittal review checklist

A truss package is ready for EOR approval — and for installation — when every link in the delegated-design chain checks out. Missing links come back as corrections, not as field fixes. • Manufacturer's PE certification letter and sealed truss drawings with calculations, for the project's state license • Truss design criteria matching the EOR's specified loads, spans, deflection limits, and code edition • Bearing reactions verified against the supporting structure's designed capacity • Connectors, hangers, and uplift anchorage specified with capacities for the calculated reactions • Permanent bracing design present, with clear responsibility assignment and connection to the lateral system • Truss placement plan coordinated with the framing geometry and MEP penetrations

What else do project teams ask?

Who designs the bracing for trusses — the manufacturer or the EOR?
Responsibility splits, and the split must be explicit. The truss manufacturer designs the individual trusses and typically provides the permanent bracing design for the truss system itself, while the engineer of record designs the overall building lateral system that the braced trusses connect into. Temporary erection bracing is usually the contractor's means and methods, guided by the manufacturer's erection guidelines. Where projects get in trouble is the gray zone: everyone assumed someone else handled the permanent lateral bracing of the truss assembly. I require the submittal to show the bracing design with clear responsibility assignment before I approve installation.
What does the EOR check in a truss submittal review?
I verify the truss design criteria against what I specified — loads, deflection limits, and the adopted code edition. I check that the reactions at each bearing point match what the supporting beams, walls, or hangers were designed to carry, because a truss that works on paper but overloads its support is a failure. I confirm connector and hanger selections have the required capacities, review the permanent bracing layout for completeness, and check the truss placement plan against the framing geometry. Any mismatch gets marked and returned; the submittal is approved only when the trusses, the supports, and the bracing form one coherent system.
Can trusses be installed before the EOR approves the submittal?
No — and this is one of the most expensive shortcuts in residential and light commercial construction. Installing trusses from an unreviewed submittal risks wrong loads, bearing conditions that don't match the design, and missing bracing, all of which are brutally expensive to fix after the roof is on. Most AHJs and most specifications prohibit installation before EOR approval of the truss submittal. The review typically takes days, not weeks, when the submittal is complete. The schedule pressure to set trusses early is real, but it never outweighs the cost of setting the wrong trusses.
Do floor trusses need the same certification as roof trusses?
Yes — the delegated-design process is the same. Floor trusses carry their own critical checks: vibration and deflection performance under foot traffic, web-member clearance for the MEP runs that will thread through them, and bearing conditions at supports and girders. The manufacturer's PE letter covers the floor truss design, and the EOR reviews it against the specified criteria just like roof trusses. I pay special attention to the MEP coordination on floor trusses, because ductwork and plumbing forced through webs that weren't designed for the penetrations is a recurring field problem.
