MEP PE Stamp in San Diego County, CA: What Gets Reviewed Before Sealing?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

An MEP PE stamp in San Diego County certifies the HVAC, plumbing, and electrical design after a California-licensed PE verifies the loads under responsible charge — with Title 24 energy compliance reconciliation as the centerpiece in a mild climate where the details decide compliance, submitted to the county or city AHJ for the site.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Title 24 in a mild climate: the details decide

The San Diego MEP review treats Title 24 reconciliation as the central discipline, because the mild climate leaves no room for sloppy margins. I read the compliance documentation against the sealed set: equipment efficiencies on the forms against the equipment schedules, lighting power densities against the lighting plans, controls sequences against what the drawings show, envelope values against the architectural backgrounds. Where the forms describe a different building than the drawings — a value-engineered equipment swap, a lighting redesign, a controls sequence shown schematically but never detailed — it gets marked as a revision before the seal. The mild climate also shapes the mechanical review itself: with small thermal loads, correct sizing matters more, not less. Oversized equipment short-cycles, dehumidifies poorly, and wastes the efficiency the Title 24 model assumed — so I verify equipment selections against the calculated loads rather than accepting rules of thumb. The sealed drawings should show a mechanical system right-sized for its climate, with the compliance documentation to prove it.

What plan check scrutinizes in a San Diego MEP set

County and city reviewers read the MEP set the way a contractor builds from it. Riser diagrams get checked against the floor plans — every fixture tracing to the riser, with pipe sizing, slopes, and venting that satisfy the plumbing code. Panel schedules get checked against the electrical riser: calculated loads, breaker sizes, and feeder sizes in agreement, with the service calculation supporting the building. Fixture counts get checked against occupancy, specialty systems against their code chapters, and the Title 24 documentation against the drawings it claims to describe. The PE's pre-seal review runs this same gauntlet, which is the entire value of review-and-seal: corrections from your own engineer are fixed in days, corrections from the AHJ cost a review cycle. I also verify the MEP layouts against the structural backgrounds — the lateral system's shear walls and collectors have to coexist with ductwork and piping — because clashes found at plan check cost a correction cycle while clashes found at the engineer's desk cost an afternoon.

MEP submittal checklist for San Diego County

An MEP package is ready for the PE's review — and then for the AHJ — when these items are resolved. Title 24 consistency and right-sized mechanical design are the themes. • AHJ confirmed by parcel: county Planning & Development Services vs City of San Diego vs the other cities • California-licensed PE in responsible charge of the MEP review • Title 24 compliance documentation reconciled line-by-line against the sealed MEP set • HVAC load calculations with realistic coastal or inland-valley conditions; equipment right-sized, not oversized • Equipment schedules, lighting plans, and controls matching the compliance forms • Plumbing risers, fixture counts, and electrical panels/riser fully coordinated • MEP layouts coordinated with structural framing and shear walls before sealing

What else do project teams ask?

Why does Title 24 matter so much in San Diego's mild climate?
Precisely because the climate is mild. With small heating and cooling loads, the energy compliance story is not carried by big equipment decisions — it lives in the details: equipment minimum efficiencies, lighting power densities, mandatory controls sequences, and envelope performance. That makes the reconciliation between the compliance documentation and the sealed drawings even more important, because small mismatches decide pass or fail. The PE reads the Title 24 forms against the MEP set line by line before sealing: equipment matching the schedules, lighting matching the plans, controls the drawings actually depict. In a mild climate, compliance is a coordination discipline.
How does the coastal climate affect mechanical design?
San Diego's coastal zone brings moderate temperatures but real design considerations: ventilation and indoor air quality carry more weight when the thermal loads are small, filtration matters in a region with wildfire-smoke episodes, and humidity control still applies even without extreme heat. I verify the load calculations used realistic coastal design conditions rather than inland assumptions, check equipment selections against the actual loads — oversizing is a common mild-climate error that hurts efficiency and dehumidification — and review the ventilation strategy as the system's primary job. Inland valley sites get the fuller heat treatment with realistic warmer design conditions.
Does the county or the city review my MEP submittal?
Whichever jurisdiction owns the parcel. The City of San Diego's Development Services, the county's Planning & Development Services for unincorporated areas, and the 17 other cities' building departments each run their own intake and checklists. I confirm by parcel at kickoff on every project, because the county's AHJ map has real variety and the answer is not always obvious. The MEP review-and-seal is the same either way; the submittal mechanics follow the AHJ, and the sealed package must be built for the department that will review it.
What MEP drawings does a San Diego County submittal require?
A complete MEP set typically includes mechanical plans with equipment, ductwork, and diffusers; the HVAC load calculation summary and equipment schedules; plumbing plans with fixture layouts; plumbing riser diagrams for water, waste, vent, and gas; electrical plans with lighting and power; panel schedules with calculated loads; the electrical riser diagram from service through distribution; lighting controls documentation; and the Title 24 compliance forms. The PE verifies these sheets agree with each other and with the energy model — equipment matching the plans, panels matching the riser, lighting matching the compliance forms — because internal mismatches are the most common corrections. One complete, coordinated package goes in.
