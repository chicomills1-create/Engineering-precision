How Is Dehumidification Designed for Indoor Farm Grow Rooms?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Dehumidification is engineered as a psychrometric process: the right technology — DX, desiccant, or integrated reheat — matched to the room's temperature regime, coils and reheat designed around the dew point rather than comfort cooling, rejected heat and condensate treated as resources, and staging plus redundancy matched to the sealed room's short failure tolerance.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Dew Point, Coils, and the Limits of Standard Cooling

The design starts on the psychrometric chart, not in an equipment catalog. The engineer plots the room's target condition — temperature and relative humidity the crop needs — finds the dew point of that air, and designs the dehumidification coil to run cold enough to condense moisture against it, with the airflow and contact time that deliver the required removal rate. The sensible heat ratio of the process is checked explicitly: if the coil's moisture removal comes bundled with more cooling than the room wants, reheat must make up the difference, and the engineer sizes that reheat rather than letting the room drift cold. This is why comfort cooling equipment fails in grow rooms even when generously sized. A standard packaged unit's coil and controls assume an office's sensible-dominated load; faced with a grow room's latent-dominated load, it satisfies the thermostat quickly and shuts off while humidity climbs — then short-cycles as the humidistat calls, never holding either target steadily. The engineer either specifies dehumidification-first equipment with its own humidity control logic or designs the integrated system with independent cooling and reheat staging, and proves the sequence on paper: at peak transpiration, at lights-off, and at the part-load conditions in between, the design holds both temperature and humidity inside their bands simultaneously.

DX, Desiccant, and Integrated Reheat Compared

Refrigerant DX dehumidifiers win on modularity. They install in or near the grow space, each with its own humidistat or network control point, and capacity scales with the operation — a phased facility adds units as rooms come online. Their constraint is heat: every pint removed rejects heat into the room, so the engineer carries that heat explicitly in the cooling load and checks that the room's air distribution can absorb it without creating hot spots near the units. Maintenance is distributed too — many filters, many coils — which the engineer weighs against the simplicity of the machines themselves. Desiccant systems win where it is cold. Below the temperatures where refrigerant coils perform well, a desiccant wheel keeps pulling moisture, regenerated by a heated airstream the engineer provides from gas, electric, or recovered heat. The engineer evaluates desiccant where rooms run cool — some propagation and storage applications — comparing the regeneration energy against the DX alternative's lost capacity. Integrated HVAC with reheat wins at scale: one engineered system dehumidifies over its cooling coil, reheats with recovered condenser heat, and serves the room through designed ductwork — more efficient per pint and easier to maintain centrally, but concentrating the failure risk in fewer machines and demanding a proper sequence of operations so cooling, reheat, and any supplemental dehumidification never hunt against each other.

Indoor Farm Dehumidification Design Checklist

Use this checklist before dehumidification equipment is selected: • Peak moisture load established with the room's temperature and humidity targets plotted on the psychrometric chart • Coil dew point and reheat strategy designed explicitly — never assumed from comfort cooling equipment • Technology selected for the temperature regime: DX, desiccant, or integrated reheat, compared on efficiency at real conditions • Equipment capacity verified at the room's actual day, night, and part-load conditions, not the catalog rating point • Rejected heat mapped to useful sinks or carried explicitly in the cooling load • Condensate collection sized for the peak removal hour, piped to drain or captured for reuse with proper trapping • Staging controls sequenced to the daily load curve with smooth capacity tracking and no short-cycling • Redundancy evaluated for sealed rooms against crop value, with N+1 capacity priced as an option

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't the air conditioner just handle the humidity?
Because its sensible heat ratio is wrong for the job. Comfort cooling equipment is built to reduce temperature with modest moisture removal as a side effect; a grow room needs the reverse — heavy moisture removal with controlled temperature. The result of asking one to do the other's job is a room that cycles between too cold and too humid. The engineer designs dehumidification around the dew point with its own control logic, paired with reheat so temperature stays where the crop needs it.
What is the difference between DX and desiccant dehumidification?
DX uses a cold refrigerant coil to condense moisture — simple, modular, and efficient at normal grow-room temperatures, but it rejects heat into the space and loses capacity in cold rooms. Desiccant uses a rotating moisture-absorbing wheel regenerated with heat, which keeps working at low temperatures where DX falters but needs a regeneration heat source. The engineer selects based on the room's temperature regime and compares both on moisture removed per unit of energy at real conditions.
What happens to the heat a dehumidifier rejects?
It goes somewhere by design, not by accident. The engineer either maps it to a useful sink — offsetting heating demand in cool rooms, preheating makeup air — or carries it explicitly in the cooling load so the HVAC accounts for it. What the design never does is ignore it: unaccounted rejected heat is one of the most common reasons grow rooms run hotter than their cooling design predicted.
Can the condensate be reused for irrigation?
Often yes, and at production scale the volume makes it worth designing for. Condensate is essentially distilled water — very pure, which means it needs mineral rebalancing before it suits most nutrient recipes. The engineer designs the collection, storage, and treatment tie-in so the water re-enters the irrigation system through the normal filtration and dosing path rather than as an uncontrolled side stream.
