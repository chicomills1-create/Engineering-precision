How Is Refrigeration Engineered for Supermarket Display Cases?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design case circuits back to the rack with electronic expansion valves and case controllers at each fixture, protect air curtain performance from HVAC interference, and stagger defrost schedules to keep product temperatures stable.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Case Circuits, Valves, and Controls

Each display case gets a home-run or looped liquid and suction circuit sized for pressure drop at full load, with isolation valves so one case can be serviced without shutting down a lineup. Electronic expansion valves hold superheat within a tight band, which keeps evaporator coils fully active and compressors efficient. Case controllers add the intelligence: temperature alarming, fan cycling, anti-sweat heater control based on store dew point rather than a fixed timer, and communication back to the energy management system. Piping details decide reliability. Suction risers get proper traps and sizing for oil return at minimum load, liquid lines are insulated and routed to avoid heat pickup, and every circuit is labeled at the rack and the case. Commissioning verifies superheat, defrost termination, and alarm setpoints at each case — a rack can be perfect and still lose product if a single case controller is misconfigured.

Air Curtains and the HVAC Interface

Open display cases are the most sensitive fixtures in the store because their air curtain is fragile. The refrigeration engineer coordinates with the HVAC designer on diffuser locations, throw patterns, and air velocities in every aisle with open cases. Store pressurization matters too: a negatively pressurized store pulls warm, humid outdoor air across the cases, collapsing curtains and frosting coils. This is why vestibules, door heaters, and building pressure control show up in refrigeration discussions. The engineer may specify night curtains or doors on open cases, aisle-by-aisle HVAC zoning, and commissioning measurements of air velocity at the case opening. When the air curtain holds, case loads drop, defrost cycles shorten, and product temperatures stay in the safe band with margin.

Display Case Engineering Checklist

A complete case design package covers the fixture, the circuit, and the store around it: • Case schedule by temperature class, rated load, and defrost type for every fixture • Liquid and suction piping sized for pressure drop with isolation and service valves • Electronic expansion valves and case controllers with EMS communication • Staggered defrost schedules that avoid simultaneous defrosts on one suction group • Air curtain protection: HVAC diffuser coordination and aisle velocity verification • Low-heat LED case lighting with dimming or occupancy control • Commissioning of superheat, defrost termination, and temperature alarms per case

What else do project teams ask?

Why do open display cases use so much more energy than doored cases?
An open case has no physical barrier between the cold product zone and the warm store, so it depends entirely on an air curtain — a moving sheet of cold air — to hold temperature. Any disturbance from HVAC airflow, customer traffic, or store pressure imbalance lets warm air infiltrate and cold air spill out, which the refrigeration system must continuously replace. Adding doors or night curtains to open cases is one of the highest-return efficiency measures in a supermarket because it directly cuts that infiltration load.
What does a case controller do that a thermostat cannot?
A thermostat only switches cooling on and off at a temperature setpoint. A case controller manages the electronic expansion valve for precise superheat, schedules and terminates defrost, cycles evaporator fans, modulates anti-sweat heaters based on actual dew point, logs temperature history for food safety records, and raises alarms for out-of-range conditions. It also talks to the store's energy management system so operators see every case from one dashboard.
How often should low-temperature display cases defrost?
Defrost frequency depends on store humidity, case design, and door discipline, but typical low-temp cases defrost two to four times per day. The right answer comes from monitoring: defrost should terminate on coil temperature, not just a timer, so the cycle ends the moment frost is gone. Over-defrosting wastes energy and warms product; under-defrosting chokes the coil and starves the case of cooling. Commissioning sets the schedule, and ongoing monitoring keeps it honest.
Do LED lights in display cases really affect refrigeration load?
Yes. Older fluorescent case lighting added meaningful heat inside the refrigerated envelope, and that heat had to be removed by the rack. LED retrofits cut lighting heat dramatically while improving product appearance with better color rendering. Adding occupancy sensors or dimming during low-traffic hours cuts the remaining heat further. In a store with hundreds of feet of case lighting, the combined lighting and refrigeration savings pay back quickly.
