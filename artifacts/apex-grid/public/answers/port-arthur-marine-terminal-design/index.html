How Is a Marine Terminal Engineered at Port Arthur?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A marine terminal at Port Arthur is engineered as a pile-supported wharf or dolphin system designed for vessel berthing and mooring forces, hurricane wind and surge, crane and cargo loads, and the corrosive Sabine-Neches waterway environment - permitted through the city or county plus the Corps of Engineers for in-water work.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Vessel Forces: Berthing, Mooring, and Surge

Berthing analysis starts with the design vessel - its displacement, approach velocity, and angle - and converts kinetic energy into a fender reaction the structure must absorb. The engineer selects fender type and spacing for the energy, then designs the piles and dolphin caps for the resulting lateral loads with deflection limits tight enough to keep fenders, bollards, and loading arms in alignment. Abnormal berthing - a harder-than-planned landing - gets its own check so a single bad approach does not destroy the structure. Mooring analysis maps every line from the vessel to the terminal and applies wind and current per marine standards to find the maximum tension in each. The structure is designed for those pulls acting simultaneously in the worst pattern, with bollards and hooks rated above the line breaking strength so the hardware is never the weak link. Hurricane surge adds a vertical dimension: the engineer models the design surge elevation, checks the deck and piles for hydrostatic uplift and wave impact, and details the connection between deck and piles for the reversal - a wharf that only works in compression will fail when the surge tries to lift it.

Piles, Fenders, and Marine Durability

The pile foundation is the terminal's backbone. Steel pipe piles driven to bearing handle the big lateral berthing loads through flexure; concrete piles suit lighter structures and resist corrosion better in the splash zone. The engineer designs for combined axial and lateral loading, checks drivability, verifies capacity with dynamic testing, and details the pile-to-deck connection for the full moment and shear - plus uplift from surge. Scour protection at the mudline keeps currents from undermining the piles over time. Durability detailing targets the 50-year-plus service life these terminals need. Coatings protect steel in the atmospheric and splash zones, cathodic protection guards the submerged length, and the deck concrete uses low-permeability mixes with corrosion-resistant reinforcement. Fenders are wear items - the engineer details them for replacement without structural surgery, and the owner inspects them on a schedule because a degraded fender transfers berthing energy straight into the piles. Every material choice assumes the Sabine-Neches environment: brackish water, humidity, and chemical exposure from the cargoes handled.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between a wharf and dolphins?
A wharf is a continuous pile-supported deck along the shoreline where vessels berth alongside and cargo is handled on the deck. Dolphins are isolated pile structures - breasting dolphins absorb berthing impact and hold the vessel off the structure, mooring dolphins anchor the mooring lines. Many terminals combine both: a wharf for cargo with dolphins extending the berth for larger vessels.
How are berthing forces calculated?
From the design vessel's kinetic energy: displacement times approach velocity squared, adjusted for eccentricity, added water mass, and fender efficiency. That energy must be absorbed by the fender deflection and pile flexure, which gives the reaction force the structure is designed for. The engineer checks normal berthing and an abnormal, harder landing so one bad approach does not cause collapse.
Why do marine terminals need cathodic protection?
Submerged steel cannot be recoated once in service, and coatings alone eventually fail underwater. Cathodic protection - sacrificial anodes or impressed current - makes the piles the cathode of an electrochemical cell so they cannot corrode, protecting the steel for the structure's design life. Monitoring stations let the owner verify the system is working without diving on every pile.
Which permits does a Port Arthur marine terminal need?
City of Port Arthur or Jefferson County building permits for the structures; Corps of Engineers permits for work in navigable waters; Coast Guard review of waterway suitability and navigation impacts; and potentially Sabine-Neches Navigation District review. The federal waterway permits usually control the schedule, so they start early while local building review runs in parallel.
