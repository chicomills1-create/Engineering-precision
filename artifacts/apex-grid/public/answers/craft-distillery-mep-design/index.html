How Is MEP Designed for a Craft Distillery?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A craft distillery is designed around ethanol vapor ventilation and detection, classified electrical systems in hazardous areas, high-hazard fire protection for barrel storage, still-heat management, and a multi-agency permit path including TTB, the fire marshal, and the state liquor authority.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Ethanol vapor: ventilation and detection

I design the vapor strategy from the process: where ethanol is open to the air, where transfers happen, and where vapors can migrate. General ventilation provides the continuous dilution baseline, sized to the vapor generation rate with margin; local exhaust captures vapors at stills, condensers, and filling points before they disperse. The airflow pattern is designed deliberately — supply and exhaust placed so the air sweeps from clean areas toward the vapor sources and out, never pushing vapors toward electrical rooms, offices, or the tasting room. Detection backs up the ventilation with alarms at the concentrations the design targets, and the control sequence responds — increasing ventilation, alerting staff — before conditions approach anything hazardous. Spill containment is the third layer: curbed still and tank areas, separated drainage that keeps a spill from traveling to ignition sources or the storm system, and materials resistant to ethanol exposure. I also design for the maintenance reality: ventilation equipment in a distillery must be accessible and serviceable, because a vapor-management system that degrades unnoticed is a system that is not managing vapors.

Classified electrical areas

The classified-area plan is one of the first drawings I develop, because it governs the entire electrical design. I establish the boundaries around each vapor source from the process layout and the applicable classification standards, then design everything inside those boundaries accordingly: rated luminaires and devices, sealed conduit and enclosure systems, and controls and instrumentation approved for the location. Wiring methods, sealing fittings, and equipment ratings are specified as a complete system — a single unrated device in a classified area defeats the purpose. Outside the boundaries, the design returns to standard commercial practice — but the transition is detailed carefully, with sealing fittings at every boundary crossing. I also coordinate the classified design with the process controls: the distiller's automation, level controls, and safety interlocks all live in or near the classified areas, so the controls engineer and I resolve the interface early. The inspection and documentation of classified installations get extra attention, because the AHJ's electrical inspector will examine these systems closely — as they should.

Barrel storage fire protection and still heat

Barrel warehouse fire protection starts with the storage arrangement: barrel stacking height, rack configuration, and aisle widths, all designed with the fire-protection engineer and the fire marshal. The sprinkler system is designed to the high-hazard criteria for distilled spirits in wood barrels — high water demand, specific sprinkler types and spacing for the storage configuration — and I verify the water supply, fire pump if needed, and the drainage and containment for the enormous discharge a fire event produces. Separation between barrel storage and the still house, offices, and neighboring properties is part of the same design conversation. The still house HVAC manages two loads at once: the major heat the still rejects and the ventilation the vapor strategy requires. I design cooling capacity for the process heat — often substantial, and year-round — integrated with the dilution ventilation so the two systems work together rather than fighting. Heat recovery from the still and condenser cooling is evaluated where it pencils out, typically for process or building hot water, with the same isolation discipline I apply to any process-heat recovery: the building systems must never compromise the process or its safety systems. The tasting room, as in a brewery, is separated from all of this by construction and HVAC zoning — customers should experience the craft, never the hazard.

What else do project teams ask?

Why does a distillery need classified electrical areas?
Because ethanol vapors are flammable, and ordinary electrical equipment can be an ignition source — a spark from a standard switch or fixture in a vapor-laden area is exactly the accident the design prevents. Areas around stills, spirit safes, storage tanks, and transfer points are classified as hazardous locations, and everything electrical inside those boundaries — lighting, receptacles, controls, sensors — must be rated for the classification with sealed wiring methods. I establish the classified boundaries from the process layout early, because the boundary determines the cost and complexity of every electrical device in the building.
How is ethanol vapor ventilated in a distillery?
With continuous dilution ventilation sized to keep vapor concentrations far below ignitable levels, plus local exhaust at the points where vapors are generated — stills, condensers, filling and transfer operations. I design the airflow so vapors move toward exhaust and away from ignition sources and occupied areas, and I back the ventilation with vapor detection and alarms. The still house ventilation and the comfort cooling are designed as one coordinated strategy, because a cooling system that disrupts the vapor-dilution airflow pattern undermines the safety design. Makeup air is arranged so the ventilation actually sweeps the space rather than short-circuiting.
What fire protection do barrel warehouses need?
High-hazard protection designed for the commodity: barrel-stored spirits are a severe fire load — high-proof alcohol in wood barrels that can fail and release burning liquid. I design sprinkler protection to the high-hazard criteria for the storage arrangement, verify the water supply can deliver the demand, and design drainage and containment for suppression discharge mixed with spilled spirits. Barrel rack stability, aisle widths for firefighter access, and separation from other occupancies are coordinated with the fire marshal, whose review of the hazardous-materials and storage plans is one of the project's key approvals.
What permits does a craft distillery need?
More than most small manufacturers expect: the federal TTB permit for distilled spirits production (the long-lead federal item), state liquor-authority licensing, local building permits, fire-marshal review of flammable liquids and hazardous processes, health-department review where applicable, and sewer-authority approval for high-strength process wastewater. Zoning must allow the use — distilleries are restricted or conditional in many districts. I map the full permit sequence during early design and start the long-lead applications first, because the TTB and fire-marshal tracks run on their own timelines regardless of the construction schedule.
