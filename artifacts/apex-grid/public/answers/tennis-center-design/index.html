How Do You Engineer an Indoor Tennis Center That Plays True?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Tennis center design is the engineering of clear-span indoor facilities with multiple courts, court-tuned lighting, and stable climate control. It covers structural design for column-free court banks, high-uniformity glare-controlled lighting, draft-free HVAC for consistent ball behavior, and the locker, retail, and viewing spaces of a full club.

The concise answer

Tennis center design is the engineering of clear-span indoor facilities with multiple courts, court-tuned lighting, and stable climate control. It covers structural design for column-free court banks, high-uniformity glare-controlled lighting, draft-free HVAC for consistent ball behavior, and the locker, retail, and viewing spaces of a full club.\n\nThe playing environment is the product. Tennis players are exquisitely sensitive to light, air, and surface — the three things engineering controls — and a center that gets them right feels effortless while one that gets them wrong feels broken even when the courts are perfect. That makes the MEP design the heart of a tennis center in a way that's unusual for sports buildings: the mechanical and lighting engineers are designing the playing conditions, not just the building systems.

Where the engineering actually lives

HVAC design for tennis is a specialty exercise. The system has to deliver high ventilation for exertion-level activity, hold temperature and humidity steady (humidity affects both ball bounce and player comfort), and do it all without perceptible air movement at court level. That points to low-velocity air distribution, careful diffuser placement away from play, and often displacement ventilation or fabric duct — plus dehumidification capacity in humid climates so the courts don't get slick with condensation.\n\nThe structure is a long-span problem with an acoustics footnote. Court banks need 120-plus feet clear, and the roof system has to carry lighting, sprinklers, and ductwork without stealing height. Acoustically, multiple courts in one volume get loud — ball strikes, shoes, voices — so absorptive treatment on walls and ceilings keeps adjacent courts playable and conversation possible.

What I push for on day one

Tennis centers are judged by feel — and feel is engineered. The decisions that matter most are made before the structural grid is set.\n\nHere's where I start.

  • Design HVAC for still air at court level: low-velocity distribution, no drafts across play
  • Engineer lighting for the ball, not the building: uniformity and glare control over raw footcandles
  • Hold the height budget: structure, lights, and ducts coordinated to protect 30-plus feet clear
  • Specify the slab for the surface: post-tensioning and flatness tolerances matched to court type
  • Treat acoustics between courts: absorptive surfaces so adjacent play doesn't bleed over
  • Zone for efficiency: light and condition only the courts in use during off-peak hours

What else do project teams ask?

How much clear height do indoor tennis courts need?
About 30 to 40 feet over the courts for competitive play — lobs need room, and lighting and structure have to stay above the play. Recreational facilities can work at the lower end, but competitive and collegiate centers want the full height. Every foot of structure, ductwork, and lighting depth comes out of the building height budget, so the MEP and structural coordination is unusually tight.
Why is air movement such a big deal in tennis buildings?
Because a tennis ball in flight is sensitive to drafts. HVAC diffusers have to be placed and selected so supply air doesn't push balls off line — which means low-velocity displacement or carefully aimed distribution, not the high-throw diffusers used in warehouses. Players notice bad air distribution immediately, and it reads as the building 'playing wrong' rather than an HVAC problem.
What lighting do indoor tennis courts need?
High, uniform light levels with excellent glare control — typically 75 to 125 footcandles for competitive play, evenly distributed so the ball is visible against any background. Indirect or well-shielded direct fixtures, careful aiming to avoid glare in players' eyes on serves and overheads, and increasingly LED systems with dimming for non-competitive use.
What court surfaces go indoors?
Hard courts (post-tensioned concrete or asphalt with acrylic surfacing) are the most common for durability and consistent bounce; some clubs install clay or carpet systems for variety and joint-friendliness. The structural slab design follows the surface: post-tensioned slabs control cracking under hard courts, and the flatness tolerances are tight because the ball tells the truth about every bump.
