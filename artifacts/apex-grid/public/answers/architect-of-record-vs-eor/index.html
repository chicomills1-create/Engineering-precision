What Is the Difference Between the Architect of Record and the Engineer of Record?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

The architect of record seals the architectural design and coordinates the drawing set; the engineer of record takes responsible charge of the engineering systems, verifies the calculations, and seals the engineering sheets. Parallel roles, parallel seals, parallel professional responsibility — neither substitutes for the other.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Where each seal goes

On the permit set, the division is visible in the title blocks and seal blocks. The architect of record seals the architectural sheets — site plan (architectural), floor plans, elevations, sections, details, the code analysis — certifying the building design. The structural EOR seals the structural sheets, the MEP EOR seals the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing sheets, and the civil EOR seals grading, drainage, and utility sheets. Each seal block names the professional, the license number, and the firm; in Texas the engineering seal blocks also carry the firm's F-number. The cover sheet usually carries both: the architect of record's seal for the overall set and an index of the engineering consultants with their seals on their sheets. Deferred and delegated items get noted — the EOR's sheets list the delegated components and their criteria, and the delegated engineers' sealed submittals follow. When I review a set as EOR, I check that the seal map is complete before I seal: every engineered sheet has its PE, every PE is licensed in the project state, and nothing structural is sitting under the architect's seal alone.

How the two roles work together day to day

The working relationship runs on backgrounds and reviews. The architect issues backgrounds — floor plans, ceiling plans, sections — and the EOR designs the engineering to fit them; the EOR issues engineering backgrounds — structural grids, equipment locations, shaft sizes — and the architect coordinates the building around them. Each review cycle, the architect checks the engineering for fit and intent, and the EOR checks the architecture for the inputs the engineering needs: shaft space, equipment clearances, structural depths. The friction points are always the same — ceiling space, shaft space, and the program grid versus the structural grid — and they get resolved in coordination meetings, not by either professional working in isolation. I advise both sides to treat the other as a design partner rather than a service vendor. The architect who brings the EOR into programming discussions gets engineering that fits the building; the EOR who attends design reviews catches the architectural decisions that break the engineering while they are still sketch-level. The permit set that results — architecture and engineering developed together, each sealed by its responsible professional — is what plan check moves through quickly, because the coordination the reviewers look for is actually in the drawings.

Role-clarity checklist

Write the boundary down at project setup and the team never argues about it mid-permit. • Architect of record: licensed architect, seals architectural sheets, owns code analysis and set coordination • Engineer(s) of record: licensed PE(s) in the project state, each seals their discipline's sheets under responsible charge • EOR agreement documents disciplines, deliverables, and scope boundaries — where engineering stops and architecture continues • Seal map on the permit set: every engineered sheet traced to its PE, license verified, Texas F-number where required • Coordination protocol: background exchange schedule, review milestones, and who resolves the fit conflicts the reviews find

What else do project teams ask?

Can one person be both architect of record and engineer of record?
Only if they hold both licenses — a valid architecture license and a valid PE license in the project state — and even then, the roles stay distinct on the permit set, with architectural sheets under the architecture seal and engineering sheets under the PE seal. Dual-licensed practitioners exist but are rare. What is far more common, and not acceptable, is an architect sealing engineering sheets or an engineer sealing architectural sheets beyond their licensed scope — plan check rejects both. Staff the project with both professionals and let each seal their own work.
Who does the building department hold responsible for engineering errors?
The engineer of record, through the PE seal. The seal is the EOR's professional statement that the engineering was performed under their responsible charge, and the state board holds the sealing PE accountable for it. The architect of record is responsible for the architecture and for the coordination of the set, but coordination responsibility is not engineering responsibility — the architect does not inherit the EOR's liability by coordinating the drawings. This is exactly why the EOR must be genuinely engaged: the liability follows the seal, so the seal must follow a real review.
Does the architect of record need to review the EOR's engineering?
The architect reviews it for coordination — does it fit the building, does it match the program, do the drawings agree with each other — not for engineering correctness, which is the EOR's professional domain. An architect second-guessing structural calculations is outside their licensed competence, just as an engineer redesigning the architecture would be. The healthy dynamic: the architect flags coordination conflicts and design-intent deviations, the EOR resolves the engineering. I welcome architects' coordination comments on my sealed sets — they catch the fit problems I cannot see from the engineering side.
On design-build, who is the architect of record and who is the EOR?
Design-build still has both roles, though the contracting looks different. The design-build entity provides architectural services through a licensed architect who serves as architect of record, and engineering through licensed PEs who serve as engineers of record for their disciplines — the GC cannot absorb either role without the licenses. Some jurisdictions scrutinize design-build licensing carefully, so I confirm the arrangement satisfies the state board's rules at project setup. The seals on a design-build permit set look the same as on design-bid-build: architecture sealed by the architect of record, engineering sealed by the EORs.
