How Was San Antonio's Pearl Brewery Adaptively Reused?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

San Antonio's Pearl Brewery was adaptively reused by structurally assessing the 1894 brewhouse and historic buildings, strengthening floors and roofs for restaurant, retail, and hotel loads they were never designed for, inserting modern mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems, and restoring historic masonry while meeting current code through a negotiated compliance path with the City of San Antonio.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Structural Assessment and Targeted Strengthening

The assessment phase documented every load path in the historic buildings: how floors carried load to beams, beams to columns and walls, and walls to foundations. Material testing established the real capacity of century-old masonry, timber, and early steel rather than assuming modern values. The engineers also mapped every alteration the brewery had made over its working life - infilled openings, added mezzanines, equipment pads - because each changed the structure in ways the original drawings, where they existed at all, did not show. This forensic survey became the baseline every later design decision referenced. Strengthening designs were tailored member by member. A timber beam adequate for brewery storage but short for restaurant assembly loads might get a steel plate bolted to its sides, hidden above the ceiling line. A masonry wall needing more lateral capacity might receive a new steel braced frame tucked inside, leaving the exterior brick untouched. Foundations were checked for the changed load patterns - a hotel's uniform floor loads stress footings differently than concentrated vat loads - and underpinned or supplemented where the analysis required. Throughout, the structural work was coordinated with preservation goals so that the engineering disappeared into the historic fabric.

Systems Integration and the Code-Compliance Path

Inserting modern systems into the Pearl's buildings meant solving routing problems new construction never faces. Commercial kitchens need grease-rated exhaust ductwork running to the roof; the engineers threaded it through the old structures without cutting the primary timber framing. Hotel guest rooms need individual HVAC, plumbing stacks, and fire sprinkler coverage in buildings whose floor-to-floor heights and structural bays were set by brewing equipment. The MEP design worked within those constraints, using the buildings' generous ceiling heights for horizontal distribution and carefully located vertical chases for risers. Code compliance for the district ran through the City of San Antonio's review of existing buildings. Rather than forcing 19th-century structures to meet every new-construction provision literally, the project used the code's existing-building compliance methods - evaluating what the buildings provided, upgrading where the change of occupancy demanded it, and proposing equivalent alternatives where literal compliance was impossible. Fire separations, egress paths, and structural safety received the most scrutiny, exactly as they should in public assembly and hospitality occupancies. The authority having jurisdiction reviewed each approach and decided what was acceptable; that negotiated, documented path is how complex adaptive reuse projects earn their approvals.

What is adaptive reuse in structural engineering?
Adaptive reuse converts an existing building to a new purpose while keeping its essential structure - the Pearl's brewhouse became restaurants, shops, and a hotel instead of being demolished. The structural engineer assesses what the building can carry, strengthens what falls short of the new use, and upgrades lateral and fire performance, all while preserving the character-defining features that make the building worth saving.
Why couldn't the Pearl's buildings be used as-is for restaurants and hotels?
They were engineered for brewing: heavy but localized equipment loads, minimal plumbing and HVAC, and industrial-era fire and egress standards. Restaurants, retail, and hotels impose different load patterns, far denser mechanical and plumbing systems, and much stricter requirements for exiting, fire protection, and accessibility. The structures needed assessment, selective strengthening, and new systems to serve those uses safely.
How are historic masonry buildings strengthened without changing their appearance?
The work happens inside or invisibly: steel frames tucked behind historic facades for lateral strength, sistered members hidden above ceilings, composite floor overlays that read as the original surface, and repointed or rebuilt masonry matched to the historic mortar and brick. The engineering goal is a building that performs to modern standards while looking like itself.
Who decides how a historic building meets current code?
The authority having jurisdiction - in the Pearl's case, the City of San Antonio. Existing-building code provisions give the framework: evaluate current conditions, upgrade for the new occupancy, and propose equivalent alternatives where literal new-construction compliance is impossible. The design team proposes the path, but the AHJ reviews and approves it. No engineer can promise a particular compliance outcome in advance.
