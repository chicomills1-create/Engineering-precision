How Do Architects Get MEP Designs Reviewed and Sealed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Architects engage a licensed MEP engineer as EOR or reviewing PE, who verifies load calculations, equipment selections, and code compliance across mechanical, electrical, and plumbing — then seals the coordinated MEP set. Complete architectural backgrounds are the critical input; ceiling-space coordination is where most MEP reviews find their biggest issues.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What the MEP PE verifies before sealing

The mechanical review starts with the load calculations — the foundation everything else inherits. I verify the outdoor design conditions are appropriate for the climate (Houston humidity and Phoenix heat are not interchangeable problems), internal gains are realistic for the occupancy, ventilation rates meet code, and latent loads are broken out and visible. Equipment selections get matched against both sensible and latent capacity, and I check the ventilation strategy as a system: code-required outside air in a humid climate is a dehumidification load, not just a duct connection. The plumbing review traces the riser logic: every fixture on the plans should trace to a riser, pipe sizing and venting should satisfy code, fixture counts should match occupancy, and specialty systems — grease waste, medical gas, commercial kitchen exhaust — should carry their own proper detailing. The electrical review checks the distribution chain end to end: service calculation supporting the building, feeders and panels agreeing between schedules and riser, overcurrent protection coordinated, and lighting meeting energy code. Across all three disciplines, I verify the energy compliance documentation matches the sealed design. Only when the systems are right, coordinated, and documented does the seal go on.

Architect inputs that make or break the MEP review

The MEP engineer can only review what the architect provides, and three inputs matter most. First, reflected ceiling plans with the structural framing shown — this is where ductwork, lighting, sprinkler, and structure negotiate for the same inches, and the PE needs to see the negotiation. Second, the space program with real occupancies and uses — a "flex space" label tells the engineer nothing about ventilation or plumbing fixture requirements, and assumptions made on vague programs are where corrections come from. Third, building sections through the tight spots: the corridor with the low structure, the mechanical room with the big equipment, the kitchen with the exhaust makeup air path. I also need the architect's design decisions that constrain MEP: equipment location preferences, noise criteria for sensitive spaces, any owner-mandated systems or manufacturers. And I need them at the review milestone, not after — an MEP review of backgrounds that change weekly is not a review, it is a moving target. Architects who freeze backgrounds for the MEP review milestone get a clean, fast seal; architects who keep "just one more change" coming get a review that never converges.

Architect's MEP sealing checklist

Deliver these and the MEP review stays on schedule and on target. • Engage the MEP EOR (or reviewing PE) by design development — MEP concepts need engineering input while systems are still selectable • Provide complete backgrounds: floor plans, reflected ceilings with structure, sections through tight spaces, real space program • Freeze backgrounds at each review milestone so the PE reviews a stable design, not a moving target • Confirm who seals each MEP scope — EOR-designed, delegated design-build, or specialty — with named, state-licensed PEs • Reconcile energy compliance documentation (Title 24 in CA) against the sealed design before the PE seals, not after plan check asks

What else do project teams ask?

What MEP deliverables does the architect need for permit?
Mechanical plans with equipment, ductwork, and diffusers; the HVAC load calculation summary and equipment schedules; plumbing plans with fixture layouts and riser diagrams for water, waste, vent, and gas; electrical plans with lighting and power layouts; panel schedules with calculated loads; the electrical riser from service through distribution; lighting controls; and energy compliance documentation. The PE's review verifies that these sheets agree with each other — equipment schedules matching plans, panel loads matching the riser, energy forms matching the design. I also confirm the MEP set coordinates with the structural and architectural drawings, because the most expensive MEP problems are coordination problems wearing an engineering disguise.
Who seals the MEP design on a design-build MEP scope?
The design-build subcontractor's licensed PE seals the delegated MEP design after performing it under responsible charge. The architect's MEP EOR then reviews that sealed submittal for conformance with the performance criteria in the architect's documents. This two-seal structure is normal and healthy: the sub's engineer owns the system design, the project's EOR confirms it meets the criteria and coordinates with the building. What does not work is nobody sealing it — I have seen architects assume the sub's "engineered" shop drawings were sufficient for permit, and plan check disagreed. Name the sealing PE for every MEP scope in writing.
How does Title 24 affect the MEP seal in California?
Title 24's energy standards shape the entire California MEP review: the PE verifies that envelope, lighting, and mechanical designs meet the efficiency requirements and that the compliance documentation reflects the actual sealed design — not a model run on different equipment six months ago. California plan reviewers check this reconciliation closely, and mismatches between the energy forms and the drawings are among the most common MEP corrections in the state. I reconcile the compliance model against the permit set as part of the review, before the seal goes on. An MEP set sealed without that reconciliation is a correction round waiting to happen.
Why do MEP sets get more plan-check comments than structural sets?
Because MEP design has more code chapters, more interdependent systems, and more opportunities for internal inconsistency — a panel schedule that disagrees with the riser, an equipment schedule that disagrees with the plan, a controls sequence that was never written. Structural review is deep but narrow; MEP review is broad, and reviewers read MEP sets the way contractors build from them, checking that the logic holds together sheet to sheet. The PE's pre-seal review runs that same gauntlet deliberately, so the corrections come from the engineer — who can fix the design in days — instead of from the city, which costs a review cycle each time.
