How Is Drainage Designed for Houston Elevated Highways?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Drainage for Houston elevated highways is designed by capturing deck runoff in scuppers and downspouts, sizing the collection system for intense Gulf Coast rainfall, conveying it safely to grade without eroding bridge substructures, and tying into detention or the storm sewer network — coordinated with TxDOT standards and the City of Houston or Harris County Flood Control.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Deck Collection: Scuppers, Inlets, and Gutter Flow

The engineer calculates gutter flow for the design storm along each drainage segment of the deck, accounting for the deck width, cross slope, longitudinal grade, and the rainfall intensity the project criteria specify. Scupper and inlet spacing is set so the spread of water stays within the allowable limit — typically keeping the travel lanes clear — at the design flow. Superelevated curves get special treatment: the entire deck width drains to the low side, doubling the gutter flow the low-side inlets must intercept, and the engineer checks the transition zones where superelevation reverses. Inlet hydraulics are checked in two regimes: weir flow at low heads and orifice flow when the inlet submerges during intense bursts. The engineer sizes the throat and grate for the controlling regime with a clogging factor applied, and details the inlet-to-pipe connection so the transition does not choke the flow. Bridge rail and parapet details are coordinated because scupper openings through the parapet affect both the drainage and the crash performance — the structural and drainage designs are developed together, not sequentially. Maintenance access is designed in: inlets that cannot be reached for cleaning will clog, so the engineer provides access from inspection walkways or the shoulder.

Downspouts, Discharge, and Scour Protection

Downspout sizing follows the collected tributary area of each bent or pier, with the pipe diameter checked for the design flow at the available head. The engineer designs hangers and guides for the bridge's thermal expansion and for wind-induced vibration on long vertical runs, and specifies materials — typically galvanized steel or corrosion-resistant alternatives — for decades of exterior exposure in Houston's humid, salty air. Cleanouts are placed at bends and at grade so maintenance crews can clear blockages without dismantling the system. The discharge at grade is where drainage failures erode bridges. The engineer designs energy dissipation — riprap aprons, concrete splash pads, or stilling basins — sized for the downspout's exit velocity, and checks that the discharge does not undermine the bent footings, the embankment slopes, or nearby utilities. Where downspouts connect to buried storm sewers, the engineer designs the junction for the sewer's hydraulic grade line under the design storm, with backflow protection where the receiving system can surcharge. Erosion at the discharge point is inspected after major storms, and the design provides access for that inspection.

Detention, Outfalls, and Multi-Agency Coordination

An elevated highway adds acres of impervious surface, and Harris County's drainage criteria require that new runoff be mitigated — typically with detention basins that hold the design storm and release it at pre-project rates. The engineer sizes the detention volume by routing the highway's hydrograph through the basin, designs the outlet structure for the controlled release rate, and checks the basin's performance across the full range of storm durations, not just the single design event. Where right-of-way is too tight for surface basins, underground detention or oversized conveyance with restricted outfalls may serve, at higher cost. Outfall analysis proves the design does not flood anyone downstream. The engineer models the receiving channel or storm sewer with the highway's discharge added, checking water surface elevations against the criteria the flood control district sets — often a no-adverse-impact standard. Water quality units treat the first flush of oils, metals, and sediment from the deck before discharge where required. TxDOT's hydraulic standards govern the highway drainage itself; the City of Houston reviews tie-ins to city sewers; Harris County Flood Control reviews detention and channel impacts. The engineer sequences these reviews and reconciles their sometimes-differing criteria, and each authority having jurisdiction grants its own approval.

What else do project teams ask?

How does water get off an elevated highway deck?
Through scuppers and deck inlets spaced along the roadway by hydraulic calculation. Roadway cross slopes direct water to the gutters, the inlets intercept it before it spreads into the travel lanes, and collector piping carries it to downspouts at the bents. The whole system is sized for intense Gulf Coast rainfall with clogging factors, because debris in the inlets during a cloudburst is expected.
Why is the discharge at the bottom of downspouts such a concern?
Because a downspout concentrates the runoff from a large deck area into a single high-velocity jet at grade. Without energy dissipation — riprap aprons, splash pads, or piped connections — that jet scours the soil around the bent foundations and embankment slopes, undermining the bridge structure itself. The engineer designs the discharge to protect the substructure it lands next to.
What does detention have to do with a highway?
An elevated highway is acres of new impervious surface shedding water that used to soak into the ground. Harris County's drainage criteria require that added runoff be detained and released at pre-project rates so the highway does not worsen downstream flooding. The engineer sizes detention basins — or underground alternatives where space is tight — to meet that standard for the full range of design storms.
Who reviews elevated highway drainage in Houston?
TxDOT sets the standards and reviews work on the state highway system; the City of Houston reviews connections to city storm sewers and local interfaces; the Harris County Flood Control District reviews detention design and impacts to channels. The engineer coordinates the drainage design across all three agencies, and each one approves its own portion through its own process.
