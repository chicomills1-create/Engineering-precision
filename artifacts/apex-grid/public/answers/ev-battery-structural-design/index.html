What Does Structural Engineering for a Gigafactory Involve?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Million-square-foot scale, heavy concentrated process loads, vibration control, crane loads, and phased expansion — coordinated with the dry-room envelope and fire-protection strategy, not designed in isolation.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. Apex Grid is a veteran-owned company. I'm not a PE; the responsible licensed professionals make technical, sealing, and project-specific decisions.

Designing for the process, not just the code

Code-minimum structural design keeps the building standing; process-driven design keeps it producing. Electrode coating lines impose line loads and vibration limits that standard industrial slabs don't anticipate. Drying ovens bring thermal expansion and concentrated high-temperature loads. Formation areas stack electrical and thermal equipment densely. Overhead cranes and automated material handling add dynamic loads across long bays. The structural engineer needs the process equipment list — weights, footprints, anchor points, vibration criteria — early enough to shape the framing, because a column grid that's perfect for the building shell can be exactly wrong for the process lines inside it.

  • Coating lines: line loads and vibration limits beyond standard industrial slabs
  • Ovens, formation, and material handling add thermal, dense, and dynamic loads
  • Column grids must serve the process lines, not just the shell
  • Process equipment data needed early — it shapes the framing

Vibration, movement, and the envelope

Precision processes — coating uniformity, cell assembly tolerances — need floor vibration held below thresholds that ordinary industrial design ignores. That means stiffer slabs in process zones, isolation of vibration sources (compressors, cooling towers, material handling), and detailing that keeps mechanical equipment vibration out of the process floor. Meanwhile the structure carries the dry-room envelope: long-span roofs over humidity-controlled space, wall systems that support vapor-tight construction, and penetrations for every utility that must be sealed without compromising the structure. Thermal movement across a million-square-foot roof needs expansion-joint planning that doesn't create envelope leaks.

  • Floor vibration thresholds for coating and assembly precision
  • Source isolation: keep mechanical vibration out of process zones
  • Structure carries the vapor-tight dry-room envelope
  • Expansion joints planned so thermal movement doesn't breach the envelope

Phased expansion without demolition

Almost no gigafactory is built at full capacity on day one — the economics demand phases. The structural design anticipates that: foundations and framing sized or detailed for future bays, expansion joints placed where phase two will attach, crane runway extensions pre-planned, and utility corridors routed so future tie-ins don't require cutting the operating plant. The Kentucky and South Carolina projects show why: markets pivot, ownership changes, and the plant that was designed only for phase one becomes the plant that's expensive to grow. Expansion-ready structure costs little extra on day one and saves fortunes later.

  • Foundations and framing detailed for future bays from day one
  • Expansion joints and crane extensions pre-planned
  • Utility corridors routed for future tie-ins without cutting the operating plant
  • Expansion-ready costs little now, saves fortunes later

What else do project teams ask?

What foundation type do gigafactories use?
It depends on soils and loads — deep foundations where soils demand it, heavy-duty slabs-on-grade with thickened zones under process equipment where they don't. The geotechnical investigation and the equipment load list drive the answer together.
How are vibration criteria set?
From the process equipment vendors' requirements, translated into floor vibration criteria (often expressed as VC curves or peak particle velocity limits) that the structural design must achieve in each zone.
Does the structure need to consider thermal runaway?
The fire-protection strategy sets requirements the structure must meet: fire ratings for separations, protection of structural steel in high-hazard areas, and containment details. It's coordinated, not separate.
What seismic considerations apply?
Seismic design category from the site location governs the lateral system, but battery plants add nonstructural-component anchorage for process equipment and utilities — the equipment must survive the earthquake the building is designed for.
