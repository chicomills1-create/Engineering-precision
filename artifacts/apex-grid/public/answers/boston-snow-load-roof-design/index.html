How Are Boston Roofs Designed for Snow Loads?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Boston roofs are designed for snow loads by calculating the ground snow load for the Boston area, adjusting for roof slope, exposure, drifting at parapets and rooftop units, and unbalanced loading, then sizing rafters, trusses, or deck systems so the structure resists the resulting uniform and drifted loads - verified during plan review by the local building department.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

How Snow Loads Are Calculated

The engineer begins with the ground snow load mapped for the Boston area - a statistical value representing the snow weight expected at the site - and converts it to a roof snow load through factors the code prescribes. Exposure accounts for whether surrounding buildings and trees shelter the roof or leave it open to wind scouring; thermal condition accounts for whether the building's heat melts snow from beneath, which is why heated buildings get some relief and unheated ones like open parking structures do not; and slope factor reduces the load on steep, slippery surfaces while leaving flat roofs near full value. Drift is where the math gets site-specific. The engineer identifies every discontinuity - parapets, penthouse walls, rooftop units, the step between a high and low roof - and calculates the drift surcharge height from the upwind fetch distance, producing a triangular load that peaks at the obstruction. On Boston's flat commercial roofs with full-height parapets, drift at the parapet often controls the design of the perimeter framing. Unbalanced loads are checked on sloped and curved roofs for the windward-scoured, leeward-loaded case. The final design considers uniform, drifted, and unbalanced patterns in the load combinations the code requires.

Existing Roofs, Additions, and Warning Signs

Most Boston snow-load engineering involves existing buildings rather than new ones. The engineer surveys the roof framing - rafter sizes and spans in wood-frame houses, joist and deck systems in commercial buildings - and compares actual capacity against current code-level snow loads. This evaluation is routinely triggered by proposed rooftop additions: a deck, a solar array, or new mechanical units add dead load that combines with snow, and the engineer must verify the structure handles both. Change of occupancy can also trigger the check, since the code ties structural evaluation requirements to how a building's use changes. Distress signs tell the engineer where to look hardest. A sagging ridge or wavy roof plane suggests rafters stressed beyond their capacity; cracked or split rafters, especially at notches over bearing walls, indicate overload or deterioration; and interior finishes that crack in a star pattern around a ceiling point can mark a framing member deflecting under load. After an extreme snow event, the engineer can assess whether observed distress is cosmetic or structural and whether temporary shoring or snow removal is warranted - while noting that intentionally overloading a roof by piling removed snow against parapets creates its own drift problem.

What else do project teams ask?

How much snow can a typical Boston roof hold?
It depends on the roof's design, age, and condition - there is no single number. Roofs designed to current code handle the area's mapped snow loads with the code's safety factors, while older roofs were built to earlier standards and may have deteriorated framing. An engineer evaluates the specific structure: member sizes, spans, wood condition, and any added loads like solar panels. When in doubt after a major storm, an engineer can assess whether the roof needs snow removed.
Should I shovel or rake snow off my roof?
Roof raking from the ground can reduce load on a stressed roof, but it must be done carefully - pulling snow unevenly can create unbalanced loading, and standing on a snow-loaded roof is dangerous. If the roof shows distress like sagging, cracking sounds, or sticking doors, call a structural engineer before attempting removal. Never pile removed snow against parapets or in drifts that add concentrated load.
What is snow drift and why does it matter?
Wind moves snow across a roof and deposits it against obstructions - parapets, penthouses, equipment, and steps between roof levels - forming deep triangular drifts. These drifts can impose loads several times the uniform snow load on the framing beneath them. Drift is the controlling design case for many flat commercial roofs and a frequent cause of localized failures near rooftop obstructions.
Is the snow load code the same across the Boston area?
Yes - Massachusetts enforces a single state building code, so the snow load provisions do not change at city lines. What changes is the reviewer: Boston's Inspectional Services Department handles permits inside the city through its own portal, while each surrounding city and town runs its own building department with different submittal requirements, fees, and timelines. The authority having jurisdiction for the project's address decides approvals.
