How Do Engineers Handle Expansive Clay Foundations in Murfreesboro?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Murfreesboro expansive clay is managed with swell testing that sets the design, drilled piers below the active zone or stiffened slabs designed for differential movement, and strict moisture control through grading, drainage, and planting limits. City permits go through the Murfreesboro Building and Codes Department; county sites go through Rutherford County.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Testing, the Active Zone, and Foundation Choices

Swell testing turns a vague clay problem into numbers the engineer can design to. The lab measures how much a sample swells under a light surcharge and the pressure it exerts when prevented from swelling, and the field program maps how deep seasonal moisture change reaches, defining the active zone. Those results sort sites into low, moderate, and high swell potential, and the foundation system follows the classification rather than habit. Drilled piers with structurally supported slabs are the robust answer for high-swell sites: the piers carry the building on soil below the active zone, and void forms under grade beams leave a crushable gap so swelling clay cannot lift the beams. Stiffened slabs suit moderate conditions, with thickened ribs in both directions giving the slab the stiffness to bridge differential movement. Both systems get their detailing from the geotechnical parameters, and both are inspected during construction because a void form left out or a rib poured shallow defeats the design.

Moisture Control and the Owner's Role

A foundation designed for stable moisture fails if the moisture is not stable. The grading plan holds positive slope away from the building for the life of the structure, and the owner maintains it: settled backfill along the foundation gets regraded, not ignored. Roof water is collected and discharged well away from the footprint, and irrigation near the foundation is limited because a sprinkler zone that soaks one side of a slab all summer creates exactly the differential swell the engineer designed against. Trees deserve their own warning. A mature tree can draw hundreds of gallons a day from the surrounding clay, shrinking the soil on its side of the building while the other side stays swollen. The geotechnical report typically sets minimum planting distances by tree species and mature size, and root barriers are specified where landscaping must sit closer. When distress appears anyway, the forensic path starts with moisture: the engineer looks for the leak, the broken gutter, or the new planting bed before blaming the foundation design.

What else do project teams ask?

What makes clay soil expansive?
Certain clay minerals absorb water between their layers and swell, then shrink as they dry. Residual clays weathered from limestone in the Murfreesboro area contain these minerals. The damage comes from differential movement: the soil does not swell evenly, so foundations bend and crack as one area rises or falls relative to another.
What is the active zone?
The active zone is the upper layer of soil where moisture content changes with the seasons, typically the top several feet. Below it, moisture stays relatively constant and the clay does not swell or shrink. Drilled pier foundations extend below the active zone so the building bears on stable soil, while the slab above is detailed to tolerate movement in the zone it sits in.
What is a stiffened slab foundation?
A stiffened slab is a slab-on-grade with a grid of deepened ribs, either conventionally reinforced or post-tensioned, that gives the slab the stiffness to span localized soft spots and resist bending from differential soil movement. It is designed for a predicted amount of edge lift and center lift from the geotechnical report, and it suits moderate expansive conditions.
Why are trees a problem near foundations on clay?
Trees withdraw large volumes of water from the soil, drying and shrinking the clay on their side of the building while the rest stays swollen. That differential shrinkage cracks slabs and foundations. Geotechnical reports set planting setbacks by species and mature size, and root barriers can protect foundations where trees must be planted closer.
Who permits a foundation project in Murfreesboro?
Inside Murfreesboro city limits, the city's Building and Codes Department reviews permits; in unincorporated Rutherford County, the county's building department is the AHJ. Smyrna and La Vergne have their own city departments. The AHJ decides what is approved, so the engineer confirms the parcel's jurisdiction before finalizing the foundation submittal.
