How Is Infrastructure Engineered for Border Colonias?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Colonia infrastructure engineering designs first-time water distribution, wastewater collection or compliant on-site systems, paved roads, and drainage for unincorporated border communities. The county governs the work, state and federal programs typically fund it, and every system is sized for the community's full buildout.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Water and Wastewater: First-Time Service

A first-time water system is designed like a small municipal utility. The engineer starts with a demand study, current population, growth projections, and the fire flow the fire service requires, then sizes every component to that demand: supply wells or a wholesale connection, ground or elevated storage that balances daily peaks and provides emergency reserve, and a distribution grid with looping that keeps pressures stable and lets any single main be isolated for repair without cutting off the community. Water quality drives treatment and disinfection design, and the system is permitted through the state's drinking water program with the county or a local utility district as the owner-operator. Wastewater design mirrors that completeness. Gravity collection is laid out to follow the terrain, with manholes, cleanouts, and slopes that keep solids moving; lift stations go where gravity cannot, each with duplex pumps, backup power, and alarms, because a failed lift station backs sewage into the lowest homes first. Treatment is sized for the design flow with capacity for growth, and the discharge meets the state's wastewater permit limits. Construction in an occupied colonia is phased street by street so residents keep access to their homes, and the engineer coordinates service connections house by house, since every connection is a family getting sewer service for the first time.

Roads, Drainage, and County Standards

Paving a colonia's roads is pavement engineering on difficult ground. The existing dirt roads are proof-rolled and tested, unsuitable material is removed, and the new section is built up with compacted base and an asphalt or concrete surface designed for the axle loads of buses, fire trucks, and delivery vehicles. In expansive clay areas, the design includes moisture control, lime or cement stabilization of the subgrade where the geotechnical report recommends it, and drainage that keeps the subgrade from cycling wet and dry. The road profile is set to drain, with cross-slope and ditches that move stormwater to an outfall instead of letting it pond on the new pavement. Drainage in the flat borderlands is a grading exercise. With little natural fall, the engineer designs shallow ditches, culverts at every crossing, and detention or outfall channels that carry the design storm without flooding homes. Lot grading keeps finished floors above the surrounding grade, and the drainage plan is reviewed against the county's standards and, where floodplain is involved, the county floodplain administrator's requirements. Because the county owns and maintains the public roads and drainage, the design follows the county's construction standards and inspection process, and the project closes out with as-built records the county can maintain for decades.

What else do project teams ask?

What is a colonia?
A colonia is an unincorporated residential community along the Texas-Mexico border that developed without standard infrastructure, often with homes built on subdivided rural land lacking water, sewer, paved roads, and drainage. State programs identify these as economically distressed areas eligible for infrastructure assistance, and engineering projects bring them first-time water, wastewater, road, and drainage systems.
Who governs infrastructure projects in a colonia?
The county, since colonias are in unincorporated areas. The county reviews and approves the road, drainage, and development work, administers floodplain permits, and typically owns the public improvements afterward. Water and wastewater systems may be owned by the county, a utility district, or a water supply corporation, and state agencies permit the drinking water and wastewater facilities.
Why is fire flow part of a colonia water project?
Because a water system without hydrants leaves the community without fire protection, and many colonias have exactly that history. Designing distribution mains and storage for fire flow, the volume and pressure firefighters need, means sizing pipes and tanks larger than domestic demand alone would require. The engineer coordinates the required flow with the local fire service during design.
How are colonia projects funded?
Typically through state and federal assistance programs aimed at economically distressed areas, administered with county sponsorship. The funding programs set their own eligibility, environmental review, and procurement rules, which run alongside the engineering design. The engineer scopes the project to the program's requirements as well as the county's construction standards, since both govern what gets built.
