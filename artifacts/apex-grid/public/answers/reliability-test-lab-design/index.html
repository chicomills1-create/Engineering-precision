How Are Reliability Test Labs Designed for Product Testing?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers size power and cooling for fully loaded burn-in racks and chambers, isolate vibration tables structurally, provide data acquisition infrastructure, and design safety systems for the specific failure modes being tested.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Burn-In Racks, Chambers, and Power Distribution

The electrical design starts with the product load: how many units under test, each unit's power draw, the duty cycle of the test profile, and the growth the lab plans for. Burn-in racks concentrate load densely — the engineer designs distribution with rack-level panels, adequate branch capacity, and monitoring of current per rack so an overloaded rack is caught before it trips. Power quality matters for the test validity: voltage sags during a burn-in run can invalidate the stress the test was supposed to apply. Thermal chambers are designed into the room as heat sources and power loads simultaneously. The engineer sizes the room HVAC for the chambers' full heat rejection plus the burn-in load, provides the electrical circuits each chamber requires, and designs drainage and water supply for humidity-generating chambers. The layout gives chambers service clearance and a rigging path, because chambers are heavy, and positions them so their heat does not cook the adjacent burn-in racks into derating.

Vibration Tables, Monitoring, and Test Safety

Vibration tables need structural engineering that serves the test: isolated foundations or seismic masses that keep the table's energy in the product rather than the floor, with the engineer verifying the isolation against the table's force and frequency range. The tables sit in the test floor layout with clearance for the full stroke and for loading large products, and the design keeps vibration-sensitive neighbors — metrology, offices — separated by distance and structure. Monitoring infrastructure is the lab's nervous system. The engineer provides network drops and power at every test station for data acquisition, a central monitoring location with visibility to chamber controllers and product telemetry, and alarming that distinguishes a product failure (expected, recorded) from a facility failure (chamber excursion, power loss) requiring immediate response. Safety design follows the test hazards: battery testing gets thermal runaway containment and ventilation, high-temperature testing gets fire detection appropriate to the environment, and every station gets emergency power-off that the test engineers can reach without crossing the hazard.

Reliability Test Laboratory Design Checklist

A complete reliability lab design stresses products without stressing the facility: • Electrical distribution sized for full burn-in load with rack-level monitoring • Room cooling sized for combined burn-in and chamber heat rejection • Vibration tables on isolated foundations verified for force and frequency • Thermal chamber utilities: power, water, drainage, and service clearances • Data acquisition infrastructure: network, power, and sensor drops per station • Central monitoring with alarming that separates product vs facility failures • Hazard-specific safety: thermal runaway, ventilation, detection, emergency stops

What else do project teams ask?

What is burn-in testing and what does it need from the facility?
Burn-in runs product at elevated temperature under power to weed out early-life failures before shipment. From the facility it needs dense electrical distribution for the powered racks, massive cooling for the heat those racks reject, and monitoring to track which units fail and when. The engineer designs both the power and the cooling for the fully loaded condition — a burn-in lab with half the needed cooling derates its own throughput, because racks that cannot be cooled cannot run.
Why do vibration tables need isolated foundations?
A vibration table applies controlled force to the product — and by Newton's third law, an equal force to whatever it sits on. Without isolation, that energy enters the building structure, shaking neighboring labs, disturbing precision instruments, and in the worst case exciting structural resonances. Isolated foundations or seismic masses absorb the reaction forces so the table's energy goes into the product under test. The engineer designs the isolation for the table's specific force and frequency range, verified against the manufacturer's requirements.
How is battery testing made safe in a reliability lab?
Through layered protection designed for thermal runaway: test stations with fire-rated separation or containment enclosures, ventilation that handles off-gassing, fire detection appropriate to the chemistry, and emergency procedures the design supports — like the ability to kill power to a station while keeping monitoring alive to record the event. The engineer works from the battery chemistries and test profiles to size each layer, because a generic lab fire design does not address what a failing lithium cell actually does.
What monitoring does a reliability lab need?
Two kinds: product monitoring through the test engineers' data acquisition — temperatures, voltages, currents, vibration levels on thousands of channels — and facility monitoring of chamber conditions, room environment, and power quality. The engineer provides the infrastructure for both: network and power at every station, central monitoring with trending, and alarming that tells operators whether a failure is a product under test (expected) or the facility itself (urgent). Without that distinction, every alarm becomes noise and real facility failures get missed.
