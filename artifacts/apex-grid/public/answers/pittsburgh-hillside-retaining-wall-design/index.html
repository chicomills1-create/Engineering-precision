How Are Pittsburgh Hillside Retaining Walls Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Pittsburgh hillside retaining walls are designed from a geotechnical report that sets soil pressures and drainage, using cantilevered concrete, soldier-pile-and-lagging, or anchored walls sized for surcharge loads from the slope above, with weep systems and tiering to keep walls within stable heights.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Choosing the Right Wall for the Slope

The engineer matches the wall to the height, the soil, and the available room. Gravity walls of stone or mass concrete rely on their own weight and suit low heights where the look matters - common in Pittsburgh's historic neighborhoods. Cantilevered reinforced concrete walls use a footing that extends back under the retained soil, letting the soil's own weight stabilize the wall; they are the standard choice for cuts up to moderate heights. Above that, or where the retained height includes a surcharged slope, the design moves to counterforted walls, anchored walls, or mechanically stabilized earth with geogrid reinforcement. Soldier-pile-and-lagging walls deserve special mention in Pittsburgh because so many lots are narrow with structures close to the property line. The piles are drilled or driven first, excavation proceeds downward, and lagging is placed between the piles as the cut deepens - no wide open excavation, no undermining the neighbor. Where even that is too disruptive, tieback anchors let a thin wall hold back a tall cut by reaching into stable ground behind the active zone. The engineer sizes every anchor for its tributary area and specifies testing, because an untested anchor is an assumption holding up a hillside.

Drainage and Global Stability

A retaining wall that holds back soil but not water is a dam, and dams fail. The design includes a drainage blanket of free-draining stone directly behind the wall face, a perforated pipe at the heel that carries water to daylight or a storm connection, and surface grading above that keeps runoff from pouring over the top. Weep holes through the face give water a visible exit and give the owner an early warning: a weep hole that suddenly runs muddy is telling you soil is migrating and the drain needs attention. The engineer details filter fabric or graded filters so fine soils cannot clog the stone. Global stability is the check most often skipped and most often needed. It asks whether the entire slope - wall included - could slide along a deep failure surface that passes beneath the wall's footing. The engineer runs a slope stability analysis with the wall in place, because a wall can be perfectly stable on its own while the hillside carries it downhill. On Pittsburgh's colluvial slopes this check frequently controls the design, pushing footings deeper, adding a key, or extending the wall's influence with soil nails above. A wall designed without it is designed for half the problem.

What else do project teams ask?

Why do so many old Pittsburgh retaining walls lean or fail?
Most were built without drainage and without engineering for the real soil pressures. Water builds up behind the wall, doubling or tripling the load, while the region's creeping colluvial soils push relentlessly. An unreinforced or undrained wall eventually rotates, slides, or bulges. Replacement walls are engineered for saturated pressures, include full drainage, and are checked for global slope stability - the three things the old walls lacked.
What is global slope stability and why does it matter for my wall?
Global stability looks at the whole hillside, not just the wall, asking whether a deep slide surface could carry the wall downhill. The engineer analyzes potential failure surfaces passing beneath and behind the wall. If the slope itself is marginally stable, the wall design must address it - deeper foundations, tiebacks into stable ground, or slope work above - because a wall cannot hold up a moving hillside.
How are tieback anchors tested?
Each production anchor, or a representative sample per the engineer's specification, is loaded with a hydraulic jack to a proof load above its design load and held while movement is measured. Anchors that creep excessively are rejected or re-grouted. Testing happens before the wall is loaded, so the engineer verifies capacity in the actual ground rather than trusting the design assumptions.
Do I need a permit for a retaining wall in the Pittsburgh area?
Usually yes, and the path depends on where the wall sits. The City of Pittsburgh reviews walls through its building permit process with zoning and sometimes geotechnical review; each surrounding borough and township is its own authority having jurisdiction with its own application, review timeline, and hillside or grading rules. Walls above certain heights or supporting structures nearly always trigger engineering and permits. The engineer confirms the AHJ and its submittal requirements before design begins.
