How Is MEP Designed for a Milwaukee Brewery?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

MEP for a Milwaukee brewery is designed around process loads: steam or electric brewing kettles, glycol chilling, large makeup-air and exhaust for the brewhouse, floor drainage and trench drains sloped to handle washdown, explosion-proof electrical in grain-handling areas, and pretreatment of high-strength wastewater before it enters the city sewer.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Process Heating, Cooling, and Ventilation

The thermal design starts with the brewer's process flow: mash, lauter, boil, whirlpool, knockout, fermentation, conditioning, and packaging, each with its heating or cooling demand and its timing. The mechanical engineer converts the equipment list into a diversified load schedule — the boil kettle and the glycol chiller rarely peak simultaneously, and the design takes credit for that diversity without undersizing. Steam systems get condensate return designed for the full load with proper trapping and insulation; glycol systems get primary-secondary pumping, expansion accommodation, and insulation with vapor barriers on every cold surface to prevent condensation and ice. Brewhouse ventilation is a life-safety system as much as a comfort one. The engineer sizes exhaust to remove process heat and steam, provides tempered makeup air so the building does not go negative, and adds dedicated CO2 detection with alarming in the cellar, cooler, and any pit or low area where the heavy gas can accumulate. Combustion air for boilers and direct-fired equipment is provided per code, separated from the general ventilation. All of this is documented on the mechanical plans with sequences of operation, because the installing contractor and the plan reviewer both need to see how the systems respond across the brewing cycle.

Washdown Drainage and Wastewater Pretreatment

Brewery floors are working surfaces that get hosed down daily, so the plumbing design treats drainage as process infrastructure. Production floors slope to trench drains and floor sinks sized for washdown flows with hot water and chemical cleaners; the trench drains get sediment buckets or strainers to catch grain and solids before they enter the piping. Pipe materials and joint methods are selected for hot, chemically aggressive effluent, and the drainage layout maintains the slopes and cleanout access the maintenance staff will need for the life of the building. Before that effluent reaches the city sewer, it passes through pretreatment. Brewery wastewater carries high biochemical oxygen demand, suspended solids, and pH swings from cleaning chemicals, so the engineer designs screening, pH neutralization, and sometimes equalization tanks that smooth out slug discharges from tank dumps. The design targets the sewer utility's discharge limits for BOD, solids, pH, and temperature, with sampling manholes so compliance can be verified. The engineer coordinates the pretreatment design with the utility early, because the utility's approval is a separate gate from the building permit and its requirements shape the equipment selection.

Electrical Classification and Milwaukee Permitting

The electrical design separates the brewery into classified and unclassified areas. Grain milling, conveying, and dust collection can produce combustible dust atmospheres, and the engineer applies the hazardous-location classification to those areas — specifying dust-ignition-proof enclosures, sealed wiring methods, and equipment listed for the classification. Everywhere else, the design provides robust industrial-grade distribution: adequate service capacity for the chiller plant and process loads, dedicated circuits for critical controls, and emergency power or orderly-shutdown provisions for the processes that need them. Permitting in Milwaukee runs through the Development Center, where the MEP drawings undergo plan review alongside the architectural and structural sets. The health department reviews the brewery as a food-production facility, covering finishes, hand sinks, and process hygiene. The sewer utility — Milwaukee Metropolitan Sewerage District for regional treatment — reviews and approves the pretreatment design against its discharge rules. Each authority decides its own approvals on its own timeline, so the engineer sequences the submittals to keep all three tracks moving and confirms the requirements of each before the design is finalized.

What else do project teams ask?

Why does a brewery need glycol cooling?
Fermentation and conditioning must happen at precise, cool temperatures — lagers near freezing — and serving tanks need to hold that temperature steadily. A glycol chiller circulates food-grade antifreeze through jackets on the tanks, giving the brewer tight temperature control for each vessel independently. It is typically one of the brewery's largest mechanical and electrical loads, so the engineer sizes it from the brewer's production schedule.
What makes brewery wastewater different from ordinary commercial wastewater?
It is much stronger: high in organic material (sugars, yeast, proteins) measured as biochemical oxygen demand, with suspended solids from grain and wide pH swings from caustic cleaners and acid rinses. Discharging it untreated can violate sewer utility limits and incur surcharges, so the engineer designs screening, pH adjustment, and sometimes flow equalization as pretreatment before the city sewer.
Why is carbon dioxide a design concern in a brewery?
Fermentation releases large volumes of CO2, which is heavier than air and collects in low spots — cellars, pits, walk-in coolers — where it can asphyxiate workers without warning. The engineer designs continuous ventilation for these areas plus fixed CO2 detection with audible and visual alarming, treating it as a life-safety system in the MEP design.
Who reviews brewery MEP plans in Milwaukee?
Three tracks: the city's Development Center reviews the building and MEP plans, the health department reviews the food-production aspects, and the sewer utility reviews the wastewater pretreatment design. Each is a separate authority with its own submittal requirements and timeline. The MEP engineer coordinates all three from the start so the process design satisfies every reviewer.
