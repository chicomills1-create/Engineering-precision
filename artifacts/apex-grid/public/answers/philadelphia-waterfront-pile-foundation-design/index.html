How Are Pile Foundations Designed for Philadelphia's Waterfront?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Pile foundations for Philadelphia's waterfront are designed for soft soils along the Delaware and Schuylkill: driven piles or drilled shafts that reach competent bearing strata, with lateral analysis for flood and scour forces. Design follows the Philadelphia Building Code, ASCE 7 flood-load provisions, and FEMA requirements, with the Army Corps and Pennsylvania DEP involved where work extends into regulated waterways.

Pile Types and Bearing Strata

The boring program is the foundation of the foundation design. Deep borings along the waterfront typically reveal fill and soft river clays near the surface, transitioning with depth to denser sands and gravels and eventually to bedrock — and the pile design targets the competent layer that can reliably carry the building. The geotechnical engineer establishes the bearing stratum's elevation across the site, because it varies, and provides the soil parameters the structural engineer uses to calculate end-bearing and skin-friction capacity for each candidate pile type. Where the bearing layer is deep or variable, the design may specify piles driven to a defined resistance criterion rather than a fixed tip elevation, so every pile proves its own capacity during installation. The structural design turns capacity into a buildable foundation. The engineer lays out the pile groups under each column, designs the reinforced-concrete pile caps that distribute column loads into the piles, and details the connections for uplift as well as compression — because flood buoyancy and wind overturning can pull on piles that are usually thought of as pushing down. Corrosion detailing reflects the environment: steel piles get protective coatings or increased sacrificial thickness in the splash and tidal zones, and concrete piles use durable mixes. The specifications define the installation criteria, the testing program, and the acceptance standards, so the piles the contractor installs are the piles the engineer designed.

Flood, Scour, and Waterway Permits

Flood design treats water as a structural load. The engineer applies the code's flood-load provisions to the piles and pile caps: hydrostatic uplift on submerged elements, hydrodynamic drag from moving water, and wave forces where the fetch and depth produce them. Scour is analyzed as a loss of soil support — the design checks pile stability and capacity with the scoured soil profile, not the pre-flood grade, because a pile designed for full soil support can be under-designed the first time a major flood strips the riverbed around it. Utilities and grade beams in the flood zone are detailed for submergence, and anything the flood can carry away is anchored or relocated. Permitting follows the water. The building itself is permitted through Philadelphia's Department of Licenses and Inspections under the Philadelphia Building Code, with structural plan review of the pile design, caps, and flood detailing. Work in or over regulated waterways — pile installation from barges, shoreline structures, dredging — brings in the Army Corps of Engineers and the Pennsylvania DEP, each with its own application, environmental documentation, and timeline. The engineer coordinates the foundation design across these reviews so the pile layout the waterway permits authorize is the layout the building permit approves, and construction sequencing respects any seasonal or environmental restrictions the permits impose.

What else do project teams ask?

Why do waterfront buildings need piles instead of spread footings?
Because the near-surface soils along the rivers — soft clays and loose fills — are too weak and compressible to support a building on shallow footings; they would settle excessively or fail. Piles bypass these layers and transfer the building's load to dense sands, gravels, or bedrock far below, where the ground can reliably carry it.
How is pile capacity verified in the field?
Through installation monitoring and testing: driven piles are verified by driving resistance correlated to the design capacity, often with dynamic pile-driving-analyzer testing, while major projects use static load tests that load a test pile to multiples of the design load and measure settlement. Drilled shafts are verified by concrete integrity testing and base cleanliness inspection. The engineer specifies the testing program and acceptance criteria in the construction documents.
What is scour and why does it matter for pile design?
Scour is the erosion of soil from around piles during floods or strong currents, which removes the lateral and skin-friction support the design counted on. The engineer analyzes the piles for the scoured condition — reduced embedment, higher lateral loads — so the foundation remains stable even after the riverbed around it has been stripped away.
Do waterfront pile projects need permits beyond the Philadelphia building permit?
When work touches regulated waterways — piles installed from the water, shoreline structures, or dredging — the Army Corps of Engineers and Pennsylvania DEP have jurisdiction in addition to the city's Department of Licenses and Inspections. The foundation design must satisfy all of them, and each authority has its own application, review timeline, and inspection requirements.
