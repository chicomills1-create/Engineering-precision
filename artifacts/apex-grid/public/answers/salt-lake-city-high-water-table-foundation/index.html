How Are Salt Lake City Foundations Built in High Water Tables?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Salt Lake City foundations in high water tables are built with dewatered excavations, waterproofed structural concrete with waterstops at joints, slabs and footings sized for hydrostatic uplift, and permanent sump and perimeter drainage - all based on measured groundwater levels and reviewed by the city or county building department.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Where the Water Comes From

The Salt Lake Valley's shallow groundwater has several sources, and a site can be affected by more than one. The lakebed clays perch infiltrating water near the surface because the fine soils drain so slowly. Snowmelt swells the water table each spring, and the valley's irrigation canals and irrigated landscaping leak water into the shallow soils all summer. Near the Jordan River and its tributaries the water table simply sits high year-round, and springs emerge where sloping bedrock forces groundwater to the surface along the valley margins. The geotechnical investigation maps this water regime before design. Borings record the depth where water is first encountered and where it stabilizes, piezometers track seasonal fluctuation, and lab tests establish the soil's permeability - which controls how fast water will flow into an excavation and how the permanent drains must be sized. The design high-water level is set conservatively from this data, because a foundation designed to the average water level fails at the spring peak. The report also flags chemistry: sulfates or chlorides in the groundwater can attack concrete and reinforcement, driving material specifications.

Designing for Uplift and Seepage

Hydrostatic uplift is simple physics with serious consequences: water pressure acts upward on every submerged surface at about sixty-two pounds per square foot per foot of submergence, and a basement slab several feet below the water table can see thousands of pounds of uplift per square foot of area. The engineer compares that force against the dead weight of the structure with the code's required safety margin. Where weight alone is not enough, the design adds tension capacity - piles or micropiles designed for uplift, a thickened mat that adds mass, or ground anchors - and details every connection in the uplift load path. Seepage control is the companion discipline. The concrete mix is specified for low permeability with crack-width limits, because a crack is a water path regardless of the concrete's strength. Waterstops - PVC, bentonite, or hydrophilic strips - go in every construction joint below the water line, and the waterproofing system is selected for the exposure: adhered membranes for positive-side protection, bentonite panels that self-seal, or crystalline admixtures that grow pore-blocking crystals. Penetrations for pipes and conduits get water-tight seals detailed on the drawings, not left to the field. The engineer assumes water will test every detail and designs accordingly.

Building It Dry

Concrete placed underwater, or in an excavation filling with seepage, does not achieve its designed strength or watertightness - so the excavation is dewatered first and kept dry through the foundation work. Wellpoints or deep wells around the excavation lower the water table locally, with the pumping rate matched to the soil's permeability from the geotechnical report. The discharge plan matters as much as the pumping: dewatering effluent needs a discharge permit from the city or the state, sediment must be controlled, and the water cannot be sent to the storm drain or a neighbor's lot without approval. Dewatering affects more than the site. Drawing down the water table can consolidate compressible soils beneath adjacent streets and structures, so the engineer monitors neighboring ground and structures during pumping and designs the shoring system to protect them. Once the foundation is complete and backfilled, the temporary wells come out and the permanent system - exterior perimeter drains in filter-wrapped gravel leading to a sump pit with primary and backup pumps, high-water alarms, and ideally emergency power - takes over. The owner inherits that system: pumps are tested, alarms are kept live, and the discharge point is kept clear. A high-water-table foundation is a machine for staying dry, and machines need maintenance.

What else do project teams ask?

What is hydrostatic uplift?
Hydrostatic uplift is the upward force groundwater exerts on any structure below the water table - water pressure pushing up on the bottom of slabs and footings. It increases with depth below the water level, and on a deep basement it can exceed the weight of the building itself. Engineers calculate it from the design high-water level and resist it with the structure's dead weight plus tension piles, thickened mats, or anchors where needed.
What is the difference between waterproofing and dampproofing?
Dampproofing resists moisture vapor and minor dampness in soil that drains well; waterproofing resists water under hydrostatic pressure. Below the water table, dampproofing is not enough - the design needs true waterproofing: low-permeability concrete, waterstops at joints, and a membrane or crystalline system rated for the head of water the site will see. The geotechnical report's groundwater data decides which level of protection the code requires.
How is groundwater measured before foundation design?
The geotechnical engineer drills borings and records both the depth where water is first encountered and where it stabilizes after drilling, then often installs piezometers - small monitoring wells - to track the water level across seasons. Permeability tests establish how fast water moves through the soil. The design uses the high-water condition from this data, not a single dry-season reading.
Do dewatering discharges need permits in Salt Lake City?
Yes. Dewatering effluent cannot be discharged to the storm drain system, a waterway, or neighboring property without the proper approvals - the city regulates storm drain discharges and the state regulates discharges to waters of the state. The dewatering plan, including treatment for sediment, is part of the permit submittal. Inside city limits Salt Lake City is the AHJ; in unincorporated areas it is Salt Lake County, and discharge rules follow the receiving system.
