How Is Inventory Lot Lighting Designed for Dealerships?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design inventory lot lighting with photometric pole layouts that merchandise display rows and eliminate dark pockets, using full-cutoff LED fixtures, security-coordinated coverage, and scheduled dimming for energy compliance.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Photometric Layout and Display Merchandising

The photometric plan is the design document: every pole located, every fixture aimed, and calculated light levels across the lot proving uniformity. The engineer designs display rows for higher illuminance with good color rendering so vehicles read well at night, while storage rows get security-appropriate levels. Uniformity ratios matter more than peak levels — a lot with bright spots and dark gaps feels less safe than a lot with even, moderate light. Mounting height and optics are chosen together. Taller poles with well-controlled optics cover more area per pole with fewer shadows from the vehicles themselves, while the street frontage may use lower, more architectural fixtures for brand presentation. The plan also accounts for future inventory reconfiguration, keeping the lighting grid flexible rather than married to one row layout.

Security Coverage, Glare Control, and Neighbors

Security lighting is designed, not assumed. The engineer eliminates dark pockets where theft or vandalism can hide, coordinates light levels with camera positions so surveillance actually sees, and lights the perimeter, gates, and building entries as part of the same system. Motion-responsive boost in low-traffic zones can raise levels when activity is detected. Glare and trespass control protect the dealership's neighbors and its own drivers. Full-cutoff fixtures keep light on the lot and out of adjacent properties and the night sky, house-side shields handle poles near property lines, and the design verifies compliance with local lighting ordinances. A lot that blinds passing traffic or floods neighboring homes creates liability and community problems no sales gain offsets.

Inventory Lot Lighting Design Checklist

A complete lot lighting package merchandises, secures, and complies: • Photometric plan with pole locations, heights, and calculated light levels • Display rows at merchandising light levels; storage rows at security levels • Full-cutoff LED fixtures controlling glare and light trespass • Security coordination: no dark pockets, camera positions matched to light levels • Perimeter, gate, and entry lighting integrated with the lot system • Scheduling, dimming, and motion response for overnight energy control • Energy code and local lighting ordinance compliance documented

What else do project teams ask?

How bright should a dealership inventory lot be?
Bright enough to merchandise and secure, not as bright as possible. Display rows typically target higher levels with good color rendering so vehicles look appealing at night, while general lot areas and storage rows use moderate security levels. What matters most is uniformity — even light without dark pockets — rather than peak brightness. The engineer sets targets from industry practice and the owner's risk assessment, then proves them with photometric calculations before poles are ordered.
What are full-cutoff fixtures and why do lots need them?
Full-cutoff fixtures direct all light downward with no uplight and minimal glare at high angles. They keep illumination on the lot instead of into drivers' eyes, neighboring properties, or the night sky. Most local lighting ordinances now require or effectively require them, and they are simply better engineering — light sent skyward is wasted energy. The design pairs cutoff optics with appropriate mounting heights so coverage stays even without spillover.
How does lot lighting help prevent vehicle theft?
Through visibility and deterrence. Even, shadow-free light eliminates the dark pockets where theft and vandalism happen unobserved, supports camera surveillance with usable light levels, and signals active management of the property. The engineer coordinates lighting with the security design — camera positions, fence lines, gate areas — so the two systems reinforce each other. Lighting alone does not stop a determined thief, but darkness is an invitation the design removes.
Can lot lighting dim after the dealership closes?
Yes, and it should. Controls scheduling drops display merchandising levels to security levels after closing, with further dimming or motion-responsive operation in low-traffic zones overnight. This cuts energy costs substantially across a large lot while maintaining the security baseline the risk assessment requires. The control design documents each mode — sales hours, after-hours, deep night — so the operation matches the intent instead of running full bright all night by default.
