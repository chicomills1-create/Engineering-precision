How Are Shipping Container Buildings Designed Structurally?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Container buildings are engineered by treating each box as corrugated steel shear walls, designing reinforcing frames around every cut opening, verifying stacked corner-post loads and welded or bolted connections, and rejecting corroded or damaged units before design begins.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Opening reinforcement design

I design every container opening as a small steel frame inserted into a corrugated shear wall. The header carries the gravity load from the corrugation above, the jambs carry it down to the sill or the floor, and the whole frame is welded to the remaining wall so the in-plane shear has a continuous path around the opening. For window and door openings, tube steel sized for both the gravity and the shear demand is usually enough. For storefront-scale openings I run the numbers on the wall segment that remains — narrow corrugated piers between big openings can buckle or shear-fail, and I would rather find that in calculation than in the field. Removing an entire long wall to join containers side by side is a different animal: at that point the wall is gone as a structural element and I design a replacement lateral system. That usually means a steel moment frame or braced frame along the open side, with the roof and floor of the container acting as the diaphragm to deliver loads to it. I detail the frame-to-container connections for the full demand, because a beautiful open-plan container space with an under-designed frame is a collapse waiting for a windstorm.

Stacking, welding, and foundations

Multi-story container buildings live or die on the corner connections. I calculate the axial load at each corner casting from the units above, check the corner post for buckling under that load, and design the inter-box connection for the net uplift after dead load — wind on a tall, light box produces real overturning. Welds are specified to structural welding standards: weld type, size, and length on the drawings, qualified welders, and inspection. Field welding on thin corrugated sheet is unforgiving, so I keep the critical welds on the heavier corner and rail material and use bolted connections where the base metal is thin. Foundations are conventional engineering. I design piers, strip footings, or slabs for the soil conditions on the geotechnical report, with anchor bolts or weld plates positioned to land under the corner castings. The foundation also sets the finished floor elevation for flood compliance and keeps the bottom rails out of standing water — the two cheapest corrosion protections available are elevation and drainage, and I make sure the civil design delivers both.

Container building structural checklist

A container building is ready for permit when the box, the cuts, and the connections are all engineered — the container's original strength covers none of the modifications. • Inspect and grade every unit before design: reject deep corrosion, deformed corner castings, and compromised floors • Design a welded reinforcing frame around every opening, sized for gravity and shear, tied into the remaining corrugation • Replace fully removed walls with engineered steel frames; verify the container roof and floor as diaphragms • Check corner-post buckling under stacked loads and design inter-box connections for net wind uplift • Specify structural welding standards, qualified welders, and inspection — no improvised field welds on primary structure • Found the building on engineered footings with anchorage at the corner castings, elevated and drained against corrosion

What else do project teams ask?

Are shipping containers strong enough to be buildings?
Unmodified, yes — they are designed for brutal service, stacked fully loaded on ships in heavy seas. The strength lives in the corrugated walls and the corner posts, and it is directional: enormous in-plane shear, modest out-of-plane bending. The engineering problem is never the intact box; it is the modified box. Every opening cut, every removed wall, and every nonstandard stacking arrangement must be re-engineered, because the container's original certification covers the box as manufactured, not as remodeled.
What happens structurally when you cut openings in a container?
You remove shear wall, and the building has to get that strength back somewhere. Small openings get a welded tube-steel frame that bridges the cut corrugation and restores the load path. Large openings — removing most of a long wall to combine containers — delete the wall as a lateral element, and I replace it with an engineered steel frame sized for the wind and seismic loads. The remaining corrugation beside and above the opening still works, but only if the reinforcing frame ties into it with real welds, not tack welds and hope.
How are stacked containers connected?
Through the corner castings, which is where the container is designed to bear. I specify either the proprietary twist-lock stacking connectors with verified capacity or welded plate connections, and I check the corner posts for the accumulated axial load from every unit above. What I do not accept is stacking on makeshift blocking or partial bearing — the load must land on the castings, centered, with a connection that resists both gravity and uplift. Wind uplift on a light steel box is significant, so the base container gets anchored to the foundation for the full overturning demand.
Do container buildings rust out?
They can, which is why condition assessment and coating are structural issues, not cosmetic ones. Containers are weathering steel with marine coatings, but used units often arrive with the coating compromised and active corrosion underway — especially at the bottom rails, corner castings, and anywhere water ponds. I require inspection and rejection criteria in the specifications, then a proper surface-prep and coating system for the accepted units. A container building with its corrosion managed will outlast the mortgage; one without it becomes a maintenance project within a decade.
