How Are Boise Roofs Designed for Snow Loads?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Boise roofs are designed for snow loads by taking the ASCE 7 ground snow load for the site, adjusting it for roof slope, exposure, and thermal conditions, then adding unbalanced and drift loads at parapets, valleys, and step roofs - and checking that the structure and connections handle those loads safely.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

From Ground Snow to Roof Snow

The ground snow load is the starting point, not the answer. ASCE 7 and the local jurisdiction publish ground snow values derived from decades of weather data, and the engineer converts that to a roof load with a set of adjustment factors. The slope factor reduces load on steep, slippery roofs that shed snow and increases the effective demand on low-slope roofs that hold it. The exposure factor accounts for whether wind can scour the roof - an open, windswept site carries less than a sheltered one. The thermal factor reflects heat loss through the roof: a well-insulated cold roof holds snow longer than a warm roof that melts it from below. The importance factor then scales the load to the building's risk category - a hospital or school gets a higher factor than a storage shed, because the consequences of failure differ. Minimum roof snow loads apply regardless of the calculation, so very low ground-snow cases still produce a designable load. The engineer documents each factor and its source in the calculations, because the plan reviewer checks the chain from ground snow to member design. A missing or misapplied factor is one of the most common corrections in structural plan review.

Drift, Unbalanced Loads, and the Re-Roof Check

Drift is the silent overload. Wherever wind-blown snow piles against an obstruction - a parapet, a rooftop unit, the step where a low roof meets a taller wall - the engineer calculates a drift surcharge and applies it as a triangular load on the structure below. The drift height grows with the upwind fetch and the ground snow load, and on large commercial roofs the surcharge can govern the design of entire bays. Unbalanced loads cover the windward-scour, leeward-deposit pattern on pitched roofs, plus the sliding snow case where snow sheds off an upper roof onto a lower one. Each of these is a separate load case in the analysis, not an afterthought. Re-roofs deserve the same rigor as new designs. Swapping roofing, adding insulation, or installing solar changes the dead load, the thermal factor, or both - and the original structure may predate current snow provisions entirely. The engineer surveys the existing framing, confirms member sizes and spans, and runs the current loads through the actual structure. Where capacity falls short, the fix might be sistering joists, adding beams, or limiting the new assembly's weight. It is far cheaper to find the shortfall on paper than under a February snowpack.

What else do project teams ask?

What ground snow load does Boise use?
The design ground snow load is set by the authority having jurisdiction - the City of Boise publishes its value for sites inside city limits, and Ada County publishes its own for unincorporated areas, with higher values typical at foothill elevations. The engineer confirms the site-specific number rather than assuming the valley figure, because the structural design and the plan reviewer both key off it.
What is a snow drift load, and why does it matter?
Wind piles snow against obstructions like parapets, rooftop units, and step roofs, creating a triangular surcharge that can be several times the uniform snow load over a narrow strip. Drift loads govern many commercial roof designs in Boise because they concentrate force exactly where the structure may be weakest - at the step between roof levels or along a parapet line.
Do I need an engineer when re-roofing in Boise?
Often, yes. A re-roof changes dead load and insulation, which changes the thermal factor the original design assumed - and many older roofs were never designed for current snow provisions. The engineer verifies the existing framing against today's loads and specifies reinforcement where it falls short. Most jurisdictions require structural review when the roofing assembly's weight or the building's use changes.
Does the permit path differ between Boise and Ada County?
Yes. Inside city limits, the City of Boise's planning and development services reviews the structural package against the city's adopted snow load and submittal checklist. In unincorporated Ada County, the county is the AHJ with its own forms, published loads, and review timelines. The engineer confirms the jurisdiction and its ground snow value before calculating, because both the numbers and the paperwork differ. The AHJ decides approvals.
