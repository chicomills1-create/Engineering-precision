What Does a Structural PE Stamp Cover in Arlington, TX?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A structural PE stamp in Arlington certifies the gravity system, lateral system, and foundations after a Texas-licensed PE verifies the calculations under responsible charge per TBPELS Rule 137.33 — covering everything from entertainment-district commercial to corridor industrial, all on expansive North Texas clay.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Arlington submittal path

Structural permits in Arlington run through the city's plan review process, where the sealed drawings, calculations package, and geotechnical report are submitted electronically and routed to the discipline reviewers. I confirm the current intake checklist before uploading, because file standards and submittal requirements get updated and a set that fails intake never reaches a reviewer. The seal block gets its own look at intake — Texas PE seal plus the firm's name and F-number — so the registration paperwork is settled before engineering review begins. Corrections come back consolidated per cycle, and the resubmittal answers every item with clearly clouded changes. The submittals that move fastest through Arlington are the complete ones: full structural set, calc package, and geotechnical report in the first upload, with the design criteria and code references shown on the sheets.

Lateral design across Arlington's building mix

The lateral review follows the building type, and Arlington has several. Long-span commercial and assembly buildings need lateral systems that work around open floor plates — moment frames, braced frames, or shear walls placed where architecture allows, with diaphragms that actually deliver the loads there. Tilt-up industrial lives or dies on its connections: panel-to-foundation, panel-to-roof, and the roof diaphragm's ability to span between panels. Multifamily podium buildings need the transfer level and the lateral continuity through it checked carefully. Across all of them, North Texas wind is the demand — straight-line storms are the local lateral event — and the PE verifies the wind design criteria, the load path continuity, and that every connection detail matches the calculated reactions. Vague lateral sheets are the fastest route to a correction list.

Arlington structural submittal checklist

A structural package is ready for the PE's review — and then for the city — when these items are resolved. System-appropriate lateral detailing and honest foundations are the themes. • Texas-licensed PE in responsible charge under TBPELS Rule 137.33, with firm name and F-number on the seal block • Geotechnical report with boring logs and site-specific foundation recommendations for expansive clay • Structural design criteria, load combinations, and wind parameters shown on the drawings • Gravity framing complete with member sizes traceable to the calculations package • Lateral system suited to the building type, with a detailed continuous load path • Connection details — especially tilt-up panel and long-span connections — matching calculated demands

What else do project teams ask?

What structural systems does an Arlington PE review most often?
The Arlington mix is broad. Around the entertainment district and the commercial corridors, I see a lot of long-span steel and concrete framing for assembly, retail, and hospitality — buildings where the lateral system has to work around big open spaces and tall storefronts. Along the industrial corridors, tilt-up concrete dominates, which puts the emphasis on panel design, connections, and roof diaphragm action. Multifamily brings podium construction and mid-rise wood or steel framing with their own lateral and detailing demands. The PE's review is calibrated to the system in front of it: the failure patterns of a tilt-up warehouse and a long-span retail building are completely different, and the verification follows the building, not a template.
How do Arlington's expansive soils affect foundation review?
The same way they affect it across North Texas: the clays swell when wet and shrink when dry, and foundations designed for stable ground crack and heave. Before sealing, the PE needs the geotechnical report's boring logs, plasticity data, and site-specific recommendations, and then checks the foundation design against them — pier depths below the active zone, slab stiffness, post-tensioning where it's used, and perimeter moisture control. Arlington's mix of new development and redevelopment means some sites also carry fill or variable soils from prior use, which the report has to address. I don't seal foundation designs on assumed parameters in this market, period.
What does responsible charge require of the sealing PE in Texas?
TBPELS Rule 137.33 requires that the PE only seal work they performed or that was performed under their direct supervision — the PE takes professional responsibility for the engineering. Practically, that means the sealing engineer reviewed the structural scheme for code compliance, verified or re-performed the calculations, checked the details for constructability, marked every required revision, and re-checked the corrections. The seal block also carries the firm's name and Texas F-number. Responsible charge is the legal backbone of the entire review-and-seal model: it's what makes the stamp a statement of engineering judgment rather than a signature on someone else's drawings.
Can a PE seal structural drawings that another engineer designed?
Yes — but only through a genuine review-and-seal engagement, never by signing the set as-is. The Texas-licensed PE takes responsible charge, which means reviewing the design for code compliance, verifying the calculations, checking details and coordination, marking required revisions, and re-checking the corrections before sealing. If the scheme has fundamental problems — a lateral system that doesn't suit the building, foundations the geotechnical report won't support — the honest answer is redesign, not a stamp. Rule 137.33 makes the sealing engineer professionally responsible for the design, so no competent PE seals a structural set they haven't truly reviewed, regardless of whose title block it started in.
