How Is Plumbing Designed for Grocery Stores and Food Prep Areas?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design grease interceptors for food prep waste, trap-primed floor drainage, zoned hot water with reclaim preheat, and backflow prevention at every hazard connection.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Grease Interceptors and Food Prep Drainage

Grease interceptors are sized from the fixture units and flow rates of everything upstream — pot sinks, pre-rinse sinks, floor drains in cooking areas, and any fixture that can carry fats, oils, and grease. The engineer locates interceptors outside the food prep area where possible, with direct access for the pump-out truck, because an interceptor that cannot be serviced will be neglected and will fail. Inside the prep areas, drainage is designed for sanitation: floor drains with trap primers or deep-seal traps in every wet area, coved floor-to-wall transitions by the architect, and indirect waste connections with air gaps for food prep sinks, ice machines, and walk-in evaporator drains. The code requires the air gap; the engineer makes sure it is actually buildable and maintainable rather than a detail that gets value-engineered into a violation.

Hot Water Zoning and Backflow Prevention

Not all hot water is the same. Warewashing and sanitation need high-temperature water with proper recovery, hand sinks need tempered water with anti-scald protection, and mop/service sinks need their own supply. The engineer zones the system accordingly, sizes heaters and storage for the cleanup-shift peak — the hardest hour of the day — and integrates heat reclaim preheat where the refrigeration design provides it. Backflow prevention protects the public water supply from the store's hazards. Reduced-pressure-zone assemblies guard irrigation, beverage carbonators, chemical dispensers, and boiler makeup; the design shows each assembly's location with the clearances testers need for annual certification. Cross-connection control is documented on the plans because the health department and the water purveyor both review it.

Grocery Plumbing Design Checklist

A complete grocery plumbing design protects food and water: • Fixture and equipment schedule covering every deli, bakery, meat, and produce water use • Grease interceptors sized for fixture flow with pump-out truck access • Floor drains with trap primers and indirect waste with air gaps in food areas • Hot water zoned by temperature need with recovery sized for the cleanup peak • Heat reclaim preheat integration with the refrigeration system • Backflow preventers at every hazard with tester clearances documented • Water, waste, and vent sizing per code with health department coordination

What else do project teams ask?

How is a grease interceptor sized for a grocery deli?
Sizing starts with the fixtures discharging to it — each sink, dishwasher, and floor drain contributes flow based on its fixture units or rated GPM — combined with the grease production of the menu. The engineer applies the plumbing code's sizing method, selects the interceptor volume that provides adequate retention time for grease separation at peak flow, and verifies the location allows pump-out access. Undersized interceptors pass grease to the sewer, which brings fines and blockages; the design sizes honestly for the real menu.
Why do floor drains in food prep areas need trap primers?
A trap holds a water seal that blocks sewer gas from entering the room, but in a floor drain that rarely sees water, the seal evaporates — leaving an open path for gas and pests into a food area. Trap primers automatically add a small amount of water to keep the seal full. The engineer specifies primers (or deep-seal traps, or both) on every food-area floor drain because a dried trap in a deli is both a health code violation and a customer complaint waiting to happen.
What is an air gap and where is it required?
An air gap is a physical vertical separation between a drain line and the receiving drain — the pipe ends above the flood rim so wastewater can never siphon back into the fixture. Plumbing and health codes require indirect waste with air gaps for food prep sinks, ice machines, steam equipment, and walk-in cooler drains. The engineer details each air gap location so it is visible and maintainable; hidden or improper connections are among the most common health inspection findings.
Can refrigeration heat reclaim preheat domestic hot water?
Yes — it is one of the best pairings in the store. The compressor racks reject heat continuously while the store needs hot water for warewashing, cleanup, and hand sinks. Reclaim preheats the incoming cold water before it reaches the water heaters, cutting their energy use substantially. The engineer sizes the preheat exchange, adds storage to smooth demand spikes, and keeps conventional heaters as the final temperature guarantee so food safety temperatures are never at the mercy of the refrigeration load.
