What Engineering Letters Do Owner-Builders Need for Permits?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Owner-builders need PE letters when the AHJ asks for an engineer's statement on a specific condition — structural observations, adequacy of existing elements, or special-inspection closeouts. Each sealed letter follows real investigation or analysis, states exactly what was verified, and answers the reviewer's written question precisely.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The three letters owner-builders meet most

First, the structural observation letter: the PE visits the site at the required stages (often before cover — framing, shear walls, holdowns, foundation steel), observes the work against the sealed drawings, and writes the sealed letter stating conformance or noting the deficiencies to correct. I coordinate observation visits with the owner-builder's inspection schedule so one trip serves both the observation and the inspection prep. The letter goes into the permit file and the inspector reads it — a clean observation letter smooths every subsequent inspection. Second, the adequacy or evaluation letter for existing conditions: the owner-builder's project incorporates something already there — an existing foundation, a retaining wall, a structure being added to — and the AHJ wants an engineer's statement that it is adequate for the new loads. I investigate: review whatever documentation exists, visit and observe, perform the structural analysis, and write the letter with the basis, the calculations referenced, and the limitations stated plainly. Where the existing element cannot be verified without destructive investigation, I say so and recommend the investigation — a letter that overstates its basis is worse than no letter. Third, the special-inspection final letter: where the permit required special inspections (structural masonry, driven piles, spray-applied fireproofing, certain welding), the inspecting engineer's final sealed letter summarizes the inspections performed and states conformance with the approved documents. I run special-inspection programs with the reporting built in — each inspection documented as it happens — so the final letter writes itself from the record. Owner-builders sometimes discover the special-inspection requirement at the inspection stage; the time to engage the inspecting engineer is at permit issuance, when the inspection program gets defined.

Getting the letter accepted the first time

Acceptance is about precision. The letter must answer the reviewer's written question, reference the permit number and the specific element, state the PE's license and seal it properly (including digital seal compliance where the AHJ requires it), and limit its statements to what was actually investigated. I also copy the relevant backup into the submittal package — the observation notes, the calculation summary, the inspection reports — because a letter with its evidence attached gets accepted while a bare assertion gets questioned. The other half of acceptance is timing. Observation letters must precede cover — I tell owner-builders to call me when the work is ready for observation, not after the drywall crew has been scheduled. Adequacy letters should precede the correction response they support. Special-inspection letters close out the inspection program before final inspection is requested. And every letter engagement starts with the written ask from the AHJ, so the letter and the question match exactly. Precision plus timing: that is the whole acceptance strategy, and it works in every jurisdiction I practice in.

Owner-builder PE letter checklist

Letters are precision instruments — aim them exactly. • Get the AHJ's request in writing: the specific question, the element, the code section — before engaging the PE • Match the letter to the job: observation letters need site visits before cover; adequacy letters need analysis; never substitute a letter for required sealed drawings • Schedule observations with the inspection sequence — one coordinated trip serves the observation and the inspection prep • Require the sealed letter to state its basis and limitations plainly — restraint is what makes the AHJ trust it • Attach the evidence: observation notes, calculation summaries, inspection reports travel with the letter

What else do project teams ask?

The counter asked for an 'engineer's letter.' What do I do first?
Get the request in writing — the reviewer's comment, the correction notice, or an email stating exactly what the letter must address. "Engineer's letter" is not a scope; it is a category, and the PE needs the specific question: which element, what condition, which code section. Then engage the PE with that written request plus access to the site and any existing documentation. I scope the letter engagement from the written ask: site visit needed or analysis only, what investigation the question requires, and what the letter will state. Owner-builders who skip the "in writing" step reliably end up paying for two letters — one answering the assumed question, one answering the real one.
What is a structural observation letter, and when is it required?
It is the PE's sealed statement that they visited the site, observed specific structural work, and found it in general conformance with the approved plans and the engineering intent. AHJs require it where the permit conditions call for structural observation — common on owner-builder projects with engineered lateral systems, retaining walls, or foundation work — and at final inspection where the reviewer wants the engineer's eyes on the finished structural work. The letter describes what was observed and when; it is not a guarantee of every concealed condition, and I state its scope and limitations explicitly. Schedule the observation before the work gets covered — a letter about framing written after drywall is a letter about nothing.
Can a PE letter substitute for sealed drawings?
No — they are different instruments for different jobs. Sealed drawings certify the design; a letter addresses a specific question about a condition, an observation, or an inspection. When the AHJ's correction requires design — resizing, re-detailing, new analysis — the answer is revised sealed drawings, not a letter explaining the old ones. I turn down letter requests that are really drawing requests in disguise, because a letter cannot carry design responsibility it was never structured to carry. If the reviewer wants engineering, give them engineering: the sealed revision with the calculations behind it.
How fast can I get a PE letter for my permit?
It depends on what the letter requires: a letter based on document review and analysis can sometimes turn in days; a structural observation letter requires scheduling a site visit, and the letter follows the visit — no legitimate PE writes an observation letter without observing. Special-inspection letters follow the inspection program's completion. I give owner-builders an honest timeline at engagement based on the written ask, and I prioritize permit letters because I know the inspection or the correction round is waiting on them. What I will not do is backdate, pre-write, or otherwise shortcut the investigation — the letter's value to the AHJ is precisely that it was earned.
