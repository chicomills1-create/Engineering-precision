Do I Need a PE Stamp for a Rooftop Solar Installation?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Most jurisdictions require a PE-stamped structural review for rooftop solar, verifying the existing roof can carry the added load, the mounting or ballast system is adequate, and the array resists wind uplift in every roof zone — with Florida's hurricane wind checks being the most demanding.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Verifying the existing roof

Every rooftop solar review stands on the existing structure assessment. I start with the available structural drawings and work forward: what is the framing system, what are the member sizes and spans, what was the original design load, and how much capacity remains for the solar addition? The array's distributed weight is usually modest, but concentrated loads at attachment points and the ballast weight of unattached systems can govern locally. Where drawings are missing or the building's history suggests modifications, field verification fills the gap — measuring members, confirming spans, and checking condition, because deterioration or prior alterations change the capacity math. Roof condition matters beyond structure. The review notes the roofing membrane's age and state, because a solar array with a 25-year life on a roof with five years left is a planning failure the PE should flag even though it's not strictly structural. Drainage matters too — ballasted systems and rail layouts that pond water create problems no structural calculation fixes. A good solar structural review sees the roof as a system, not just a platform.

Wind design for the array

The wind review follows the code's component-and-cladding logic applied to the array as roof-mounted equipment. I check that the design wind speed and exposure match the site, that the array layout respects the roof's pressure zones — with edge and corner zones carrying the higher uplift coefficients — and that the mounting hardware has tested capacities meeting or exceeding the calculated demands in each zone. For attached systems, that means the connection into the framing: fastener pullout and lateral capacity in the actual wood or steel, with the right safety factors. For ballasted systems, it means the dead weight in each zone demonstrably resists the uplift and sliding demands without credit for friction the code doesn't allow. In Florida and the Gulf Coast, I run this check at the jurisdiction's full design wind speed with the hurricane-zone detailing the local amendments require, and I confirm the racking system carries the product approvals the AHJ expects. Plan check in these markets will ask for the wind calculation trail — pressures, zones, hardware capacities — and the sealed set provides it without a second request.

Rooftop solar PE review checklist

A solar structural submittal is ready for the PE's seal when the existing roof, the mounting, and the wind design are all verified. Guessing at any of the three earns a correction. • Existing roof structure documented — framing, member sizes, spans — field-verified where drawings are unreliable • Array layout with panel weights, mounting type, and attachment or ballast details fully specified • Added dead loads checked against remaining roof capacity, including concentrated attachment and ballast loads • Wind uplift analysis by roof zone, with hardware capacities or ballast weights adequate in every zone • Waterproofing and flashing details for attached systems; drainage review for ballasted layouts • Racking product approvals where the AHJ requires them, especially in Florida's hurricane zones

What else do project teams ask?

What's the difference between attached and ballasted solar mounting structurally?
Attached systems penetrate the roof and connect mechanically to the framing — the engineering verifies the connection capacity into the actual members, the added point loads on those members, and the waterproofing details at every penetration. Ballasted systems sit on the roof held by weight — the engineering verifies the roof can carry the ballast plus panels as added dead load and proves the wind design keeps the array stable without mechanical attachment. Ballasted avoids penetrations but adds more weight; attached adds less weight but lives or dies on connection quality. I see the most problems with attached systems on roofs where the framing layout was assumed rather than verified — the lag bolts have to hit structure, not hope.
Why is wind uplift such a big deal for solar permits in Florida?
Because Florida's design wind speeds are among the highest in the country, and solar panels are essentially sails mounted on the most wind-exposed surface of the building. The code's wind provisions assign much higher uplift pressures to roof edges, corners, and ridges than to the interior field — and arrays often extend into exactly those zones. Florida AHJs, especially in the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone, scrutinize the wind calculations, the mounting hardware's tested capacities, and product approvals for the racking system. A solar structural set for Florida that treats wind as an afterthought will not survive plan check. The review has to show the array staying put in the design event, zone by zone.
Does the PE need to visit the site for a solar stamp?
Often, yes — or the review needs reliable documentation of the existing roof. If the building has trustworthy as-built structural drawings and no history of undocumented modifications, a thorough desk review can suffice. But for older buildings, buildings with prior roof work, or any case where the drawings and the structure might disagree, I field-verify: framing type, member sizes, spans, and condition. Sealing a solar review on assumed existing structure is exactly the kind of shortcut responsible charge prohibits. The site visit is cheap insurance against the most common failure mode in solar structural work.
What about solar on carports or ground mounts — same PE review?
The PE review applies, but the engineering questions shift. Carport and canopy solar structures are new structures: the PE designs or verifies the columns, beams, and foundations for gravity, wind, and seismic loads, with open-structure wind behavior that differs from rooftop arrays. Ground mounts need foundation or ballast design for the site's soils and wind exposure. In both cases the review is fuller than a rooftop addition because there's no existing building carrying the load — the support structure itself is the engineered product. The permit submittal includes the structural drawings and calculations for the support system, sealed by the PE in responsible charge.
