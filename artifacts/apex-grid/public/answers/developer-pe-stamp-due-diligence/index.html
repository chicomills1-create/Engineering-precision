What Engineering Due Diligence Does a Developer Need Before Closing on a Site?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Developers need PE-level engineering due diligence before closing: a feasibility review of soils, floodplain, utilities, and zoning constraints, plus a permit-path map showing which AHJ, what sealed engineering, and where the entitlement risks sit — delivered as a written findings memo to underwrite against.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What the feasibility review covers

I work through the site systematically. Soils and foundations first: the existing geotechnical data gets read for bearing conditions, expansive-soil risk, groundwater, and what foundation systems the site will support — and if there is no geotechnical data, that is itself a finding, priced as the cost of the investigation plus the foundation contingency the unknown requires. Floodplain and drainage next: the FEMA maps, the local drainage criteria, and whether the site's grading concept can actually move water the way the jurisdiction requires. Then utilities — water, sewer, power, gas — checked for capacity and proximity, because off-site extensions are the budget item developers most consistently underestimate. Zoning and entitlements get an engineering read: setbacks, height limits, density, parking ratios, and landscape requirements tested against the program massing to confirm the intended project fits the entitlements envelope — or to quantify exactly what variance or rezoning risk the program carries. Finally the permit path: which AHJ has jurisdiction, what their submittal requirements and review timelines look like, what sealed engineering the project will need, and where the discretionary approvals sit. The memo ties it together as priced risks with recommended next steps, written for the investment committee, not for engineers.

Red flags that kill or reprice deals

Some findings are fatal, most are repricing events, and the diligence memo distinguishes them clearly. Fatal flaws are rare but real: a site substantially in a regulatory floodway with no feasible mitigation, contamination beyond the project's remediation budget, or access that cannot be legally secured. More common are the repricers: expansive soils that move the foundation budget up by six figures, a sewer district capacity fee or off-site main extension the pro forma never carried, a zoning overlay that cuts achievable density by twenty percent, or an entitlement path requiring a full rezoning with a twelve-month timeline the capital stack cannot absorb. I price every finding in ranges, because the developer's decision is always about money and time, not about engineering elegance. A $400,000 foundation upgrade on a $20 million project is a renegotiation; on a $6 million project it may be a walk-away. The memo also sequences the findings: which risks can be retired with more investigation during diligence, which require design-phase engineering to resolve, and which are permanent site characteristics the underwriting must simply carry. Developers who get this memo before close make decisions with leverage; developers who discover the same facts during construction make them with a general contractor's change orders.

Pre-close engineering diligence checklist

Commission these during the feasibility contingency — in this order — and close with eyes open. • Fatal-flaw screen in week one: floodplain status, zoning fit for the program, obvious geotechnical or access red flags • Geotechnical data review (or scope the investigation if none exists) — foundation risk is the largest silent budget item • Utility capacity and proximity check with the serving districts — price any off-site extensions before underwriting • Zoning and entitlement analysis: setbacks, density, height, parking tested against program massing; discretionary approval risks named • Permit-path map: the AHJ, submittal requirements, sealed-engineering scope, review timelines, and correction-round expectations

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between engineering due diligence and a Phase I environmental?
A Phase I environmental assesses contamination risk — recognized environmental conditions on the site. Engineering due diligence assesses buildability: soils and foundations, floodplain and drainage, utility capacity, access and grading, zoning and setback feasibility, and the permit path with its sealed-engineering requirements. They are complementary, not interchangeable, and sophisticated developers order both during the feasibility period. I have seen clean Phase I reports on sites that were unbuildable for engineering reasons — the environmental consultant is not checking whether the sewer main has capacity for your unit count.
How much does engineering due diligence cost relative to the risk?
A PE-level feasibility review typically runs a small fraction of one percent of total project cost — thousands of dollars against a multi-million-dollar development — and it prices risks that routinely run into six or seven figures: foundation upgrades on bad soils, off-site utility extensions, floodplain mitigation, entitlement redesign. I scope diligence to the site's risk profile: a flat infill lot with existing utilities gets a lighter review than a hillside parcel with no geotechnical data. Developers should think of it as the highest-return spending on the project — it either confirms the underwriting or kills a bad deal before the earnest money goes hard.
Can due diligence be done during the feasibility contingency period?
That is exactly when it should be done. I structure diligence deliverables to the contingency calendar: a quick-turn fatal-flaw screen in the first week or two (floodplain, zoning, obvious geotechnical red flags), then the deeper feasibility memo before the contingency expires. The findings memo is written for decision-making — risks named, priced in ranges, and tied to specific next steps — so the developer can renegotiate price, adjust program, extend diligence, or terminate with cause. Order it the day the LOI is signed; diligence that arrives after the contingency expires is just expensive hindsight.
Does the diligence PE become the project's engineer of record?
Not automatically, but it is often the natural progression — and there are advantages when it happens. The diligence PE already knows the site's constraints, the AHJ's posture, and the program's engineering drivers, so the EOR engagement starts with months of context. There is no conflict: diligence is advisory, sealing is responsible charge, and the same PE can do both sequentially. I tell developers to select the diligence PE the way they would select the EOR — licensed in the project state, experienced with the AHJ — because on a good site, that is exactly who they become.
