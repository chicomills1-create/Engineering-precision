What Does a Structural PE Review for an Owner-Builder?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A structural PE review verifies foundations against the soils report, gravity framing against the loads, and the complete lateral load path — with independently checked calculations and required revisions made — before sealing. For owner-builders, the review also produces a genuinely buildable structural set.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Foundations: where the review starts

I start every owner-builder structural review at the ground, because everything above inherits the foundation's assumptions. The soils report gets read first — bearing values, expansive-soil potential, groundwater, the recommended foundation system — and the foundation design gets checked against it line by line: footing sizes against the bearing capacity, pier depths against the active zone, slab detailing against the expansiveness, drainage and moisture protection against the site's water behavior. The most common foundation finding on owner-builder reviews is a design that ignores the soils report entirely — a conventional slab drawn for soil the report describes as highly expansive. Where no soils report exists, I determine whether the project can proceed on presumptive values or whether the investigation is non-negotiable — and I make that call conservatively, because the PE sealing the foundation owns the geotechnical assumption. I also review the foundation's constructability for the owner-builder specifically: formed footings versus trench footings, pier drilling access on the actual site, the drainage detailing the owner will actually install. A foundation design the owner cannot build correctly is not a good design, however elegant the calculations.

Framing and lateral: the two systems above grade

The gravity review is methodical: every beam, joist, header, post, and slab checked against the tributary loads, with spans, species and grade (for wood), and connection capacities verified. I pay special attention to the conditions owner-built designs get wrong — the long header over the great-room opening, the point load from the post above landing mid-span on the beam below, the deck ledger connection, the stair opening framed as an afterthought. Each gets a calculated answer and a detail, because "looks about right" is not a structural design method I can seal. The lateral review is where the PE earns the fee. I trace the complete load path — diaphragms, shear walls or frames, holdowns, straps, anchor bolts — verifying capacities and, just as importantly, buildability: a holdown the framer cannot install in the detailed location is a failed detail. Shear wall schedules get checked against the architectural openings (the wall the lateral design needs is so often the wall with the big window), and the foundation anchorage gets verified against the accumulated uplift. When the gravity system is verified, the lateral path is continuous and detailed, and the revisions are incorporated and re-reviewed, the structural set is ready for the seal — and for the framer.

Owner-builder structural review checklist

Bring these to the review and the process moves fast. • Soils report in hand before the foundation review starts — or a clear PE decision on presumptive values • The most complete drawings you have: plans, sections, framing intent, truss quotes, the lumber-yard takeoff — everything is input • Expect the lateral system to get the most scrutiny: shear walls, holdowns, drag struts, and the continuous load path • Plan for one to three revision rounds in the budget and schedule — the rounds are the design becoming sealable • Build from the sealed set: the details, schedules, and connection callouts are there so the 6 a.m. framing crew gets it right

What else do project teams ask?

My lumber yard gave me a framing takeoff. Is that engineered?
No — a takeoff is a materials list, not an engineering design. It tells you what to buy, not whether the structure works: no load calculations, no lateral system design, no connection engineering, no foundation design, and no PE seal. I see owner-builders arrive with takeoffs expecting the PE to "check them over," and the honest answer is that the review starts from the structural design, which the takeoff is not. Bring the takeoff — it is useful information about the intended framing — but expect the engagement to include real structural engineering under the PE's responsible charge before anything is sealable.
What is a lateral load path, and why does the PE care so much about it?
The load path is the continuous chain that carries wind and seismic forces from where they hit the building down to the foundation: roof diaphragm to shear walls, shear walls through holdowns to the floor below, accumulating down to the foundation anchorage. Every link needs a calculated capacity and a buildable detail — one missing holdown or one unblocked diaphragm breaks the chain. I care because lateral failures are the catastrophic ones: gravity problems sag and crack, lateral problems collapse. On owner-builder reviews the lateral system is where I spend the most time, because it is where non-engineered designs most often have nothing at all.
Do I need a soils report before the structural review?
For any foundation design beyond the simplest prescriptive footing, yes — and I require it before sealing foundations in expansive-soil or hillside country, which covers much of Arizona, Texas, and California. The PE cannot verify a foundation design without knowing what it bears on; "assume good soil" is not an engineering assumption I will seal. If your AHJ does not require a soils report for your project type, I may be able to work from conservative presumptive values — but that is my professional call under responsible charge, made after seeing the site conditions. Order the investigation early; everything structural waits on it.
How many revision rounds does a typical owner-builder review take?
Usually one to three, driven by the starting point's completeness. A set drawn by a competent designer with a soils report in hand often clears in one solid revision round; an owner-sketched concept with a lumber-yard takeoff takes more, because the review is doing genuine design development along the way. I quote the engagement with a defined number of review rounds and a clear rate for additional rounds, so there are no surprises. The rounds are not a penalty — each one is the design getting closer to something I can professionally stand behind, which is the entire point of the exercise.
