How Is a Commercial Building Designed in an Iowa Floodplain?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Commercial buildings in Iowa floodplains are designed by first establishing the base flood elevation, then elevating the lowest floor above it or dry-floodproofing the structure, with foundations engineered for hydrostatic and hydrodynamic forces, flood-resistant materials below the flood level, and permits through the city or county floodplain administrator under the local floodplain ordinance.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Elevation Versus Dry-Floodproofing

Elevation is the most reliable floodplain strategy and the one engineers prefer when the site allows it. The lowest floor — including basements — is built above the base flood elevation plus any locally required freeboard, often on fill, stem walls, or piers. Enclosed areas below the elevated floor need flood openings sized to let water enter and exit automatically, equalizing hydrostatic pressure so walls are not pushed in. The engineer designs the elevated foundation for the flood forces and documents everything on an elevation certificate that the floodplain administrator and the owner's insurer both rely on. Dry-floodproofing keeps the building at grade but makes it watertight up to the flood level. Walls are sealed or built of impermeable materials, openings get removable flood shields or permanent watertight doors, and the structure is engineered to resist the full hydrostatic and hydrodynamic load without failing or leaking excessively. This approach suits existing commercial buildings and downtown sites where elevation is impractical, but it demands rigorous detailing — every penetration, joint, and shield seat is a potential leak path — and a professional certification of the floodproofing design. Wet-floodproofing, which allows controlled flooding of unoccupied areas with flood-resistant materials, is a third option for certain accessory spaces.

Flood Forces on Foundations and Walls

The structural calculations for a floodplain building cover a load set that starts with hydrostatic pressure: water against a wall exerts a triangular pressure distribution that grows with depth, and the engineer designs walls, slabs, and connections for it. Buoyancy is checked by comparing the building's weight to the displaced water volume at the design flood depth — a light commercial building on a slab can literally float if this is ignored, so the design adds weight, anchors, or flood vents as needed. Where the site is subject to moving water, hydrodynamic drag and debris impact add lateral loads that the foundation must transfer to the ground. Below the base flood elevation, material selection is governed by survivability. The engineer specifies concrete and masonry for walls, avoids moisture-sensitive finishes, and details connections with corrosion-resistant hardware. Utilities get the same treatment: electrical service equipment, transformers, and HVAC units are elevated above the flood level or housed in floodproof enclosures, and sanitary sewers get backflow prevention so floodwater does not back up into the building. Every one of these decisions is documented for the permit, because the floodplain administrator reviews the structural and MEP drawings against the ordinance before issuing approval.

Iowa's Local Floodplain Permitting

In Iowa, floodplain permits are administered locally — by the city if the site is inside city limits, or by the county for unincorporated areas — under each community's floodplain management ordinance. Many Iowa cities along the Cedar, Iowa, and Des Moines rivers enforce freeboard above the federal base flood elevation and restrict floodway development more tightly than the minimum standards, a direct response to the state's major flood events. The engineer verifies which ordinance applies and what it requires before the design is finalized, because freeboard and floodway rules change the building height and site layout. The submittal typically includes a site plan showing the floodplain and floodway boundaries, structural drawings with the lowest-floor elevation clearly noted, floodproofing details and certifications where applicable, and hydraulic analysis if any work occurs in the floodway. Review timelines differ: a city floodplain administrator may coordinate review with the building department in one combined process, while a county review can run on a separate track with its own meeting schedule. The authority having jurisdiction decides approvals, and the engineer's job is to give that reviewer a complete, ordinance-matched package the first time.

What else do project teams ask?

What is the base flood elevation?
The base flood elevation is the computed height floodwater is expected to reach during the 1%-annual-chance (100-year) flood, shown on FEMA Flood Insurance Rate Maps. Iowa floodplain designs key everything off this number: lowest floors must sit above it (plus any local freeboard), and dry-floodproofing must protect the building up to it. An elevation certificate documents the as-built heights relative to this elevation.
Can a commercial building be constructed in the floodway?
It is heavily restricted. The floodway is the channel plus adjacent land that must stay clear to carry floodwater, and most ordinances prohibit development there unless a licensed engineer demonstrates through hydraulic analysis that the project causes no rise in flood levels. Most Iowa commercial projects are sited in the flood fringe instead, where elevation or floodproofing satisfies the ordinance.
What materials survive flooding below the flood level?
Flood-resistant materials include concrete, masonry, steel, pressure-treated or naturally decay-resistant lumber, closed-cell foam insulation, and ceramic tile. Standard gypsum drywall, fiberglass batt insulation, particleboard, and carpet are not flood-resistant and should not be used below the base flood elevation — they absorb water, harbor mold, and must be torn out after every flood.
Who issues the floodplain permit — the city or the county?
It depends on the site's location: cities administer floodplain permits inside their limits, and counties handle unincorporated areas, each under its own floodplain ordinance. Requirements, freeboard, review timelines, and submittal portals differ between jurisdictions. The engineer confirms the authority having jurisdiction early, because a design that satisfies one Iowa community's ordinance may need adjustments for the neighboring one.
