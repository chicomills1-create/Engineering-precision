How Is Liquefaction Near Utah Lake Designed Against?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Near Utah Lake, engineers design against liquefaction by investigating saturated lakebed soils, then either improving the ground with stone columns or deep soil mixing, or bypassing it with piles and drilled shafts to stable soil - and detailing the structure to tolerate residual settlement and lateral spreading.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

How the Liquefaction Hazard Is Quantified

The geotechnical investigation is the foundation of every liquefaction design near Utah Lake. Borings are advanced through the full depth of the young lakebed deposits, with in-situ testing - standard penetration tests, cone penetration tests, or shear-wave velocity measurements - that measures how dense and how saturated each layer is. Groundwater levels are recorded, sometimes with monitoring wells, because liquefaction requires saturation: dry soils do not liquefy. The lab work classifies fines content and plasticity, which control whether a soil layer can actually trigger. The triggering analysis then compares the soil's cyclic resistance against the earthquake demand the code assigns the site - a function of the design ground motion, the magnitude of the controlling earthquake, and the overburden stress at each depth. The output is a depth profile of safety factors, plus estimates of post-liquefaction settlement and lateral spread displacement. Those numbers become design inputs: the structural engineer sizes foundations for the degraded bearing capacity and details for the predicted movements. Without this analysis, any foundation near the lake is guesswork.

Ground Improvement vs. Deep Foundations

Ground improvement treats the soil itself. Stone columns - compacted gravel piers installed on a grid - densify loose sands and provide vertical drains that relieve the pore-water pressure spikes driving liquefaction. Compaction grouting injects grout bulbs that displace and compact surrounding soil. Deep soil mixing blends cement into the ground to form stiff columns. The choice depends on soil type, depth of the liquefiable layer, and what sits above: stone columns suit thick clean sands, grouting suits variable fills, and soil mixing suits sites where the engineer wants the columns themselves to carry load. Every method ends with verification testing, because the design is only as good as the installed result. Deep foundations skip the problem layer instead of fixing it. Driven piles or drilled shafts pass through the liquefiable soils into dense sand, gravel, or bedrock below, so the building's support does not depend on ground that may temporarily lose strength. The design still accounts for liquefaction: the piles are designed for downdrag as settling soil drags on the shafts, for loss of lateral support in the liquefied zone, and for the lateral spreading forces where the site slopes toward the lake or a canal. Pile caps, grade beams, and utility connections are detailed for differential movement. Either path - improve or bypass - produces a building the engineer can stand behind; the geotechnical report recommends which fits the site.

What else do project teams ask?

What is liquefaction, and why is it a concern near Utah Lake?
Liquefaction happens when loose, saturated soils are shaken hard enough that water pressure between the grains spikes and the soil temporarily loses its strength - buildings can settle, tilt, or be pushed sideways. The shoreline around Utah Lake is young lakebed: loose sands and silts with a high water table, which is the textbook recipe. That is why sites near the lake get liquefaction-specific geotechnical study and foundation design rather than conventional shallow footings.
What does a liquefaction geotechnical investigation include?
Borings through the liquefiable deposits, in-situ density testing such as standard or cone penetration tests, groundwater measurements, and laboratory classification of the soils. The engineer runs a triggering analysis comparing the soil's resistance to the design earthquake's demand, and reports expected settlement and lateral spreading. Those quantities become the structural engineer's design inputs for foundations and detailing.
Is ground improvement or deep foundations better for my site?
It depends on the soil profile, the depth of the liquefiable layer, and the building. Ground improvement such as stone columns or compaction grouting often costs less for light buildings over moderate depths. Deep foundations suit heavier structures, very thick weak deposits, or sites with lateral spreading risk. The geotechnical engineer recommends the approach in the report; the structural engineer prices and details the options so the owner can compare real numbers.
Do Provo and Utah County handle liquefaction reviews differently?
The technical standard is the same, but the submittal path is not. Within Provo, the geotechnical report goes through the city's development services review with its own checklist and portal. In unincorporated Utah County, the county is the authority having jurisdiction with different forms, timelines, and peer-review triggers. The engineer confirms which AHJ governs the parcel before the investigation begins so the report meets that reviewer's expectations. The AHJ decides approvals.
