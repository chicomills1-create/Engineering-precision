How Is Indoor Pool Dehumidification Properly Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Indoor pool dehumidification is designed to remove moisture at the pool's evaporation rate, holding air slightly warmer than the water at 50 to 60 percent humidity, with air distribution that keeps every cold surface above the dew point and corrosion-resistant construction throughout.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Sizing the latent load

Everything in a natatorium design flows from the evaporation rate. I calculate it from pool surface area, the temperature difference between water and air, air velocity over the water, and an activity factor — a calm residential-style pool, a busy recreation pool with a slide, and a therapy pool with high water temperature and agitation are three very different loads that happen to share a building type. Hot tubs, spray features, and spectator areas each get their own line in the load calculation. The dehumidification unit is selected for the worst-case hour, not the average day, and I include the outdoor air latent load on top of the pool evaporation — summer ventilation air in a humid climate is a major load of its own. Heat recovery is standard in my designs: the refrigeration circuit that wrings moisture from the air rejects heat that goes back into the pool water or the space, turning the dehumidification penalty into pool heating. I show the owner this energy balance explicitly, because it's the argument that justifies proper equipment over a cheap exhaust fan and a prayer.

Air distribution that stops condensation

The supply air has one critical job beyond comfort: keep every interior surface above the space dew point. I lay out supply to wash exterior glazing, curtain walls, and cold wall surfaces with warm, dry air, using long-throw diffusers or duct socks that reach the glass without blasting bathers. Returns go high, pulling the warmest, most moisture-laden air back to the dehumidifier, and the whole space gets enough air movement that no pocket sits stagnant against a cold surface. Stratification is the enemy — a tall natatorium with a cold roof deck will condense at the ceiling while the deck level reads fine on the thermostat. I design for full mixing or targeted destratification, and I verify with the envelope consultant that the coldest interior surface temperature in the design condition still sits above the design dew point. That single check, done during design, prevents the most expensive natatorium failures I know of.

Corrosion and envelope protection checklist

A natatorium is a corrosive, wet industrial environment wearing a recreation building's clothes, and the design has to respect that. The envelope and the mechanical systems are one coordinated defense — the best dehumidifier in the world cannot save a leaky, uninsulated envelope, and the best envelope cannot survive without dehumidification. I lock these decisions in during schematic design with the architect, because nearly all of them are structural or envelope commitments. • Hold space air ~2°F above water temperature at 50 to 60 percent relative humidity • Wash all glazing and cold surfaces with warm, dry supply air; verify coldest surface stays above dew point • Run the pool enclosure slightly negative to adjacent spaces to contain humid, chlorinated air • Specify coated coils, corrosion-resistant drain pans, and non-corroding ductwork materials • Place the vapor retarder on the warm side with airtight construction and thermal breaks at structure • Recover dehumidification heat to the pool water or space air to offset operating cost

What else do project teams ask?

What temperature and humidity should an indoor pool space hold?
The widely used design practice holds the space air about 2°F warmer than the pool water — typically low 80s air over low 80s water for recreation pools — with relative humidity between 50 and 60 percent. Warmer air relative to the water suppresses evaporation, which is the whole game: less evaporation means a smaller dehumidification load, less condensation risk, and lower heating bills. Competition pools and therapy pools adjust from this baseline for their water temperatures and bather loads.
Why do indoor pools destroy buildings?
Condensation. Warm, moisture-laden pool air that reaches a surface below its dew point — a window frame, a steel beam inside a wall, the underside of a roof deck — deposits water that rots wood, corrodes steel, and grows mold where nobody can see it until the damage is structural. The defense is a designed system, not a bigger exhaust fan: correct dehumidification capacity, supply air washing every cold surface, an airtight envelope with the vapor retarder on the warm side, and thermal breaks. Pools that fail almost always failed at the envelope details, not the equipment.
Can outdoor air ventilation replace a dehumidifier in a natatorium?
Only in consistently dry climates, and even there it's a compromise. Ventilation dilutes humidity but can't control it — on a humid summer day you're bringing moisture in, not removing it, and in winter you're throwing away enormous heating energy. A dedicated dehumidification unit with heat recovery captures the latent energy from the moisture it removes and returns it to the pool water or space air, which is why properly designed systems often heat the pool nearly for free. I design dehumidification as the primary system and ventilation as the air-quality complement.
How do you protect HVAC equipment from pool air corrosion?
By assuming the air is corrosive and specifying accordingly: coated condenser and evaporator coils, stainless steel or polymer drain pans, corrosion-resistant fasteners and cabinets, and ductwork materials that won't corrode in a chloramine environment. I also keep electrical and control components out of the direct airstream where possible and slope all drain pans and ductwork to drain — standing water plus pool chemicals equals rapid failure. Maintenance access matters too, because coated equipment still needs its coils cleaned and its drains verified in an environment that punishes neglect.
