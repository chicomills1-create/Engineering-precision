How Is Wind Engineering Done for a Coastal Hotel?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Wind engineering for a coastal hotel designs the tower's lateral system for ASCE 7 wind overturning and drift, engineers the facade and glazing for severe localized pressures and debris impact, anchors every rooftop unit and amenity against uplift, and details egress and refuge areas so guests stay safe through the design storm.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Tower Structure, Drift, and Foundations

The lateral design begins with the wind tunnel of the code: the engineer establishes the basic wind speed, exposure category, and topographic effects for the beachfront site, then runs the directional or envelope procedure to get story forces. On hotels the serviceability checks often govern member sizes — the engineer limits drift and acceleration so the building does not alarm guests or crack the facade during frequent wind events, not just the design storm. Torsional response gets checked where the tower is asymmetric or the podium below is stiffer than the tower above, since the stiffness transition at the podium level concentrates demand. Foundations translate the tower's overturning into the ground. The engineer designs for the combined axial, shear, and moment at the base, checking that the foundation — piles, drilled shafts, or mat — resists net uplift at the windward edge under the factored wind load. On constrained beachfront lots the overturning lever arm is short and the uplift is large, which is why deep foundations are common even where soils could otherwise support a shallow system. The engineer also coordinates with the geotechnical report on liquefaction and scour where the water table is high, since a hurricane brings the water table to the surface.

Facade, Glazing, and Rooftop Anchorage

Curtain wall and window wall systems are engineered as structural assemblies: mullion spans, anchor clips, and pressure plates are sized for the component-and-cladding pressures at each elevation and zone, with the corner zones getting the heaviest design. The engineer requires product approvals or test reports matching the specified pressures and the missile-impact rating, and reviews the approved shop drawings against the structural drawings so the anchors land on structure, not on sheathing. Sealant joints are detailed for the movement the drift analysis predicts, because a facade that cannot move with the frame leaks and then fails. Every rooftop item gets an anchorage detail. The engineer provides the design wind pressures for rooftop equipment at its height and exposure, and the mechanical drawings carry the curb, rail, and anchor details derived from them — packaged units bolted through curbs into structure, exhaust fans on anchored curbs, and screens and parapets designed as wind elements in their own right. Signage, lighting poles, and flagpoles are designed for wind with their foundations. The rooftop amenity program is reviewed item by item: fixed elements are anchored, loose elements are specified with a storm-stowage plan, and the operations manual the engineer helps draft tells staff exactly what gets secured and when.

Coastal Hotel Wind Engineering Checklist

Use this checklist before the structural and envelope sets are issued: • Lateral system sized for ASCE 7 wind overturning, shear, and guest-comfort drift limits • Foundation designed for net uplift and overturning on the constrained beachfront footprint • Facade components engineered for corner-zone and upper-floor cladding pressures • Impact-rated glazing or shutters specified for the wind-borne debris region with matching product approvals • Every rooftop unit, screen, sign, and amenity element anchored for wind uplift with details on the drawings • Balcony railings, dividers, and doors checked for pressure, impact, and wind-driven rain • Egress stairs enclosed and pressurized; refuge areas designated away from glazing • Product approval regime confirmed: Florida Product Approval or local evaluation per the jurisdiction

What else do project teams ask?

Why do upper hotel floors need stronger windows than lower floors?
Wind pressure increases with height, and the localized pressures at building corners and roof edges — where airflow separates and accelerates — run much higher than the average pressure on the building. A window on the 15th floor corner can see double the pressure of the same window at the third floor mid-facade. The engineer calculates component-and-cladding pressures for each zone and elevation, and the glazing specification steps up accordingly rather than using one window type for the whole tower.
What happens to a hotel's rooftop bar in a hurricane?
It is designed to be secured, not to ride out the storm in service. Fixed elements like pergolas and railings are anchored for the design wind pressures, while furniture, planters, umbrellas, and loose equipment follow a storm-stowage plan that moves them inside or to a secured area. The engineer reviews the amenity program during design and helps write the operations procedure, because an unsecured rooftop is the most likely source of wind-borne debris on the property — dangerous to the building itself and to its neighbors.
How do guests shelter safely in a coastal hotel during a hurricane?
The design designates refuge areas in the building core — interior corridors, ballrooms, and meeting rooms away from exterior glazing — on floors below the worst wind effects, and the engineer verifies these spaces have emergency power, ventilation, and accessible egress. Egress stairs are enclosed and pressurized so they stay usable. Many coastal hotels also plan for phased evacuation: the engineering supports the decision by showing which parts of the building are designed refuge areas and which are not.
Do Florida hotels face different product rules than Texas hotels?
Yes. Florida enforces a statewide product approval system — components like windows, doors, and roof coverings generally need Florida Product Approval or a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance in the high-velocity hurricane zone — while Texas coastal jurisdictions typically accept tested products through the local building department's review, with wind-borne debris requirements along the coast. The engineer confirms the applicable approval regime with the authority having jurisdiction before specifying any envelope component.
