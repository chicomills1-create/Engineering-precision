How Are Feedlot Cattle Loadouts Designed for Safe Trucking?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Loadouts are engineered for calm, fast loading: a gently sloped non-slip ramp fed by a curved single-file alley, bright shadow-free lighting into the trailer, a truck court sized for semi maneuvering, and staging pens plus scale integration that keep trucks moving.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Ramp Slope, Alley Geometry, and Trailer Fit

The engineer sets the ramp slope from animal safety: gentle enough that cattle walk up without rushing or slipping, typically well under 20 degrees, with the exact number matched to the rise the site requires. Cleats, grooves, or rubber surfacing give footing in all weather, and solid sides keep cattle focused forward. The approach alley's curve is drawn with a radius cattle accept — not a hairpin — and the engineer keeps the width consistent from staging pen to ramp so there is no pinch point where the flow stops. Trailer fit is confirmed with the haulers, not assumed. The engineer checks deck heights for the pot-belly and straight-deck trailers the yard ships on, designs the dock or adjustable ramp to meet them with minimal step, and confirms the dock width and gate arrangement against the trailer's compartments. Overhead clearance, dock bumpers, and the gate the crew works from the catwalk are all detailed on the plans, because the loadout gets used in the dark, in winter, and in a hurry.

Lighting, Truck Court, and Crew Safety

The lighting design treats the trailer mouth as the critical detail: the engineer brings the ramp and trailer interior up brighter than the approach alley, eliminates the black hole at the trailer door with aimed fixtures, and keeps everything out of the animals' reach and protected from kicks and equipment. The truck court and staging pens get area lighting on the same control as the loadout, so the whole operation can go to full brightness for a night load. The truck court is sized like the industrial apron it is. The engineer lays out the backing maneuver for the design vehicle — usually the longest semi combination serving the yard — with room to spare, surfaces it for all-weather truck traffic, and grades it to drain away from the dock. Crew safety is drawn in: catwalks along the ramp and dock with railings, gates operable from the safe side, no dead ends where a worker can be trapped by moving cattle, and the scale positioned so the driver weighs without unhooking or repositioning.

Feedlot Cattle Loadout Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the loadout is built: • Ramp slope gentle, well under 20 degrees, with non-slip cleated or grooved surfacing • Curved single-file approach alley with solid sides, consistent width to the ramp • Ramp height matched to the trailer types the yard ships on; minimal step up • Ramp and trailer interior lit brighter than the approach; no dark trailer mouth • All cattle surfaces drained and detailed for washdown between uses • Truck court sized for the longest semi combination to back in one smooth move • All-weather surfacing and drainage in the truck court • Staging pens sized for a full load, holding cattle calmly at the alley • Catwalks with railings, safe-side gates, and no crew dead ends • Scale, office, and paperwork flow coordinated so the truck moves once

What else do project teams ask?

How steep can a cattle loading ramp be?
Gentle — the engineer typically holds loading ramps well under 20 degrees. Steeper ramps make cattle rush, slip, and balk, which slows loading and causes the bruises and injuries that cost the operation directly. Non-slip surfacing and solid sides matter as much as the number.
Why do cattle balk at loading?
Usually because of what they see: a dark trailer mouth, a sharp shadow across the ramp, a steep or slippery slope, or a dead-end look to the alley. The engineer's loadout design removes each cause — bright even lighting into the trailer, gentle non-slip ramp, curved approach — so cattle keep walking.
How much room does a cattle truck need to maneuver?
The truck court is laid out for the longest semi-trailer combination serving the yard, with room to back squarely to the dock in one smooth movement. The engineer draws the turning paths for the design vehicle rather than guessing, and surfaces the whole court for all-weather use.
Should the truck scale be at the loadout?
Often yes — weighing on the way out folds the scale into the loading flow so the truck is weighed, loaded, and documented in one sequence. The engineer coordinates the scale position with the truck court geometry so the driver never has to unhook or reposition between weighing and loading.
