How Do Denver Foundations Handle Bentonite and Expansive Soil?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Denver's bentonitic clays swell powerfully as they absorb water, heaving foundations upward. Engineers respond with drilled piers bearing in bedrock or stable material below the moisture-active zone, with structural floors spanning over a void, or with post-tensioned slabs engineered for the site's swell potential, always paired with strict drainage and irrigation control.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Drilled Piers vs. Post-Tensioned Slabs on Expansive Ground

Drilled piers are the conservative answer for the worst clays. Shafts are drilled through the active zone and into bedrock or stable material, reinforced with steel, and filled with concrete; the building's structural floor then spans between pier-supported grade beams over a collapsible void form, so heaving soil pushes against cardboard, not concrete. The engineer sizes each pier for the building's loads plus the uplift drag the swelling clay exerts on the shaft, and specifies the void depth from the geotechnical report's predicted heave. It costs more than a slab, but on high-swell sites it is the system with the fewest callbacks. Post-tensioned slabs take the opposite approach: a stiffened, heavily reinforced slab designed to ride the ground's movement as a rigid raft. The geotechnical report supplies the design swell values, and the engineer follows post-tensioning design methods for expansive soils, laying out ribs, tendons, and edge moisture barriers so the slab stays planar even as the clay breathes beneath it. These slabs demand discipline in the field: tendon stressing records, proper subgrade preparation, and edge details that keep surface water from ponding against the slab. On moderate-swell sites with good drainage, a well-built PT slab performs reliably; on the metro's worst bentonite, most engineers reach for piers.

Water Management: The Other Half of the Design

A foundation designed for expansive soil can still fail if the site keeps feeding the clay water. Grading must fall away from the building in every direction so roof runoff and stormwater leave the site instead of ponding against the foundation; gutters and downspouts discharge well clear of the structure, not into planting beds hugging the walls. Irrigation is the quiet culprit in Denver heave cases: sprinkler heads aimed at the foundation, overwatered turf, and planting beds that trap moisture against the slab create the exact wetting the geotechnical report warned about. Landscape plans on expansive sites keep high-water plantings away from the building, use drip zones with setbacks from the foundation, and avoid grade changes that dam water against walls. The goal is consistent, moderate moisture, not desert-dry soil. Letting the clay desiccate under a long drought can cause settlement as the soil shrinks, the mirror image of heave, which is why the guidance is steady drainage rather than aggressive drying. Utility trenches get compacted backfill and leak-free connections, because a slow water-line leak under a slab is one of the most destructive and least visible wetting sources. Homeowners inherit this system at closing: keeping gutters clear, fixing leaks promptly, and not regrading beds against the house are permanent maintenance duties, not one-time construction items.

What else do project teams ask?

Why is Denver's soil so expansive?
The metro sits on weathered Pierre Shale and Denver Formation clays, marine deposits rich in bentonite minerals that absorb water between their crystal layers and swell. Some Denver-area clays rank among the most expansive soils in the United States. That is why standard footings that work fine in other cities fail here.
How deep do drilled piers go in the Denver metro?
Depth follows the geology, not a standard number. The geotechnical engineer sets pier depth from borings: piers must extend below the active zone of seasonal moisture change and into bedrock or stable material that will not swell. On a single site, depths vary with the clay profile, and the report also sets the void depth under structural floors based on predicted heave.
Can I build a conventional slab-on-grade on Denver clay?
Only if it is engineered for the clay. A standard thin slab with light reinforcement will crack and heave as the soil swells. The engineered alternatives are a post-tensioned stiffened slab designed for the site's swell values, or overexcavation and replacement of the clay with engineered fill, sometimes combined with moisture barriers. The geotechnical report determines which approach fits the site.
Does landscaping really affect my foundation?
Yes, more than most owners expect. Irrigation aimed at the foundation, planting beds that hold water against the slab, and negative grading are the most common causes of heave in otherwise well-designed homes. Keep high-water plantings away from the building, maintain positive drainage away from the walls, and fix plumbing leaks promptly.
What does a geotechnical report test for on an expansive site?
Borings map the soil profile and depth to stable material, while laboratory tests measure Atterberg limits and swell potential through swell-consolidation testing. The report defines the active zone depth, predicts potential heave, and recommends a foundation type with design values the structural engineer builds from. Denver plan reviewers expect the foundation drawings to match these recommendations.
