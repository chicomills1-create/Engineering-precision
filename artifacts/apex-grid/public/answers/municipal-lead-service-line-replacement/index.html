How Does Municipal Lead Service Line Replacement Work?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

Under the Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, every community water system must inventory its service lines by material, notify customers with lead lines, and replace lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement lines — generally within 10 years. The IIJA dedicated $15 billion through the Drinking Water SRF for this work, much of it as principal forgiveness. Delivery is a program, not a project: inventory verification, prioritized replacement design, resident coordination, and construction across thousands of properties.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The regulatory mandate and the inventory

EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require every community and non-transient non-community water system to have completed a service-line material inventory — every service line classified as lead, galvanized requiring replacement, non-lead, or unknown — and set replacement deadlines (generally 10 years, with deferred deadlines only for systems demonstrating infeasibility). Unknowns must be investigated, not assumed safe. Systems must notify customers served by lead lines annually, with sampling and filter provisions.

Building the inventory is detective work: historical records (tap cards, plumbing codes by era, main installation records), visual inspection at the meter or curb box, excavation potholing where records fail, and predictive modeling where statistical methods are approved by the state. Our engineers build inventories from records research plus field verification programs — because the inventory's accuracy determines the replacement program's scope, cost, and funding, and a bad inventory means either replacing lines that don't need it or missing lines that do.

Replacement: methods and program design

Full service-line replacement — public side plus private side, curb stop to building inlet — is the standard; partial replacements (public side only) are discouraged because disturbing the line can spike lead levels at the tap. Methods: open-cut replacement (traditional, disruptive, necessary where conditions demand), trenchless pull-through/pipe-bursting (new line pulled through the old path — less restoration, often cheaper all-in), and directional drilling for longer or obstructed runs. Method selection per property is a design decision balancing cost, disruption, and site constraints.

Program design is where engineering meets logistics: prioritizing replacements (daycares, schools, high-risk populations first in most state frameworks), packaging properties into biddable contracts (by neighborhood — mobilization efficiency matters enormously at this scale), resident coordination (access agreements for private-side work, temporary water provisions, communication in the languages the neighborhood speaks), and post-replacement sampling and flushing protocols. A 5,000-line replacement program is 5,000 small construction projects sharing one management system — the program management is as important as the pipe design.

  • Full replacement (public + private side) as the standard
  • Trenchless methods to cut cost and disruption
  • Prioritization: schools, daycares, vulnerable populations
  • Neighborhood packaging for bid efficiency
  • Resident coordination and access agreements at scale

Funding: the $15 billion opportunity

The IIJA's $15 billion lead-service-line commitment flows through the Drinking Water SRF, with states directed to provide much of it as principal forgiveness or grants — particularly to disadvantaged communities. For many systems, the replacement program's capital cost is substantially or fully forgiven: the federal government paying to remove the lead. This is the most favorable infrastructure financing most municipalities will ever see, and it's time-limited to the BIL authorization horizon.

Capturing it runs through the standard SRF path: IUP listing, engineering report, application — with the inventory as the scope basis and disadvantaged-community documentation unlocking forgiveness. The systems maximizing capture share traits: complete verified inventories, engineered replacement programs with real costs, and applications filed in the current cycles. Systems still debating whether to start are ceding the money to systems already building. The funding rewards readiness; readiness is the inventory plus the engineering.

Delivery models and what to watch

Delivery at scale demands a program manager — often the design engineer in a program-management role, or a dedicated PM supporting the utility. Design-bid-build works for the replacement contracts themselves (unit-price bidding by replacement type is the norm); construction-manager or program-manager structures handle the multi-contract, multi-year program. Keys to success: standardized details and specs across contracts (every contractor builds the same replacement), rigorous inspection (private-side work quality varies — inspection protects the utility's investment and the resident), and a data system tracking every address from inventory through verification sampling.

Pitfalls we've seen: starting construction before the inventory is verified (scope churn kills unit-price contracts); under-resourcing resident coordination (access refusals stall whole neighborhoods); neglecting restoration quality (a replacement program that leaves 5,000 torn-up yards is a political crisis); and treating sampling as paperwork (post-replacement verification protects public health and the utility's liability). Our engineers deliver LSLR programs end-to-end: inventory, program design, contract packaging, resident coordination support, construction administration, and the SRF funding applications that pay for it — because a program this large, this public, and this funded deserves engineering that treats it as the major capital program it is.

What else do project teams ask?

What is a lead service line inventory?
A complete record of every water service line's material — lead, galvanized requiring replacement, non-lead, or unknown — required of every community water system under the Lead and Copper Rule Improvements. It's built from historical records, field verification, and approved predictive methods, and it's the basis for the replacement program.
Who pays for lead service line replacement?
Largely the IIJA/BIL: $15 billion dedicated through the Drinking Water SRF, much of it as principal forgiveness or grants — especially for disadvantaged communities. Many systems' programs are substantially or fully funded. Private-side costs, traditionally the homeowner's, are eligible under these programs.
How long do utilities have to replace lead lines?
Generally 10 years under the Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, with the clock running from the rule's compliance dates. Deferred deadlines exist only where systems demonstrate genuine infeasibility — the expectation is full replacement on schedule.
Is partial lead service line replacement allowed?
Strongly discouraged: replacing only the public side disturbs the remaining lead pipe and can temporarily increase lead levels at the tap. The standard — and the funded approach — is full replacement from main to building inlet, both sides.
