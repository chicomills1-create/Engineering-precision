Does a Grease Interceptor Need PE Certification?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Grease interceptors need engineered sizing from the fixture load and usually PE certification for the plumbing permit — the engineer sizes the device per the code and the sewer utility's FOG program, designs its location and venting for maintainability, and documents the sizing basis on the sealed drawings.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Sizing from the fixture load

The sizing calculation is the engineering core. I build the fixture schedule for everything discharging grease waste: compartment sinks, prep and scullery sinks, dishwashers, cooking equipment with drains, and floor drains and floor sinks in food preparation areas. Each fixture contributes its code flow value, and the total flow determines the interceptor capacity needed for adequate retention — the dwell time that lets grease cool, separate, and float while clearer water exits. Where the utility publishes its own sizing method, I run that method and design to whichever governs. The connected-load honesty matters because kitchens change. I size for the permitted kitchen's equipment schedule, and I note where future equipment additions would exceed the interceptor — because the most common grease system failure is a kitchen that grew past its interceptor. The sealed drawings carry the sizing calculation visibly: fixture schedule, flow totals, the selected interceptor's rated capacity, and the code and utility references the sizing follows. That transparency is what lets the plumbing reviewer, the health reviewer, and the utility each confirm compliance from the same set.

Location, venting, and utility coordination

The interceptor's physical design determines whether it works for its service life. I locate gravity interceptors for pump-truck access with manhole access to every compartment, set the inlet and outlet inverts for gravity flow at proper slopes, and design the venting — interceptors need venting per the code, and poor venting causes the flow problems that push grease past the device. Flow control fittings on hydromechanical units get specified and shown. The piping materials suit grease waste service, and cleanouts are placed where the maintenance crew can actually reach them. Utility coordination is a parallel permit track. I confirm the sewer utility's FOG program requirements — sizing method, approved devices, installation standards, and the maintenance agreement — and design to them from the start. The health department's review of the kitchen plumbing runs on the same drawings, so the grease waste design anticipates the food code's fixture and indirect-waste expectations too. The PE's sealed plumbing set is the single document all three reviewers — plumbing, health, utility — read, and it's designed to satisfy all three.

Grease interceptor PE checklist

A grease interceptor submittal is ready for PE certification when the sizing, the physical design, and the utility coordination are all complete. The utility's FOG program is the requirement most often discovered late. • Fixture schedule complete: every grease-waste fixture tallied with code flow values • Interceptor sized from the flow calculation per the plumbing code and the utility's FOG sizing method — strictest governs • Interceptor type selected for the load and the utility's rules: gravity vs hydromechanical • Location designed for maintenance: pump-truck access, manhole access, proper inlet/outlet slopes and venting • Sewer utility FOG program requirements confirmed and met: approved devices, installation standards, maintenance agreement • Sizing basis documented on the sealed drawings: fixture schedule, calculation, and code references visible to every reviewer

What else do project teams ask?

How is a grease interceptor sized?
From the connected fixture load converted to a flow rate, matched against the interceptor capacity the plumbing code and the local utility require. I tally every fixture discharging to the grease waste system — three-compartment sinks, prep sinks, dishwashers, wok ranges, floor drains in grease-producing areas — apply the code's flow values, and size the interceptor so the wastewater's retention time lets grease separate before discharge. Utilities with FOG programs often publish their own sizing method or minimum sizes that govern over the code's. The sealed drawings show the fixture schedule, the sizing calculation, and the selected interceptor — the sizing basis documented, not just the box drawn.
What's the difference between gravity and hydromechanical interceptors?
Gravity interceptors are large underground tanks — typically 500 gallons and up — that separate grease by retention time; they're the standard for full commercial kitchens and what most utility FOG programs expect. Hydromechanical interceptors are smaller indoor units using flow control and separation design, suited to limited grease loads where an outdoor gravity unit isn't feasible. The choice follows the load and the utility's rules: many utilities mandate gravity interceptors above certain fixture counts regardless of what would fit indoors. I specify the type the load and the local program require, and the design shows the venting, accessibility, and piping each type needs to function.
Why does interceptor location and accessibility matter so much?
Because an interceptor that can't be pumped doesn't work. Gravity interceptors need vehicle access for the pump truck, manhole access to each compartment, and location outside the building footprint where possible — under-slab interceptors inside the building are a maintenance nightmare and many AHJs restrict them. The inlet piping needs proper slope and the outlet needs its venting and flow control. I design the location with the maintenance operation in mind: the pump truck's path, the lid access, and the piping grades. Owners discover the accessibility problem at the first pumping, when the truck can't reach the manhole — the PE's design prevents that discovery.
What do sewer utilities require beyond the plumbing code?
Often quite a bit. Utilities with FOG pretreatment programs set their own interceptor sizing tables, approved manufacturer lists, minimum sizes by establishment type, installation standards, and ongoing maintenance and reporting obligations the owner accepts with the sewer connection. The utility's requirements can exceed the plumbing code's — and where they do, the utility governs, because they control the sewer connection. I check the local utility's FOG program requirements at the start of the design, not at permit intake, and the sealed drawings note compliance with the program's standards. A plumbing permit approved without the utility's signoff is a stalled project.
