How Do GCs Coordinate PE Seals From Subs on Design-Bid-Build Jobs?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

On design-bid-build, the GC must track every delegated-design PE seal from specialty subs — writing sealed submittals into the subcontracts, verifying each sub's engineer is licensed in the project state, and sequencing delegated packages against the AHJ's deferred-submittal policy.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Building the delegated-seal register

I run this as a simple register — a living document, not a one-time list. Every delegated component gets a row: the component, the subcontractor, the sub's engineer (name, license number, state), the required deliverable (sealed shops plus calculations), the due date tied to the permit schedule, and the status. The GC's project engineer owns it and reviews it at the weekly OAC or subcontractor meeting like any other procurement log. Components get added as design clarifies — the register started at bid time will not match the register at 100% CDs, and that is fine as long as additions get engineers assigned immediately. The register also tracks the AHJ's deferred-submittal decisions: which components the building department agreed to accept after permit issuance, documented in writing or in the permit conditions. I keep the AHJ's written policy or the plan reviewer's email with the register, because personnel change and memories differ. When the permit is issued with deferred items outstanding, the register becomes the closeout tracker — every deferred seal has to land before final inspection, and the GC who tracked them from day one closes out clean.

Where the coordination usually breaks down

It breaks down in three predictable places. First, at bid time, when delegated engineering is vaguely scoped and the winning sub priced none of it — the fix is explicit subcontract language naming the sealed deliverable and the schedule. Second, at the design handoff, when the sub's engineer gets backgrounds late, in the wrong format, or with unresolved architectural decisions baked in — the fix is the GC managing the background release like a procurement delivery, with dates. Third, at the AHJ counter, when the plan reviewer rejects a delegated submittal's format — wrong seal block, missing calculations, digital seal the jurisdiction does not accept — the fix is confirming submittal standards with the AHJ before the sub's engineer starts, not after they finish. Underneath all three is the same root cause: the GC treated delegated engineering as the sub's internal affair instead of as permit-critical path. The subs do the engineering; the GC owns the schedule consequence. I tell GCs to manage delegated seals with the same intensity they manage steel delivery — because a missing truss seal holds up a permit just as surely as missing steel holds up erection.

Delegated-seal coordination checklist

Assign every delegated component an engineer and a date before the permit schedule is promised. • Build the delegated-seal register at bid time: component, sub, engineer name, license number and state, deliverable, due date • Write sealed shops + calculations, licensed in the project state, into every delegated subcontract with a dated milestone • Collect each sub's engineer credentials within days of award — verify licenses on the state board site • Confirm the AHJ's deferred-submittal policy in writing: which components, what the deferral request must show • Match every delegated submittal to the AHJ's format standards (seal block, digital seal acceptance) before the sub's engineer starts

What else do project teams ask?

Which subcontractor scopes typically need their own PE seal?
The usual list: structural steel connections (when delegated), cold-formed steel framing, wood or steel trusses, curtain wall and storefront systems, precast concrete, fire sprinkler and fire alarm design, specialty foundations like helical piles, retaining walls over the AHJ's height threshold, and commercial kitchen exhaust. The exact list depends on the project and the jurisdiction — California reviewers, for example, scrutinize curtain wall and seismic bracing of nonstructural components closely, while Florida reviewers focus on wind-borne-debris and opening protection. I build the delegated-seal list off the actual drawings and the AHJ's checklist during preconstruction, so nothing is discovered at intake.
What should the subcontract language say about PE seals?
It should name the deliverable precisely: sealed shop drawings and supporting calculations, prepared under the responsible charge of a PE licensed in the project state, submitted by a specific date, in the AHJ's required format (including digital seal acceptance where applicable). It should also require the sub to identify their engineer — name and license number — within days of award, not at submittal time. I also recommend a holdback or milestone tied to accepted sealed submittals, because a sub with no contractual consequence for late engineering will always prioritize the next job's bid over your permit. Clear language here is the cheapest schedule insurance a GC can buy.
Can delegated submittals be deferred, or does the AHJ need them upfront?
Most AHJs allow deferred submittals for delegated components, but the policy — which items qualify, what the deferral request must include, and whether the EOR must list them on the permit drawings — varies by jurisdiction. The critical point is that deferred does not mean unsealed: the component still gets a full PE review and seal from the sub's engineer, just on a later schedule, and the EOR typically notes the deferred items on the sealed permit set. I confirm the deferred-submittal list with the AHJ during preconstruction and build the submittal calendar around it. Assuming deferral without confirming it is how permits stall.
What if a sub's engineer is licensed in the wrong state?
Then the seal is worthless for your permit and you find out at the worst possible time. Every delegated seal must come from a PE licensed in the project state — the truss engineer licensed only in Georgia cannot seal the truss package for your Phoenix job, and the sprinkler designer licensed only in Nevada cannot seal for Miami-Dade. This is why I have GCs collect engineer credentials at award: license numbers are verifiable on every state board's website in minutes. Catching it at award means the sub finds a locally licensed engineer with time to do the work; catching it at submittal means starting the component design over.
