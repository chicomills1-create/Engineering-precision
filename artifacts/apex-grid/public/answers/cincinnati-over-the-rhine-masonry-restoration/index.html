How Is Over-the-Rhine Masonry Restored in Cincinnati?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Over-the-Rhine masonry is restored by assessing brick and mortar condition, repointing deteriorated joints with compatible lime-based mortar, repairing or replacing lintels and sills, adding mechanical wall ties where wythes have separated, and routing work through Cincinnati's Historic Conservation Board review before permits issue.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Mortar Matching and Repointing Method

Matching the original mortar is a materials science task. The engineer or preservation consultant analyzes the existing mortar--its binder (lime, natural cement, or early Portland blends), sand gradation, and any historic colorants--then specifies a replacement mix with equal or lower compressive strength and higher vapor permeability than the surrounding brick. Modern Type N or Type S mortar is almost never appropriate for 1800s Over-the-Rhine brick; a lime-rich mix is the default. Mock-up panels are built on an inconspicuous wall area so the color and tooling can be approved before the full facade is touched. Execution details decide whether the repair lasts. Joints are raked to a minimum depth of about twice the joint width, dust is flushed out, and mortar is packed in lifts rather than smeared across the face. Tooling matches the historic joint profile--typically a concave or weathered joint on Italianate facades. The work is scheduled for moderate temperatures and kept damp during curing, because lime mortar that dries too fast never develops its strength. The engineer specifies these means and methods in the documents rather than leaving them to field improvisation.

Wall Ties, Lintels, and Parapet Stabilization

When wythes separate--the outer brick skin pulling away from the backup--helical stainless-steel ties are the standard repair. Installed from the interior through pilot holes, they stitch the wythes together without dismantling the facade, and the engineer lays out their spacing from the tributary wall area each tie must restrain. Where bulging is severe, the engineer may instead specify partial dismantling and rebuild of the affected area, salvaging sound brick and resetting it in matching mortar. Both approaches get verified against the wall's actual thickness and condition, never assumed from drawings that may not exist. Lintels and parapets get their own engineering. Original timber or early steel lintels over windows and storefronts are inspected for section loss; where they have failed, the engineer designs replacement steel lintels sized for the masonry above, with proper bearing and flashing so the repair does not trap water. Parapets are checked for mortar loss and outward lean--an unbraced parapet with degraded mortar is a fall hazard--and the engineer either repoints and braces it or specifies a controlled takedown and rebuild. Storefront-level work is coordinated with the tenant and the historic board so structural repairs do not erase character-defining details.

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't modern cement mortar be used on Over-the-Rhine brick?
1800s brick is relatively soft and porous, and historic lime mortar is softer and more permeable than the brick--so moisture migrates through the joints and evaporates. Hard Portland cement mortar reverses that relationship: moisture gets trapped in the brick, freeze-thaw cycles spall the faces off, and the wall deteriorates faster than if it had been left alone. Compatible lime-based mortar is the only appropriate repointing material.
What approvals does masonry restoration need in Over-the-Rhine?
Over-the-Rhine is a designated historic district, so exterior work goes through the Cincinnati Historic Conservation Board for a certificate of appropriateness before the building permit issues. The board reviews mortar color and tooling, replacement materials, and any changes to character-defining features. Structural repairs that are invisible from the exterior still need the standard building permit; the engineer confirms the full submittal path with the authority having jurisdiction before work starts.
How are separated brick wythes repaired without tearing down the wall?
Helical stainless-steel wall ties are installed from the interior through small pilot holes, mechanically stitching the outer wythe back to the backup masonry. The engineer spaces them based on the tributary wall area and the out-of-plane forces the wall must resist. Severely bulged or collapsed areas may still require partial dismantling and rebuild with salvaged brick.
Do restored parapets need bracing?
Often, yes. Parapets with degraded mortar or outward lean are evaluated for out-of-plane stability; the engineer either repoints and adds bracing back to the roof structure or specifies a controlled takedown and rebuild. On contributing historic buildings, the bracing is detailed to preserve the exterior appearance while the concealed steel does the structural work.
