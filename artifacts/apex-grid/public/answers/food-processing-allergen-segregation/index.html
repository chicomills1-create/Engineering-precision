Allergen Segregation Design for Food Plants

Allergen segregation is facility design for the one hazard that can kill a consumer with a trace: undeclared allergens are the leading cause of food recalls in America, and the plant's layout, ventilation, and traffic patterns are the physical controls behind the allergen control plan. Nine major allergens (milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame) must be controlled from receiving to shipping. Here's how engineers design the segregation in.

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran

Zoning: the allergen map as floor plan

Allergen zoning starts with the product and process map: which allergens enter the plant, where they're used, and which lines and areas must stay allergen-free. The design response is physical: segregated storage with barriers (not just labels — walls, cages, or validated distance), dedicated production areas or lines where the risk justifies them, and traffic patterns for people, materials, and waste that don't cross allergen boundaries. The floor plan becomes an allergen map, and your engineer should draw it with the QA team before walls go up.

Scheduling and changeover infrastructure support the zoning: where validated cleaning (rather than dedicated lines) is the strategy, the plant needs the CIP, washdown, and downtime the cleaning validation requires — designed in, not wished for. A changeover that needs four hours of validated cleaning in a plant scheduled for two is a plan that fails on its first allergen run.

Airborne cross-contact: the ventilation answer

Airborne cross-contact is the pathway most plants underestimate: flour dust, milk powder, spice particles — all carry allergens on air currents across rooms that look separated on the floor plan. The ventilation design answers it: air pressure cascades that move from allergen-free areas toward allergen areas (never the reverse), filtration on supply air to high-care zones, airlocks at zone boundaries, and dust collection at the sources (ingredient dumping, mixing, conveying). The air balance report documents the cascade; commissioning verifies it.

Dust control extends to the building details: no ledges that accumulate allergen dust above allergen-free lines, sealed penetrations between zones, and cleaning access designed into the overhead. An allergen-free packaging room under a dusty structural deck is a design failure — the section detail matters as much as the zone map.

Storage, handling, and the label room

Storage and handling complete the physical controls: segregated allergen ingredient storage with physical barriers and spill containment, color-coded tools and containers that can't migrate between zones, captive equipment (scoops, pallets, forklifts) where the risk assessment demands it, and rework handling that keeps allergen-containing rework out of allergen-free products. Waste streams get the same discipline — allergen waste routed and contained so it can't cross back.

Labeling and packaging areas deserve special design attention: the label room controls which labels reach which line, and the design should make the wrong label hard to grab — segregated label storage, line clearance procedures supported by the layout, and vision or verification systems where the risk justifies them. Undeclared allergen recalls destroy brands; the facility design is the physical layer of the defense. Engineer it with the seriousness the hazard demands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is allergen segregation required by regulation?

Yes — FDA's Food Labeling Modernization and FALCPA/FALCPA-amended rules (including sesame as the ninth major allergen) require allergen controls, and the facility design must support the allergen control plan: segregation, dedicated equipment or validated cleaning, and traffic patterns that prevent cross-contact.

Dedicated lines or validated cleaning?

Dedicated lines are the gold standard but not always practical; validated cleaning between allergen and non-allergen runs is the common approach. The facility design supports whichever strategy the plan uses — with changeover infrastructure (CIP, washdown, air handling) engineered for it.

Does ventilation design affect allergen control?

Yes — airborne flour, milk powder, and spice dusts carry allergens across rooms on air currents. The ventilation design keeps air moving from allergen-free areas toward allergen areas, with filtration and airlocks at the boundaries. This is engineered, not hoped for.

What physical controls support allergen plans?

Color-coded tools, captive equipment, segregated storage with physical barriers, and traffic patterns that don't cross allergen boundaries. The building enforces the discipline the plan requires — design the enforcement in.
