How Does State Revolving Fund (SRF) Financing Work?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

State Revolving Funds are state-run, federally capitalized loan programs for water infrastructure: the Clean Water SRF funds wastewater and stormwater projects; the Drinking Water SRF funds drinking water projects. EPA provides capitalization grants, states add matching funds, and the state agency lends to municipalities at below-market rates — with principal forgiveness (effectively grants) for disadvantaged communities. Projects compete for funding through an annual Intended Use Plan priority list. The IIJA/BIL massively expanded both programs.

The two funds and what they finance

The Clean Water SRF, created by the 1987 Clean Water Act amendments, finances publicly owned treatment works, sewer collection and conveyance, combined sewer overflow correction, stormwater management, nonpoint-source projects, and — in many states — decentralized wastewater and water-reuse projects. The Drinking Water SRF, created by the 1996 Safe Drinking Water Act amendments, finances treatment, transmission and distribution, storage, source development, consolidation, and lead service line replacement. Together they're the largest ongoing federal investment in water infrastructure — over $200 billion in assistance provided since inception.

The mechanics: EPA awards annual capitalization grants to each state; the state contributes matching funds (typically 20%); the state agency then lends to eligible borrowers — municipalities, counties, authorities, districts, and in many states other public entities — at interest rates well below municipal bond rates, with terms up to 30 years (and up to 40 for disadvantaged communities in some states). Loan repayments revolve back into the fund for future loans — hence 'revolving.' The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act injected tens of billions in additional capitalization, with mandates that significant portions go as principal forgiveness and grants to disadvantaged communities.

The Intended Use Plan: where projects compete

Every state publishes an annual Intended Use Plan (IUP) — the ranked list of projects the SRF will fund that year. Getting on the IUP is the game, and it's scored: project priority ranking systems award points for public-health benefit, compliance need, water-quality improvement, readiness to proceed, and disadvantaged-community status. A project with a complete engineering report, environmental review, and a compliance driver outscores a vague concept every time.

This is where engineering and funding strategy merge. The engineering report the SRF requires — alternatives analysis, cost-effectiveness, environmental review — is real engineering work that must be done before the application, not after. Municipalities that hire their engineer early, complete the report, and enter the IUP cycle with a ranked, ready project get funded; municipalities that start the engineering when they 'get around to it' watch the funding go to the prepared. We sequence our design deliverables to IUP calendars as a matter of routine — because the best project that misses the cycle waits a year.

Principal forgiveness: the closest thing to a grant

Not every SRF dollar is a loan. Federal law requires states to provide a portion of their capitalization as 'additional subsidization' — primarily principal forgiveness, where part (sometimes all) of the loan is forgiven, and in some cases negative-interest loans. The IIJA dramatically expanded this: for the BIL supplemental capitalization, roughly half must go as principal forgiveness or grants, targeted to disadvantaged communities.

Each state defines 'disadvantaged' through affordability criteria — typically combinations of median household income, poverty rates, unemployment, and water-bill burden relative to income. Small, low-income communities can see 50-100% principal forgiveness on drinking water projects and substantial forgiveness on clean water projects. The practical implication: a small town's $3 million treatment upgrade might cost the town $1 million or less after forgiveness. But the criteria, the documentation, and the timing are state-specific — which is why the engineer on the project needs to know the state's program, not just the federal outline.

How municipalities actually get from idea to loan

The honest sequence: (1) Identify the need — compliance order, capacity shortfall, failing infrastructure. (2) Hire the engineer — qualifications-based selection, and early enough that the engineering report lands before the IUP deadline. (3) Complete the engineering report: alternatives, costs, environmental review, rate analysis showing the community can repay. (4) Apply for IUP listing and priority ranking. (5) Once ranked and funded, complete design, bid the project (SRF programs have procurement and American Iron and Steel / Build America Buy America requirements to navigate), and close the loan. (6) Build, with SRF construction oversight requirements.

Two pitfalls to avoid: treating the engineering report as a formality (it's the document the entire funding decision rests on — a weak report means a low ranking or a rejected application), and ignoring the federal cross-cutters (Davis-Bacon wage requirements, AIS/BABA domestic-content rules, environmental reviews) until construction — they're loan conditions, and noncompliance can jeopardize disbursement. Our engineers prepare SRF-ready deliverables as the default, because in municipal water work, the funding application and the engineering are one project.

What else do project teams ask?

What's the difference between the Clean Water SRF and Drinking Water SRF?
The Clean Water SRF funds wastewater, sewer, stormwater, and related water-quality projects; the Drinking Water SRF funds drinking water treatment, distribution, storage, and source projects. They're separate programs, often run by different state agencies, with separate Intended Use Plans and application processes.
Can SRF loans be forgiven?
Partially, yes — through principal forgiveness, which functions like a grant. Federal law requires states to provide additional subsidization, targeted especially to disadvantaged communities; IIJA supplemental funds carry roughly 50% principal-forgiveness requirements. Each state's affordability criteria determine who qualifies.
What interest rates do SRF loans carry?
Well below market — many states lend at 0-3% depending on the program, term, and borrower, versus municipal bond rates. Terms run up to 30 years (longer for disadvantaged communities in some states). The exact rate is set by each state's program.
Do private utilities qualify for SRF funding?
For the Drinking Water SRF, yes in most states — privately owned community water systems are eligible. For the Clean Water SRF, eligibility is generally limited to public entities, though specifics vary by state. Your state's program administrator confirms eligibility.
