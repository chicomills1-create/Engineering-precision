How Does Combined Sewer Separation Work in Illinois Cities?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Combined sewer separation in Illinois cities means splitting one old pipe that carries both sewage and stormwater into two systems: a sanitary sewer for wastewater and a separate storm sewer for rainfall. Engineers phase the work street by street, manage bypass pumping and private property connections, and coordinate permits with the city and state EPA.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Investigation, Modeling, and Pipe Design

The engineer begins with field truth. Crews survey rim and invert elevations, CCTV-inspect the pipes for structural condition, and flow-monitor the system through wet weather to calibrate the model. The hydraulic model simulates the combined system under design storms, locating the regulators, overflow points, and bottlenecks. That model becomes the baseline against which the separated design is tested: the engineer must prove the new sanitary sewer handles peak wastewater flows without surcharging and the new storm sewer handles its design storm without flooding streets. Pipe design follows the verified concept. The engineer selects the conversion direction, usually keeping the old pipe sanitary and building new storm, because the old pipe's depth and alignment already serve the buildings. New storm sewers get inlets spaced for the street's spread criteria, manholes at required intervals, and outfalls with energy dissipation and erosion protection at the waterway. The engineer checks the downstream receiving system for the added stormwater, designs any needed detention or green infrastructure to offset peak flows, and details the lateral reconnections property by property. Structural condition of the retained pipe is assessed, with lining or replacement where CCTV shows failure.

Phasing, Bypass Pumping, and Private Property

Construction sequencing is designed like a second project. The engineer divides the sewershed into phases that keep every resident on working sewer service throughout, designs bypass pumping with redundant pumps and monitored alarms for each phase, and specifies traffic control that keeps streets functional. Work is sequenced with other utilities so water main, gas, and telecom conflicts are resolved before the sewer trench opens. The engineer also plans for the surprises old streets hold: unknown utilities, contaminated soil, and timber piles under the pavement all get contingency details. Private property disconnection is where separations succeed or stall. The engineer designs the inflow and infiltration program: dye testing and inspections that find downspouts, sump pumps, and foundation drains connected to the sanitary side, then the corrective work that reroutes them to storm or daylight. This requires public outreach the engineer supports with clear exhibits, because homeowners must grant access and sometimes pay for their side of the fix. The design includes post-construction flow monitoring to prove the separation actually removed the stormwater from the sanitary system, which is the metric regulators and funders judge the project by.

What else do project teams ask?

What is a combined sewer overflow?
A combined sewer overflow happens when a combined sewer, carrying both sewage and stormwater, fills beyond capacity in heavy rain. Regulators in the system discharge the excess, a mix of untreated sewage and stormwater, into a nearby waterway to keep it from backing up into streets and basements. Separation eliminates these overflows by giving stormwater its own pipe, which is why regulators require cities to address them.
Why not just build a bigger combined pipe?
A bigger combined pipe still overflows in big enough storms, still sends sewage to the treatment plant diluted by rainwater that costs money to treat, and still violates the regulatory direction, which is separation or equivalent overflow control. Separation fixes the cause: stormwater never enters the sanitary system, so the treatment plant sees only sewage and the waterway sees only stormwater. Storage tunnels are the alternative some cities use, but they store the problem rather than removing it.
How long does a separation project take?
Years, phased across the city. A single sewershed phase may take one to two construction seasons, and a citywide program runs a decade or more. The engineer prioritizes phases by overflow severity, coordinates with street resurfacing so pavement is opened once, and sequences funding across fiscal years. Residents experience it block by block: months of construction on their street, then a permanently separated system.
Who permits sewer separation in the Chicago area?
Several authorities. The municipality issues the street and sewer construction permits through its own review process and portal. The Illinois EPA permits the sewer construction and the stormwater discharge. In the Chicago area, the Metropolitan Water Reclamation District has jurisdiction over sewerage and stormwater matters with its own permit requirements and watershed rules. The engineer coordinates all three, because each reviews a different part of the project and none can be skipped.
