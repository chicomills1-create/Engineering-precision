How Are Municipal Sewer Collection Systems Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

Sanitary sewer collection systems are designed as gravity networks wherever topography allows: pipes sized by Manning's equation for peak flows with self-cleansing velocities, laid at slopes that maintain 2-10 fps, with manholes at every change in direction or slope. The design must prove capacity for current and future flows, control infiltration and inflow, and prevent sanitary sewer overflows — which are Clean Water Act violations. Where gravity won't work, lift stations and force mains take over.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Gravity hydraulics: the physics that governs everything

A sanitary sewer is a gravity machine, and its design is applied fluid mechanics: peak dry-weather flow plus infiltration and inflow, routed through pipes sized so velocity stays in the self-cleansing range (typically minimum 2 feet per second at design flow — slower and solids settle, faster than about 10 fps and the pipe scours itself). Manning's equation sizes every reach; the energy grade line is checked at every manhole; slopes are set to hold velocity without excessive excavation. Deep sewers cost exponentially more than shallow ones, so the vertical alignment — balancing minimum cover, minimum slope, and existing utilities — is where design skill shows.

Capacity assurance is the regulatory overlay: many states and consent decrees require municipalities to demonstrate adequate capacity before approving new connections — no more building subdivisions upstream of a surcharged interceptor. The design proves capacity for the 20-year planning flows under peak wet-weather conditions, with the I&I allowance reflecting the system's real condition, not a textbook value. A collection system designed for dry-weather flow plus a wish about I&I is a system that overflows in the first big storm.

Infiltration and inflow: the hidden capacity thief

I&I — groundwater infiltrating through cracked pipes and bad joints, stormwater inflowing through illegal connections, manhole covers, and cross-connected drains — can multiply wet-weather flows several times over dry-weather flow in aging systems. It's the dominant cause of sanitary sewer overflows nationwide, and controlling it is usually cheaper than building capacity for it. The engineering program: flow monitoring to quantify I&I by basin, smoke testing and CCTV inspection to find the sources, then rehabilitation — cured-in-place pipe lining, pipe bursting, manhole rehabilitation, service-lateral programs — targeted where the monitoring says the water enters.

This is asset management as engineering: a condition-assessment program with PACP-coded CCTV inspection, a prioritized rehabilitation plan, and a capital program that spends rehabilitation dollars where they remove the most I&I per dollar. Municipalities with disciplined I&I programs defer or avoid treatment-plant expansions worth tens of millions; municipalities without them build capacity for groundwater. The math is rarely close.

  • Gravity design: Manning's equation, self-cleansing velocity
  • Capacity assurance for 20-year peak wet-weather flows
  • I&I quantification by basin: flow monitoring, CCTV, smoke testing
  • Trenchless rehabilitation: CIPP, pipe bursting, manhole rehab
  • SSO prevention as a Clean Water Act compliance strategy

Lift stations, force mains, and low-pressure systems

Where gravity can't reach — flat terrain, crossings, deep valleys — the system goes mechanical. Lift stations (covered in our lift station design guide) pump through force mains to the next gravity reach or the plant. Force main design adds surge analysis: the water-hammer transient when pumps trip can split pipe and blow joints, so the hydraulic design includes transient modeling and surge protection where the profile demands it. Air-release and vacuum valves go at every high point — a force main with trapped air loses capacity and gains corrosion.

For scattered development and difficult terrain, low-pressure sewer systems (grinder pumps at each property discharging to a small-diameter pressure network) can be the economical answer — lower construction cost than deep gravity, at the price of distributed mechanical equipment the municipality (or the property owner, depending on the ordinance) must maintain. The choice between gravity, conventional lift stations, and low-pressure systems is a life-cycle cost analysis, not a preference — and the engineer should show the math.

SSO prevention and the regulatory reality

Sanitary sewer overflows are unpermitted discharges under the Clean Water Act — violations, whether they reach waters of the United States or not in many states' reading. EPA's enforcement posture, plus state programs and citizen suits, has pushed thousands of municipalities into consent decrees with billion-dollar-scale capital programs (the big-city decrees make headlines; the small-city orders are quieter but just as binding). The Capacity, Management, Operation, and Maintenance (CMOM) framework — asset inventory, condition assessment, preventive maintenance, capacity assurance, overflow emergency response — is the compliance standard even where not yet formally required.

The engineering response is the program, not just the projects: system-wide hydraulic modeling (SWMM, InfoWorks) that identifies the bottlenecks before the overflows do; the I&I rehabilitation program; the capital improvement plan sequenced to the consent schedule; and the funding strategy (Clean Water SRF is the workhorse) that pays for it. Municipalities under orders need engineers who've worked inside consent-decree programs and understand that every deliverable has a court deadline. That's our practice — the modeling, the rehab design, the program management, delivered at the pace court orders require.

What else do project teams ask?

What causes sanitary sewer overflows?
Overflows happen when flow exceeds system capacity — usually wet-weather I&I overwhelming pipes and pump stations — or when blockages (grease, roots, debris) or mechanical failures stop flow. Aging infrastructure with high I&I is the dominant systemic cause; fats, oils, and grease plus deferred maintenance are the dominant operational causes.
What is CMOM?
Capacity, Management, Operation, and Maintenance — EPA's framework for municipal collection system management: know your assets, assess their condition, maintain them preventively, assure capacity for growth, and respond to overflows with a real emergency plan. It's the compliance standard collection systems are judged against.
How is sewer pipe condition assessed?
Primarily CCTV inspection coded to the NASSCO PACP standard, which gives every defect a consistent grade — the basis for prioritizing rehabilitation. Supplemented by smoke testing (finds inflow sources), dye testing, flow monitoring (quantifies I&I by basin), and sonar/laser profiling for large interceptors.
When should a city rehabilitate vs. replace sewer pipe?
Rehabilitate (trenchless lining, bursting) when the pipe's alignment and grade are sound and the problem is structural condition or I&I — typically far cheaper and less disruptive than open-cut replacement. Replace when capacity is inadequate, the alignment is wrong, or the pipe has collapsed or lost grade. The CCTV condition grade and hydraulic model together make the call.
