How Are Laboratory Exhaust Systems Properly Designed?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Laboratory exhaust is designed as 100 percent exhaust with no recirculation, sized from fume hood capture requirements with fully tempered makeup air, in either manifolded systems with redundant fans and tall discharge stacks or dedicated per-hood systems.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Fume hood capture and containment

Containment is the entire purpose of a fume hood, and it depends on more than fan size. I select hoods by hazard — standard chemical hoods for general work, and specialty hoods where the chemistry demands it, such as washdown hoods for perchloric acid that prevent explosive perchlorate salts from accumulating in the duct. Each hood gets an airflow monitor with a local alarm so the researcher knows immediately if capture is lost. Placement is half the battle. Hoods go away from doors, busy aisles, and operable windows, because cross-drafts above a fraction of the face velocity will defeat even a well-sized hood. Supply diffusers are kept clear of the hood face, and I prefer ceiling diffusers with low throw near hoods over anything that jets air across the sash plane. During commissioning I witness containment testing with theatrical smoke or tracer gas at each hood in its as-installed condition — the factory test report is not proof of field performance.

Manifolded versus dedicated exhaust

A manifolded system collects hood and general exhaust into common risers served by a bank of parallel fans, with one fan typically held as standby. The advantages compound: a single tall stack with high discharge velocity disperses the plume above the roofline and away from intakes and operable windows, fan failure triggers automatic standby start instead of a lab shutdown, and maintenance happens at a few fan locations rather than dozens. The ductwork must be chemically compatible with everything the manifold carries and detailed so a failure in one branch cannot back-feed another. Dedicated exhaust — one fan, one hood — trades that resilience for simplicity and isolation. It suits single-hood renovations, specialty hoods whose effluent cannot share ductwork, and buildings where phasing makes a manifold impractical. The penalties are real: many roof penetrations, no inherent redundancy, and low individual discharge velocities that demand careful stack design to avoid re-entrainment. Whichever architecture I choose, the discharge gets the same scrutiny — stack height, exit velocity, and distance from every intake on the building and its neighbors, verified against wind and dispersion guidance.

Makeup air, controls, and energy

A lab's makeup air system is often its largest HVAC energy load, because every air change is 100 percent outdoor air that must be heated, cooled, and dehumidified. I design the makeup air unit for the lab's worst-case latent load, use energy recovery — typically a runaround loop or heat pipe where cross-contamination between exhaust and supply is a concern — and apply variable-air-volume hood controls so exhaust and makeup track actual sash positions instead of running at design flow around the clock. Controls sequencing is what holds the whole system together. Supply and exhaust track each other to maintain each lab's pressure relationship — usually negative to corridors so any leakage flows into the lab, not out. Night setback reduces air changes when labs are unoccupied, with occupancy sensors or schedules driving the sequence and a purge mode available before re-entry. I alarm the critical points: hood low-flow, room pressure loss, and fan failure all report to the building automation system, because in a lab, the ventilation system is life safety equipment and it gets treated that way. • Size exhaust from hood capture requirements and general room air changes, then match with fully conditioned makeup air • Favor manifolded exhaust with N+1 fans and a tall common stack for multi-hood buildings • Keep supply diffusers and cross-drafts away from hood faces to protect containment • Apply VAV sash controls and exhaust energy recovery to tame the single-pass energy penalty • Alarm hood airflow, room pressure, and fan status to the BAS as life-safety points

What else do project teams ask?

What face velocity should a fume hood be designed for?
Most general chemistry hoods are designed around 80 to 100 feet per minute at the working sash opening, but the right number depends on the hood's listing, the chemicals in use, and the institution's chemical hygiene plan. Higher is not automatically better — excessive face velocity creates turbulence at the sash that can pull contaminants out of the hood. I design to the hood manufacturer's tested performance data and verify with an as-installed face velocity traverse and a containment smoke test during commissioning.
Should lab exhaust be manifolded or dedicated per hood?
Manifolded systems win for most buildings with more than a few hoods. They allow fan redundancy so one fan failure does not kill a lab, they combine flows into a tall discharge stack that disperses contaminants safely above the roof, and they reduce the number of roof penetrations and maintenance points. Dedicated per-hood exhaust makes sense for isolated or highly specialized hoods — perchloric acid or radioisotope work, for example — where cross-contamination between hoods through a common duct is unacceptable. I decide based on the hazard inventory, not on first cost.
Why can't laboratory air be recirculated?
Because the air may carry chemical vapors, particulates, or biological agents that no practical filter arrangement can reliably remove across every possible contaminant. Laboratory design standards treat lab exhaust as single-pass: it leaves the building and is replaced with outdoor air. The only common exception is air from non-hazardous support spaces like offices and corridors serving the lab area. This single-pass rule is why lab buildings use so much energy — and why energy recovery on the exhaust stream and VAV hood controls pay back so well.
How does makeup air affect fume hood performance?
Directly and constantly. If makeup air falls short of exhaust, the lab goes negative, doors slam or won't open, and hoods can lose containment as room air rushes past the sash. If supply diffusers blast air across a hood face, they disrupt the capture airflow. I locate supply diffusers away from hoods, use low-velocity displacement-style delivery near hoods where possible, and interlock makeup air volume to track exhaust volume through every mode including night setback. The air balance report is a living document for a lab, not a one-time startup task.
