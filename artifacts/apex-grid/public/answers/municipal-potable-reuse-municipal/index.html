How Does Municipal Potable Water Reuse Work?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-10-02

Potable reuse treats municipal wastewater to drinking-water quality: indirect potable reuse (IPR) recharges groundwater or augments reservoirs with advanced-treated water; direct potable reuse (DPR) sends it straight to the water treatment plant or distribution system. The treatment train — typically micro/ultrafiltration, reverse osmosis, and advanced oxidation — plus rigorous monitoring produces water cleaner than most conventional supplies. Regulations now exist in a growing number of states, and public acceptance, earned through transparency, decides as many projects as engineering does.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

IPR vs. DPR: the two architectures

Indirect potable reuse uses an environmental buffer: advanced-treated wastewater recharges a groundwater basin (spreading basins or injection wells) or augments a surface-water reservoir, then the water utility withdraws it later through its normal supply. The buffer provides retention time, natural attenuation, and — critically — public and regulatory comfort. Orange County's Groundwater Replenishment System, the world's largest IPR project, has operated this way for decades, and the model is proven at scale.

Direct potable reuse eliminates the buffer: advanced-treated water goes directly to the drinking-water plant's intake, into the raw-water pipeline, or in 'treated-water augmentation' straight to distribution. DPR needs less geography (no basin or reservoir required) and suits inland communities without a suitable buffer — which is why Texas has led American DPR with projects like Big Spring and Wichita Falls. The engineering is more demanding (no environmental buffer means the treatment and monitoring carry the entire safety case), and the regulatory path is newer — but EPA's 2024 federal guidance and a growing roster of state DPR regulations are standardizing it.

The treatment train and the monitoring that proves it

The advanced treatment train for potable reuse is well-established: secondary/tertiary wastewater treatment, then microfiltration or ultrafiltration (pathogen and particle barrier), reverse osmosis (dissolved contaminants — salts, organics, pharmaceuticals, PFAS), and UV advanced oxidation (destruction of trace organics that pass RO). The product water is typically purer than the conventional supplies it joins — RO permeate is among the cleanest water in the municipal world, remineralized and stabilized before distribution.

What makes reuse safe isn't just the treatment — it's the monitoring proving the treatment works, continuously: online surrogates (TOC, conductivity, turbidity) with critical control points, pathogen log-removal credits verified per unit process, and response protocols that divert off-spec water automatically. The regulatory frameworks (state DPR/IPR rules, EPA guidance) specify the log-removal targets (often 12-10-10 or similar for virus/Giardia/Cryptosporidium) and the monitoring that demonstrates them. Designing a reuse plant means designing the monitoring and control system with the same rigor as the treatment — because the permit is granted to the verified performance, not the process diagram.

  • MF/UF → reverse osmosis → UV advanced oxidation
  • Product water typically purer than conventional supplies
  • Continuous monitoring with critical control points
  • Pathogen log-removal credits per unit process
  • Automatic diversion of off-spec water

Regulations: the state-by-state reality

Reuse regulation is state-driven: California (Title 22 for nonpotable; IPR groundwater recharge regulations long-standing; DPR regulations advancing), Texas (case-by-case DPR approvals with the most operating experience), Florida, Arizona, Colorado, and a growing list with formal IPR/DPR frameworks. EPA's 2024 National Water Reuse Action Plan guidance supports state program development but doesn't preempt it — the permit comes from the state drinking water program, and the requirements (treatment, monitoring, reporting, operator certification) are state-specific.

The regulatory strategy for a reuse project starts 2-3 years before design: pre-application meetings with the state drinking water and water quality programs, agreement on the treatment and monitoring framework, the environmental review, and — for IPR — the groundwater or reservoir augmentation permitting. Our engineers navigate this as a regulatory project running ahead of the engineering project, because the permit framework has to be settled before the design can be finalized. Jurisdictions considering reuse should start the regulatory conversation before spending on design — the state's expectations shape the plant.

Public acceptance: the project within the project

Every potable reuse project includes a public-acceptance program, because 'toilet to tap' rhetoric has killed technically sound projects. The record shows acceptance is earned through: transparency (plant tours, water-quality data published openly, independent expert panels), terminology (the industry's shift to 'purified water' reflects real communications science), demonstration (bottled purified water at public events — the Singapore NEWater playbook), and time — acceptance builds over years of honest engagement, not a single hearing.

The engineering supports the communications: the treatment train's multiple barriers, the monitoring data, the independent oversight — all communicable facts when the project team commits to openness. Our role for municipal clients spans the full program: feasibility and alternatives analysis, regulatory strategy, treatment design, and support for the public-engagement effort with the technical substance it needs. Potable reuse is the future of water supply in arid and water-stressed America — the communities building it now are buying decades of supply security. The engineering is proven; the question is whether the community's leadership will lead.

What else do project teams ask?

Is recycled drinking water safe?
Yes — potable reuse plants produce water that meets or exceeds all drinking water standards, typically verified by more intensive monitoring than conventional supplies. The multi-barrier treatment (filtration, reverse osmosis, advanced oxidation) plus continuous monitoring is designed specifically to guarantee safety.
What's the difference between indirect and direct potable reuse?
Indirect reuse (IPR) puts advanced-treated water through an environmental buffer — groundwater recharge or reservoir augmentation — before withdrawal as supply. Direct reuse (DPR) sends it straight to the water plant or distribution system. IPR is more established; DPR is growing fast with new state regulations.
Which states allow direct potable reuse?
Texas has the most operating DPR experience (case-by-case approvals); California, Colorado, Florida, Arizona, and others have formal DPR regulatory frameworks in development or adopted. EPA's 2024 guidance supports state programs. The list grows yearly — check the current state drinking water program rules.
How much does a potable reuse plant cost?
Highly variable with capacity and context, but advanced treatment trains run roughly $5-15+ million per MGD of capacity for the reuse treatment alone, plus conveyance and integration. Against the cost of new imported supply or desalination, reuse is often the economical option — the feasibility analysis should compare full life-cycle costs per acre-foot.
