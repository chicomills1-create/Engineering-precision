How Are Storm Shelters Engineered for Oklahoma Schools?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Storm shelters for Oklahoma schools are engineered to the ICC 500 storm shelter standard: reinforced concrete or masonry construction sized for the full school population, designed for 250-mph winds and debris impact, with tested doors, protected ventilation, emergency lighting, and accessibility -- permitted through the local authority having jurisdiction.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Occupancy Sizing and Dual-Use Design

The engineer establishes the shelter population from the school's enrollment plus staff, then applies ICC 500's floor area per occupant for the intended shelter use. Standing tornado shelters need less area per person than shelters designed for longer-duration hurricane occupancy, but the engineer plans for the realistic scenario: hundreds of children and adults arriving in minutes during a warning. Wheelchair spaces, accessible routes, and companion seating are included in the count, not added later. The resulting area is large, which is why the shelter is almost always a dual-use space. Dual-use design makes the shelter affordable by giving it a daily job, typically a gymnasium, cafeteria, music room, or multipurpose hall. The engineer designs the envelope so the daily use cannot defeat the shelter: walls have no windows or only tested shelter openings, roof-mounted equipment is anchored for the design wind, and interior finishes are detailed so nothing becomes debris. The structural drawings distinguish shelter envelope elements from ordinary construction so future renovations do not accidentally cut into the protected shell. The engineer also verifies that the daily occupancy's egress and the shelter occupancy's egress both work, since the two uses have different code requirements.

Structure, Doors, and Life Support

Structural design follows the ICC 500 load combinations: extreme wind pressure plus debris impact on walls, roof, and doors, with the structure detailed for ductile behavior per ACI 318. The engineer sizes reinforcement for the combined loads, details wall-to-roof and wall-to-foundation connections as continuous load paths, and designs the foundation for the overturning and uplift the 250-mph wind produces. In Oklahoma's expansive soils, the foundation also isolates the shelter from soil movement. Peer review is required by ICC 500 for larger shelters, and the engineer plans the design schedule around that independent review. Doors, ventilation, and power are life-safety systems. Each door is a tested assembly rated for the debris impact, with hardware that children can operate and that coordinates with the school's security procedures. Ventilation provides the code-required airflow for the full occupancy with missile-protected intakes and exhaust, and the engineer verifies the system works on standby power, because tornadoes cause the outages the shelter must ride through. Emergency lighting, communication provisions, and signage complete the package. The engineer specifies special inspection of the critical elements, rebar, concrete, anchors, and door installations, because a school shelter only gets one chance to work.

What else do project teams ask?

What standard governs Oklahoma school storm shelters?
ICC 500, the national storm shelter standard. It sets the 250-mph design wind speed for Oklahoma's tornado region, debris-impact testing for walls, roofs, and doors, minimum floor area per occupant, ventilation, lighting, and accessibility requirements, and peer review for larger shelters. Oklahoma school shelters are designed, reviewed, and inspected against this standard, and compliance documentation is part of the permit submittal.
Can a gymnasium really survive a direct tornado hit?
A gymnasium designed as an ICC 500 shelter can, because it is not an ordinary gym. Its walls, roof, and doors are engineered for 250-mph wind and debris impact, its foundation resists overturning and uplift, and it has no unprotected openings. The long roof spans that make gyms vulnerable in ordinary construction are designed here for the extreme loads with the reinforcement and connections the standard demands. The dual use works only because the shelter engineering governs every element.
How many people must a school shelter hold?
The design population is the school's peak occupancy: students plus staff, and often the surrounding community if the district designates the shelter for public use. The engineer multiplies that population by ICC 500's floor area per occupant for the shelter type, including wheelchair and accessible spaces. Under-sizing is not an option the standard permits, so accurate enrollment and staffing numbers are a design input the district must provide.
Who reviews and approves a school shelter in Oklahoma?
The local authority having jurisdiction issues the building permit with structural plan review, whether that is a city like Oklahoma City, Moore, or Tulsa, or a county office for unincorporated areas. Public school projects in Oklahoma also fall under state school construction oversight, which reviews alongside the local permit. ICC 500 additionally requires independent peer review of the shelter design above its size thresholds. The engineer organizes the submittal for all reviewers, since each checks a different part of the project.
