Engineering answers: domes

How Are Air-Supported Sports Domes Engineered?

An air-supported dome is a building held up by air pressure — typically just fractions of a psi above atmospheric — which makes its engineering unlike any conventional structure. The membrane, the inflation and backup systems, and the anchorage are the structure; the mechanical design must maintain pressurization in all conditions; and the code treats the occupied dome as an assembly space with its own requirements.

The concise answer

Engineer the membrane system for the site's snow, wind, and (where applicable) seismic loads per the manufacturer's engineering and the building code's air-supported-structure provisions; design primary plus backup inflation with emergency power so the dome survives a blower failure; detail the anchorage and grade-beam foundation for uplift and the pressure differential; and design the HVAC for pressurization control — every door cycle and every exhaust fan affects the building's structural pressure.

Snow, wind, and deflation safety

Snow is the critical load: the membrane sheds some snow by flexing, but design must account for accumulation patterns and the heating/inflation strategy that manages them. Wind design follows the code's provisions for air-supported structures, and the life-safety design must address controlled deflation — how occupants exit safely if pressure is lost — with emergency power for inflation and lighting on the egress path.

HVAC in a pressurized building

Every exhaust fan in a dome fights the inflation system, so ventilation design is pressurization design: balanced systems, airlocks or revolving doors at entries, and controls that coordinate the HVAC with the inflation plant. Heating a giant uninsulated (or minimally insulated) volume is the operating-cost reality — liner systems and destination-based zoning help, but owners should budget the energy honestly.

Frequently asked questions

How does an air-supported dome stay up?
Internal air pressure — typically 0.2 to 0.5 inches of water column above atmospheric — maintained by inflation blowers. The membrane carries the pressure load; backup blowers and emergency power keep it inflated through failures.
What happens if a dome loses pressure?
Engineered deflation: the membrane is designed to descend in a controlled manner, and the life-safety plan provides illuminated egress paths. Backup inflation and emergency power are code requirements, not options.
Can domes handle snow?
Yes, when engineered for the site: the membrane's flexibility sheds some snow, inflation and heating strategies manage the rest, and the structural design accounts for the site's snow loads. Heavy-snow regions need the full engineering, not a catalog assumption.
Are domes energy efficient?
The envelope is the challenge: large surface area with minimal insulation means high heating loads in cold climates. Liner systems, efficient heating, and honest operating budgets are part of the design conversation.
