What Does Office-Over-Residential Engineering Design Involve?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Office-over-residential engineering exploits the uses' opposite schedules — independent HVAC zones and separately metered electrical distribution per occupancy, rated separations and independent egress, and shared structure designed for combined but non-coincident loads.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Load Diversity and Shared Central Plants

The engineer quantifies the diversity before sizing anything. Office cooling peaks around 3 p.m. on a summer weekday; residential cooling peaks in the early evening as occupants return and cook. The combined peak is meaningfully lower than the sum of the individual peaks, and the engineer applies diversity factors to the chiller plant, the cooling tower, and the electrical service — sizing them for the real coincident load rather than the arithmetic total. This is one of the genuine economic advantages of the mix: a smaller central plant serving both uses than either use would need alone at its own peak. The distribution keeps the savings honest. Separate risers carry chilled water, heating water, and condenser water to the office and residential zones independently, each with its own metering — BTU meters on the hydronic branches, submeters on the electrical feeders — so operating costs split fairly between the office tenants and the residential association. Control sequences are written per occupancy: the office air handlers go to night setback and the residential fan coils hold steady, and the central plant stages to whichever load is calling. The engineer documents the metering points on the drawings because the cost allocation in the condo documents or leases depends on measurement the building can actually perform.

Egress, Elevators, and Acoustic Separation

The life-safety plans show two egress systems that happen to share a building. Office floors calculate occupant load at business densities with the corresponding exit width and number of exits; residential floors calculate per dwelling unit with secured, separated paths. Stairs are assigned: office stairs discharge to the office lobby, residential stairs to the residential lobby or directly to the exterior, and the engineer verifies that no required exit path crosses the other occupancy's secured area. Where the occupancies share a floor — a residential amenity deck above office space, for example — the separation and egress are drawn with particular care. Elevatoring is programmed for the two rush hours. The office bank is sized for the morning up-peak with destination dispatch; the residential bank is sized for a gentler, distributed demand with secured access. The engineer runs a traffic analysis for each bank independently rather than averaging the building, since a combined analysis understates the office morning peak and overstates residential demand. Acoustically, the office-to-residential boundary gets the same treatment as retail-to-residential: the assembly is rated for fire and designed for sound, with special attention to conference rooms, break areas with plumbing noise, and office mechanical rooms that sit adjacent to bedrooms.

Office-Over-Residential Design Checklist

Use this checklist before the design development set is issued: • HVAC diversity quantified and applied to central plant sizing, with separate risers per occupancy • BTU metering on hydronic branches and submetering on electrical feeders for fair cost allocation • Control sequences written per occupancy: office night setback independent of residential comfort • Separate electrical services or switchboards; residential feeders isolated from office harmonics • Standby power allocated per occupancy: business continuity vs residential life-safety needs • Independent egress paths: office stairs to office lobby, residential stairs to secured discharge • Elevator traffic analysis run per bank: office up-peak vs residential distributed demand • Acoustic design at the office-residential boundary for conference, break, and mechanical rooms

What else do project teams ask?

Do offices and apartments need separate HVAC systems?
They need independently controlled and metered distribution at minimum, and usually separate air-handling equipment. Offices run high daytime cooling loads with night setback; apartments need quiet, steady, individually controlled comfort around the clock. A shared central plant with separate risers, separate air handlers, and separate controls gives both uses what they need while capturing the diversity savings on the plant size. What they cannot share is a single zone or thermostat.
How is the electric bill split between office and residential?
By measurement, designed in from the start. The engineer provides separate utility services or separately metered switchboards for each occupancy, plus submeters on the major shared loads like the central plant and site lighting. The leases and condo documents then allocate costs from the meter readings. Without designed-in metering, the split becomes an estimate — and estimates become disputes.
Can office workers use the residential amenities?
Only if the design intends it. Residential amenities — fitness centers, lounges, roof decks — are secured for residents, and the engineer designs the access control, elevatoring, and egress so office occupants cannot wander in. If the developer wants shared amenities, the design treats them as a third zone with its own access rules, hours, and cost allocation, rather than leaving the boundary to signage.
What happens during office tenant improvements?
The office floors are designed for churn: spare electrical capacity, accessible ceiling plenums, and demising flexibility so a new tenant's layout does not touch the residential systems. The engineer documents the base-building capacities and the separation boundaries so each TI engineer knows the limits — available power, available cooling, and the rated assemblies that cannot be breached. Residential operations continue undisturbed because the systems and the separations were designed for exactly this.
