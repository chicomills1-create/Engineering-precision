How Are Charleston Historic Buildings Adapted for Flooding?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Charleston historic buildings are adapted for flooding by elevating mechanical systems above flood levels, installing flood vents and water-resistant materials at lower levels, adding deployable flood barriers at openings, reinforcing foundations against scour and hydrostatic loads, and threading every change through the Board of Architectural Review, since the AHJ decides what the historic fabric allows.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The 50 Percent Rule and Historic Structures

Substantial improvement is the tripwire in every Charleston flood adaptation. The rule compares the cost of the proposed work to the building's market value before the work starts: at fifty percent or more, the building must meet current floodplain requirements as if it were new construction. For a historic masonry building, that can mean elevating the lowest floor, floodproofing, or a combination, applied to the entire structure. Owners sometimes phase work to stay under the threshold, but phased projects that together exceed it are treated as a single improvement, and floodplain administrators track cumulative work. Historic designation opens a variance path, but it is not an exemption. The floodplain ordinance allows the AHJ to grant variances for designated historic structures, provided the work preserves the historic character and achieves the greatest flood protection feasible short of full compliance. In practice the engineer documents what full compliance would require, shows why it would destroy character-defining features, and proposes the closest achievable protection: vents, barriers, elevated systems, and flood-resistant materials. The variance request goes through the same board process as any other, with the state historic preservation office sometimes weighing in where tax credits are involved.

Vents, Barriers, and Elevated Systems

Flood vents are the simplest engineered protection and among the most effective. Sized by the enclosed area below the flood level, they let water enter and leave automatically so hydrostatic pressure never builds against foundation walls. On historic buildings the engineer selects vent models the review board accepts visually, often placing them in inconspicuous locations or behind latticework that matches the building's detailing. The vents must be kept clear and operational, which becomes part of the owner's maintenance responsibility. Deployable barriers handle the openings vents cannot. Engineered flood shields, gates, and door dams are fabricated for each specific opening, stored on site, and installed when a storm threatens, with installation details simple enough for the owner's staff to execute correctly. The engineer sizes them for hydrostatic and debris loads and details the anchorage into the historic masonry without damaging it. Above, the building's systems migrate upward: electrical service, HVAC, and water heating relocate above the design flood elevation, with new distribution routed through the historic structure in chases and closets the preservation review approves. A building that floods but keeps its systems dry returns to service in days instead of months.

What else do project teams ask?

What is wet floodproofing versus dry floodproofing?
Wet floodproofing lets water in deliberately: flood vents equalize pressure, materials below the flood level resist water damage, and the building is designed to be cleaned and dried after the water recedes. Dry floodproofing keeps water out: sealed walls, shields at openings, and a structure engineered to resist the full hydrostatic and hydrodynamic loads. Charleston historic buildings typically use wet floodproofing below and dry floodproofing at openings, since old masonry walls were never designed to hold back several feet of water pressure.
Can a historic building in Charleston be elevated?
Physically, yes: engineers routinely lift masonry buildings onto taller foundations. In Charleston's historic districts the question is preservation review, not structural feasibility. The Board of Architectural Review evaluates whether the new foundation height, stairs, and detailing preserve the building's character and the streetscape. Successful elevations detail the new work to match historic materials and proportions, and the board has approved them where flood risk clearly justified the change.
How do flood vents protect a foundation?
A few feet of standing water against a foundation wall creates thousands of pounds of lateral pressure trying to push the wall in. Flood vents open automatically as water rises, letting it flow into the enclosed area so the water level inside matches outside, and the pressure equalizes instead of loading the wall. The engineer sizes the total vent opening area to the enclosed square footage per the floodplain code's formula, and the vents must remain unobstructed to work.
What is the Board of Architectural Review's role in flood adaptation?
In Charleston's Old and Historic and Old City districts, the Board of Architectural Review must approve any exterior change, including flood vents, barriers, elevated foundations, relocated equipment, and new stairs or latticework. The board applies the city's preservation standards alongside floodplain requirements, and its approval precedes the building permit. The AHJ, which includes the board's design authority and the building department's code authority, makes the final decision on what gets built.
