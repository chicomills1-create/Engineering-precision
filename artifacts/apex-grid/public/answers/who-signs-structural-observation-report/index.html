Who Can Sign a Structural Observation Report?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Only the engineer of record or a PE with responsible charge of the observation can sign a structural observation report. The signature certifies professional engineering judgment about design conformance — it cannot be delegated to the contractor, the special inspector, or unsupervised staff.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What the signature actually certifies

Every signature on an observation report carries three implicit certifications, and the signer must be able to defend all three. First, that the observations were made under their responsible charge — they directed the scope and timing of the visits and controlled how findings were gathered. Second, that the conformance assessment reflects their own engineering judgment — the work observed generally matches the design concept in their sealed drawings, with any deviations identified and dispositioned. Third, that unresolved items are clearly flagged rather than smoothed over — a report that notes a deviation and records the required correction is doing its job; one that stays silent about a known problem is a liability for everyone.\n\nThis is why the signature can't be treated as paperwork. When a building official reads an observation report at final inspection, they're reading the EOR's professional statement that the structure standing in front of them is the structure the EOR designed — or that the differences were caught, evaluated, and resolved under engineering supervision. That statement is only as credible as the responsible charge behind it.

Signature, seal, and the digital question

Signature and seal do different work on a field report, and jurisdictions treat them differently. The signature identifies the responsible PE and attests to the report's contents. The seal declares the document an engineering product issued under the PE's responsible charge, subject to the state board's authority. On interim observation visits, most AHJs accept a signed report; the seal typically becomes mandatory on the documents that close out the structural record — the final observation summary, the EOR's letter of general conformance, or the structural final certification, depending on local terminology.\n\nDigital seals have settled most of the old friction. In every state where we're licensed, a digital seal with proper authentication — verifiable, tamper-evident, under the PE's sole control — carries the same weight as a wet seal on a field report. The practical rule: use the same sealing standard for observation reports that you use for the drawings, keep the seal under the signing PE's exclusive control, and confirm any AHJ-specific format requirements before the first report goes out. A report the reviewer can't authenticate is a report that doesn't exist.

Closeout: what the building official checks

At final inspection, the structural paper trail gets read as a complete story, and the signatures are how the reviewer verifies the narrators. Assemble the closeout package so each piece answers its question without hunting.\n\n• Every structural observation report signed by the EOR (or the PE who held responsible charge), with interim reports and the final summary clearly sequenced\n• Seal applied wherever the AHJ requires it — at minimum on the final observation summary and the EOR's closeout certification\n• Special inspection reports present as the independent factual record, signed under the agency's program — never substituted for observation reports\n• Every deficiency or deviation noted in any report matched to its documented disposition: accepted, repaired per procedure, or replaced\n• Change of EOR documented with the AHJ if one occurred, with the successor's assumption of responsible charge on record\n• Digital seals verifiable and under the signing PE's sole control, matching the jurisdiction's accepted format

What else do project teams ask?

Can a junior engineer or field representative sign the observation report?
Only under the direct supervision and responsible charge of the signing PE — and even then, it's the PE's signature on the report, not the representative's. The field rep's role is gathering: documenting conditions, photographing the work, recording measurements at the milestones the EOR specified. The engineering conclusions — conformance, deviations, required corrections — are the EOR's alone. State boards are explicit that responsible charge means actual direction and control, not a rubber stamp on someone else's site visit. If the EOR never reviewed the findings and just signed the rep's notes, that's the exact practice the rules prohibit.
Does a structural observation report need a PE seal, or just a signature?
It depends on the jurisdiction and how the report will be used. Many building departments require the observation reports in the closeout package to be both signed and sealed by the EOR — the seal marks it as an engineering document issued under responsible charge. Others accept a signed report on the engineer's letterhead for interim visits and require the seal only on the final observation summary or the EOR's closeout certification letter. Digital seals are accepted in most of our licensed states now, provided the seal is verifiable and under the PE's sole control. Before the first site visit, confirm with the AHJ what the closeout package requires — discovering at final inspection that every interim report needs a seal is an avoidable scramble.
Can the special inspector sign the structural observation report instead?
No. The special inspector and the structural observer certify different things under different authority, and neither can stand in for the other. The inspector's reports are factual records of verification performed under their approved agency's program — they attest that specific work was inspected and met or failed the criteria. They deliberately do not render design-conformance opinions, because the inspector is independent of the design. The observation report is the opposite: it's the designer's own professional judgment that the work matches the design concept. Asking the inspector to sign it would put independent verification and design authorship in the same hands — the conflict the code's separation of roles exists to prevent.
What happens to observation reports if the engineer of record changes mid-project?
The successor EOR must formally assume responsible charge, and that assumption has consequences for the paper trail. The new EOR reviews the sealed drawings, the observation reports issued to date, and the field conditions, then notifies the building official that they are now the engineer of record. Observation reports signed by the prior EOR remain valid as historical records of what was observed — but the successor takes responsibility going forward and typically re-verifies any open or unresolved items personally before signing new reports. Where a jurisdiction requires it, the change of EOR is documented with the AHJ before further structural work is concealed. Continuity matters: gaps in the observation record are exactly what plan reviewers flag at closeout.
