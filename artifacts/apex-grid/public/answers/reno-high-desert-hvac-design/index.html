How Is HVAC Designed for Reno's High Desert Climate?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Reno HVAC is designed from room-by-room Manual J loads using local design temperatures, with right-sized heating, high-efficiency cooling suited to dry air, ductwork designed for low latent loads, and upgraded filtration for wildfire smoke - all complying with Nevada's energy code and reviewed by the city or county AHJ.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The High Desert Load Profile

Manual J load calculations translate Reno's climate into equipment sizes, room by room. The heating load reflects cold winter design temperatures, the thin air's poor heat retention, and infiltration through the envelope - which the engineer estimates from the blower-door target the energy code implies, not from optimistic assumptions. The cooling load reflects solar gain, internal gains, and the sensible heat of hot outdoor air, with the latent component small enough that dehumidification barely registers as a design consideration. That near-zero latent load is the defining difference from humid climates: the cooling equipment is selected for sensible capacity, and the engineer checks that the selected unit's sensible heat ratio actually matches the load. The diurnal swing reshapes the design beyond the peak numbers. A forty-degree daily temperature range means the building's mass absorbs afternoon heat and releases it at night, flattening the load curve - if the controls let it. Night-flush strategies, where cool night air purges the day's heat from the mass, work beautifully in Reno's climate and the engineer can design for them with economizer controls and operable ventilation paths. But the swing also punishes poor zoning: a single thermostat cannot serve a west-facing great room and a north-facing bedroom when they sit thirty degrees apart at 4 p.m. The load calculation's room-by-room detail becomes the zoning plan.

Equipment Selection for Dry Air and Big Swings

Heat pumps earn their place in Reno because the winter, while cold, sits largely within the efficient operating range of modern cold-climate equipment - and because one machine handles both the dominant heating load and the sensible cooling load. The engineer selects the heat pump for the heating load at the winter design temperature, verifies capacity at the coldest expected conditions, and provides supplemental heat - electric resistance or a gas furnace in a dual-fuel arrangement - for the hours the heat pump cannot carry alone. Sizing stays tight to the Manual J numbers: an oversized heat pump short-cycles in the mild swing seasons, sacrificing both efficiency and comfort. Evaporative cooling remains a legitimate Reno option precisely because the air is so dry - a well-maintained evaporative cooler delivers comfortable supply air at a fraction of the energy of refrigeration cooling. Its limits define its applications: it adds moisture the occupants may not want, it cannot filter wildfire smoke, and its effectiveness collapses during humid monsoon pulses. The engineer presents that tradeoff plainly, and many designs pair strategies - refrigerated cooling for the bedrooms where filtration and humidity control matter, evaporative for large open living areas, or a whole-house refrigerated system with the evaporative cooler as a shoulder-season economizer. Ductwork is sealed and insulated to the energy code's standards and routed inside the thermal envelope wherever possible, because the dry desert air punishes duct losses in both seasons.

Smoke, Fresh Air, and Energy Code

Wildfire smoke has moved from nuisance to design parameter. The engineer designs the filtration chain - filter rack sized for deep-pleat MERV-13 or better media, an air handler with the static pressure to drive it, and sealed ductwork so unfiltered attic or crawlspace air cannot bypass the filter - as a system, because a high-MERV filter in an undersized rack just gets bypassed or starved. Controls include a smoke mode: motorized outdoor-air dampers that close down ventilation intake during smoke events while the system recirculates and filters indoor air, with a manual override the occupants understand. The design notes explain the mode in plain language, because the best smoke strategy fails if nobody knows it exists. Energy code compliance ties the mechanical design to the envelope. Nevada's adopted energy provisions set equipment efficiency minimums, duct sealing and insulation requirements, and controls expectations that the engineer documents on the compliance forms. The plan reviewer checks the Manual J summary, the equipment schedules with rated efficiencies, and the controls narrative against those provisions. Inside Reno the city's building division is the AHJ; in the unincorporated county it is Washoe County - and each runs its own permit portal, review queue, and inspection scheduling, so the engineer confirms jurisdiction before the submittal goes in.

What else do project teams ask?

Does evaporative cooling work in Reno?
Yes, when the air is dry - which is most of Reno's summer. An evaporative cooler can deliver comfortable cooling at much lower energy use than refrigerated air conditioning in single-digit humidity. Its limits are real: it adds indoor moisture, it cannot filter wildfire smoke, and its cooling effect fades during humid monsoon pulses. The engineer weighs those tradeoffs per project, and many homes pair evaporative cooling for living areas with filtered refrigerated cooling for bedrooms.
Why does Manual J matter more in a desert climate?
Because the loads are lopsided in ways rules of thumb miss: nearly all sensible cooling with almost no latent load, intense solar gain through unshaded glass, and a dominant heating season. A rule-of-thumb tonnage sized for a humid climate will be wrong in both directions in Reno - oversized for the mild latent load, undersized for the solar-driven sensible peak. Room-by-room Manual J is the only way to get zoning, equipment selection, and duct sizing right.
How is wildfire smoke addressed in HVAC design?
With a filtration and controls strategy: MERV-13 or better filtration on a properly sized filter rack, an air handler with enough static pressure to push air through it, sealed ducts that prevent bypass, and a smoke mode that closes outdoor-air dampers and recirculates indoor air through the filter during smoke events. The design keeps code-required ventilation available in normal operation and gives occupants a simple way to switch modes when the sky turns orange.
Does mechanical permitting differ between Reno and Washoe County?
Yes. Projects inside the City of Reno go through the city's building division, while unincorporated Washoe County projects go through the county's building department. Both enforce Nevada's adopted mechanical and energy codes, but their permit portals, plan-review timelines, fees, and inspection scheduling differ. The engineer confirms the authority having jurisdiction at the start so the submittal package matches the right checklist.
