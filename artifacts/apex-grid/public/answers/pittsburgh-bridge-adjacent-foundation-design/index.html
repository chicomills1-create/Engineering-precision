How Are Foundations Designed Next to Pittsburgh Bridges?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Foundations next to Pittsburgh bridges are designed by mapping bridge substructures and right-of-way limits, modeling vibration and excavation impacts on the bridge, using deep foundations or secant-pile shoring to protect it, and coordinating with PennDOT and the City of Pittsburgh for encroachment permits and construction monitoring.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Vibration and Excavation Impact Analysis

Vibration analysis starts with the source and the soil. The engineer estimates peak particle velocity at the bridge from the planned equipment--driven piles, hoe rams, vibratory rollers--using attenuation relationships for the site's soils, then compares the result against the bridge's tolerance. Historic masonry or early concrete bridges tolerate far less vibration than modern structures, and bearings and expansion joints are often the most sensitive elements. Where predicted vibrations exceed limits, the design changes the method: drilled shafts instead of driven piles, chemical or mechanical rock breaking instead of blasting, static rollers instead of vibratory. The specification writes these method restrictions into the contract, because the low-bid alternative is usually the high-vibration one. Excavation impact analysis models what removing soil does to the bridge's support. The engineer calculates the lateral pressure relief on the bridge foundation, estimates ground movements using empirical and numerical methods, and checks that the bridge's tolerance--often just fractions of an inch for bearings--is not exceeded. Dewatering gets its own analysis: drawdown consolidates compressible soils, and the settlement trough extends well beyond the excavation. Cutoff walls or recharge systems limit drawdown where the analysis shows risk, and the monitoring plan puts survey points and inclinometers on the bridge before any ground is broken.

Protective Foundation and Shoring Systems

Deep foundations are the default protective strategy. Drilled shafts socketed into bedrock carry the building's loads deep below the bridge's foundation zone, with minimal excavation and no driving vibration. Micropiles serve where access is tight or loads are moderate--their small drilling equipment works in constrained riverfront sites and produces negligible vibration. The engineer designs these foundations for the building's loads plus any lateral earth pressure from the adjacent bridge approach fill, and details the pile caps and grade beams to bridge over utilities and old foundations the borings reveal. Shoring is designed as a protection system, not just an excavation support. Secant pile walls or slurry walls form a rigid, nearly watertight barrier that limits both ground movement and dewatering drawdown--critical next to a bridge. The wall is designed for the full earth and hydrostatic pressures, braced or tied back with systems that do not load the bridge, and monitored with inclinometers during excavation. Underpinning is the last resort: if analysis shows the bridge foundation itself needs support, the engineer designs micropile or jet-grout underpinning installed with the bridge owner's direct oversight. Every one of these systems is reviewed by the transportation agency before construction, because the bridge stays open and safe is the non-negotiable constraint.

What else do project teams ask?

How close can you build to a bridge in Pittsburgh?
There is no single setback--it depends on the bridge's foundation type and depth, the soils, the proposed construction methods, and the right-of-way boundaries. The engineer maps all of these, models the construction impacts, and proposes a layout the transportation agency accepts. Building inside a PennDOT or railroad easement is generally prohibited regardless of engineering.
Can pile driving be used near a bridge?
Usually not near sensitive or historic bridges. Driven piles generate the highest construction vibrations, and the engineer typically specifies drilled shafts, augered cast-in-place piles, or micropiles near bridges instead. Where driving is proposed, the analysis must show vibrations at the bridge stay within the agency's limits, with monitoring to prove it.
What permits are needed to build next to a Pittsburgh bridge?
Work near state-owned bridges requires PennDOT review and encroachment permits, with the agency setting conditions on methods, monitoring, and setbacks. The City of Pittsburgh issues the building permits and reviews the structural design. Railroad bridges add the railroad's own engineering review. The authorities having jurisdiction decide approvals--the engineer coordinates all of them before finalizing the foundation design.
How is the bridge monitored during construction?
With a monitoring plan the engineer designs and the agency approves: survey prisms or automated total stations tracking bridge movement, vibration monitors with alarm thresholds that stop work, inclinometers in the shoring, and piezometers tracking groundwater. Readings are taken before construction to establish baselines, then continuously during excavation and foundation work.
