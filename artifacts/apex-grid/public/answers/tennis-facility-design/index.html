What Engineering Goes Into a Tennis Facility That Lasts Decades?

The concise answer

Tennis facility design is the engineering of court bases for flatness and crack control, surfacing systems, subsurface drainage, fencing, and even court lighting, plus clubhouse MEP. It covers post-tensioned or asphalt court construction, site grading and stormwater, and support buildings. A tennis facility is a precision-paving project with a hospitality building attached. The courts are the product, and they live or die on what's underneath: the subgrade preparation, the slab or pavement design, and the drainage. Owners see the green surface and the white lines; engineers see the base section, the joint layout, and the grading plan. The facilities that still play beautifully after fifteen years were engineered from the dirt up.

Where the engineering actually lives

Court civil and structural design defines the playing product: subgrade preparation and testing, base selection (post-tensioned concrete versus asphalt) for the soils and budget, flatness tolerances verified at construction, and the acrylic or clay surfacing specified as a system with the base. Fencing, windscreens, and net-post footings get structural design for wind and use loads. Site civil design grades the court battery for surface drainage, provides subsurface drainage and stormwater management, and lays out parking, paths, and landscaping. Electrical design delivers the court lighting — pole heights, fixture aiming, and neighbor shielding designed photometrically, not by guess — plus clubhouse power. The clubhouse gets full MEP: locker-room plumbing for tournament peaks, HVAC, and food-service support where provided.

What keeps a tennis facility project on track

Build from the dirt up: subgrade, base, and drainage decide the courts' fate long before the surfacing goes down. Then light them right and support them properly. The essentials:

  • Prepare the subgrade properly: testing and preparation that prevent heave and settlement
  • Choose the base for the site: post-tensioning where soils or longevity demand it
  • Verify flatness at construction: tolerances checked while corrections are possible
  • Drain every court: surface grading plus subsurface systems where soils require
  • Design lighting photometrically: even coverage with neighbor shielding and good controls

What else do project teams ask?

Post-tensioned concrete or asphalt for tennis courts?
Post-tensioned concrete resists cracking better and lasts longer, making it the premium choice; asphalt is cheaper upfront but cracks more and needs resurfacing sooner. The decision is lifecycle cost on the site's soils — expansive soils strongly favor post-tensioning.
How do tennis courts drain?
Through surface grading (typically a slight single-plane slope) plus subsurface drainage where soils are tight, so no court holds water. Standing water damages surfacing and stops play — drainage design is fundamental, not optional.
What lighting do tennis courts need?
Even, glare-free illumination across every court — typically 30-plus footcandles for club play — on tall poles with aimed fixtures, shielding for neighbors, and controls. Poor lighting is the most common evening-play complaint.
What does a tennis clubhouse need?
Pro shop, locker rooms with showers, viewing areas, and often food service — with MEP sized for tournament peaks. Restroom and parking counts should reflect the facility's busiest events, not average daily use.
