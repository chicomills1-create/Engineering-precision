How Is Emergency Power Designed for Municipal Buildings?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers classify loads by code category, size generators for sequenced startup of critical city functions, provide multi-day fuel storage, and prove the system with monthly loaded testing.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Load Classification and Critical Load Selection

The electrical code sorts backup loads into categories with real consequences. Emergency systems — egress lighting, fire alarm, elevators for firefighter use — must transfer within seconds and ride on dedicated wiring. Legally required standby covers systems the code or the authority having jurisdiction demands: smoke control, certain communications, and in municipal practice often the 911 center and EOC. Optional standby is everything the city chooses to protect beyond code: the data room, the public counter, the fuel pumps. The engineer builds the load list with the city's emergency manager and IT staff, because only they know which systems the city actually needs in a disaster. Each load gets a category, a transfer-time requirement, and a place in the startup sequence. The one-line diagram shows separate transfer switches and distribution for each category — mixing them is a code violation and an operational mess.

Generator Sizing, Transfer, and Fuel Strategy

Generator sizing is analytical, not generous. The engineer totals the critical loads with diversity, then models the motor-starting sequence — dispatch UPS recharge, HVAC compressors, pump motors — staggering starts so inrush never exceeds the generator's capacity. Oversizing wastes fuel and causes wet-stacking in diesels; undersizing fails the city when it matters most. The sizing calculation is documented and reviewable. Transfer switches are specified per category with the right timing: emergency loads in seconds, standby loads after. Fuel strategy follows the design event — on-site diesel sized for the target runtime with polishing and testing, or natural gas where the utility's earthquake and storm resilience justifies it. The engineer also designs the refueling plan for events that outlast the tank, because the design basis is the beginning of the plan, not the end of it.

Municipal Emergency Power Checklist

A complete emergency power design keeps essential city functions running: • Load inventory classified into emergency, legally required, and optional standby • Generator sized by sequenced motor-starting analysis, not nameplate stacking • Separate automatic transfer switches per code category with correct timing • Fuel storage sized for the design runtime with polishing and refueling plan • UPS bridging for dispatch, EOC, and communications through the transfer gap • Monthly loaded testing program with logged results • Documented one-line, load lists, and sequences for emergency operators

What else do project teams ask?

What municipal loads are legally required to have backup power?
It depends on the occupancy and the authority having jurisdiction, but typical requirements include egress lighting, fire alarm and detection, elevator recall, smoke control systems, and in many jurisdictions the 911 dispatch and emergency communications the city depends on. Beyond code, cities add optional standby loads — the EOC, data rooms, water and sewer controls — as policy decisions. The engineer documents the classification of every load so the design, the inspector, and the operator all agree.
How long should a municipal generator run on stored fuel?
Design practice commonly targets 48 to 72 hours of on-site fuel for municipal critical facilities, with a refueling contract and plan for longer events. The right number comes from the region's hazard profile and the city's emergency plan — how long until fuel trucks can reliably reach the site after the design earthquake, hurricane, or ice storm. The engineer sizes the tank to the plan and documents the resupply chain beyond it.
Should municipal buildings use diesel or natural gas generators?
Diesel offers on-site fuel independence — the generator runs as long as the tank and the refueling plan hold, regardless of utility damage. Natural gas avoids fuel storage and maintenance but depends on the gas distribution system surviving the same event that killed the power. Many cities choose diesel for their most critical facilities and gas for secondary ones. The engineer presents the tradeoff with the region's utility reliability data so the choice is evidence-based.
Why test generators under load every month?
Because unloaded exercise only proves the engine starts — it does not prove the transfer switch transfers, the generator carries the real load, or the cooling system handles full heat. Monthly loaded tests run the entire emergency power chain against actual building load, revealing weak batteries, clogged filters, and transfer failures before the storm does. The results are logged for the maintenance record and often for the insurer.
