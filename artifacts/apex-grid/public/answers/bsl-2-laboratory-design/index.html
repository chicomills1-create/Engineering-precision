How Are BSL-2 Laboratories Designed for Safe Research Work?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers design BSL-2 labs with directional airflow into a negative-pressure zone, biosafety cabinets for open agent work, decontaminable finishes, an accessible autoclave, and controlled access.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Directional Airflow and Pressure Relationships

Directional airflow is the invisible wall of a BSL-2 lab. The engineer sizes supply and exhaust so the lab runs slightly negative to the corridor — typically verified with a pressure differential monitor or at minimum a visual indicator — and sequences the air to move from offices and corridors into the lab, never the reverse. Where the lab has an anteroom or airlock, the cascade steps down through it, adding a buffer zone that protects the pressure relationship every time the door opens. Exhaust placement matters as much as quantity. General room exhaust grilles are positioned to sweep air across the room toward the biosafety cabinets and out, avoiding short-circuiting where supply air jumps straight to the exhaust without ventilating the breathing zone. Fume hoods and biosafety cabinets get their own dedicated exhaust connections sized for the cabinet's rated flow, with ductwork that maintains containment even as filters load. The design documents the air balance schedule so the testing and balancing contractor can prove every relationship on paper and in the field.

Biosafety Cabinets, Autoclaves, and Decontamination

The biological safety cabinet is the primary containment device, and the engineering around it starts with selection: the cabinet class and type must match the work, with ducted models requiring a dedicated exhaust connection sized to the manufacturer's specified flow and pressure. Placement rules are strict — cabinets sit away from doors, high-traffic paths, and supply diffusers whose air currents can disrupt the cabinet's protective airflow. The engineer shows clearances for certification access on the drawings, because a cabinet that cannot be certified cannot be used. The autoclave is the lab's decontamination workhorse. The design provides steam or electric service per the unit's requirements, tempered cooling water, floor drainage rated for hot discharge, and ventilation for the heat the sterilizer rejects into the room. Locating the autoclave adjacent to the lab minimizes the distance contaminated waste travels, and the layout keeps clean and dirty workflows separated so sterilized materials never cross paths with waste awaiting treatment.

BSL-2 Laboratory Design Checklist

A complete BSL-2 design addresses containment, decontamination, and the people using the lab: • Lab held negative to adjacent spaces with a documented pressure cascade • Directional airflow from clean areas toward the lab and its exhaust points • Biological safety cabinets selected, placed, and ducted per manufacturer requirements • Autoclave in or adjacent to the lab with steam, water, drainage, and ventilation • Seamless, chemical-resistant finishes with coved flooring for decontamination • Handwashing sink near the exit; eyewash and emergency shower within required travel • Self-closing lockable doors with access control and biohazard signage

What else do project teams ask?

What is the difference between BSL-1 and BSL-2 laboratory design?
BSL-1 covers work with well-characterized agents not known to cause disease in healthy adults, and its design is essentially a standard lab with good practices. BSL-2 steps up for moderate-risk agents: the design adds directional inward airflow with the lab negative to surrounding spaces, biological safety cabinets for procedures that can create aerosols, an autoclave for decontaminating waste, restricted access with self-closing doors, and decontaminable finishes. The engineering controls are what separate the two levels, not just the procedures.
Do BSL-2 labs need an anteroom?
Not strictly required by the baseline guidance, but frequently designed in because anterooms make everything else work better. An anteroom provides a pressure buffer so the lab's negative relationship survives door openings, a place for donning and doffing protective equipment, and separation between the corridor and the work zone. For labs handling agents near the upper end of BSL-2 risk, or where the corridor serves many occupants, the engineer will usually recommend one.
Where should the biosafety cabinet be placed in the room?
Away from doors, operable windows, high-traffic walkways, and supply air diffusers — anything that creates air currents across the cabinet face can break its containment. The cabinet needs the clearance the manufacturer specifies for certification and service, typically open space in front and access to the top or sides. The engineer coordinates the final location with the HVAC layout so no diffuser throws air toward the cabinet opening, and verifies placement during commissioning with a smoke test of the room's airflow patterns.
Can a BSL-2 lab share HVAC with the rest of the building?
Supply air can come from a central system, but exhaust from a BSL-2 lab should not be recirculated to other spaces — it is exhausted directly outdoors, discharged away from intakes and operable openings. The design also needs to hold the pressure relationship when the central system modulates, which often means dedicated exhaust with its own controls rather than a branch off a variable building system. The engineer verifies the pressure cascade holds across all operating modes, not just design conditions.
