How Is Stormwater Designed for Fayetteville's Karst Terrain?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Fayetteville stormwater design meets the city's four drainage standards, water quality, channel protection, overbank, and extreme flood protection, while keeping concentrated runoff out of the karst limestone below. That means detention over infiltration on karst sites, geotechnical mapping of voids beneath every basin, lined or underdrained treatment features, and permanent maintenance obligations recorded on the plat.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Four Standards and Karst-Safe Compliance

Fayetteville's drainage framework asks every regulated development to control both the quality and the timing of its runoff. The water-quality standard requires capturing and treating runoff to remove a high percentage of total suspended solids, which designers meet with bioretention cells, wet ponds, permeable pavements, or manufactured treatment devices sized to the manual's criteria. The channel-protection standard addresses the frequent storms that shape streams: the added runoff volume from the one-year storm must be detained and released over an extended period, commonly around forty hours, so downstream channels see pre-development-like flow durations instead of erosive pulses. Overbank and extreme flood standards then size the system for the larger design storms, keeping structures and finished floors safe and preventing the development from worsening flooding downstream. On karst, each standard gets a siting filter. The geotechnical investigation identifies sinkholes, voids, springs, and shallow rock across the site, and the civil engineer routes basins, swales, and outfalls away from those features. Bioretention and other filtration practices get underdrains and impermeable liners where karst is present so treated water leaves through a pipe rather than percolating into a conduit. Wet ponds are checked for leakage potential, since a pond that will not hold water is a maintenance failure and a possible karst trigger. Outlet structures are designed for controlled release rates with trash and sediment forebays, and the calculations document pre- versus post-development hydrology for the reviewer's check. Nothing in this approach conflicts with the manual; it is the manual applied with knowledge of what lies beneath.

Streamside Protection and the Review Process

Fayetteville's streamside protection rules add a second regulatory layer along regulated streams, with protected zones, buffer widths, and strict limits on what can be built or graded near the water. Stormwater outfalls that must cross these zones are designed for minimal disturbance: few discharge points, alignments that clear the least vegetation, non-erosive velocities, and energy dissipation at the outlet. The civil engineer coordinates the stream buffer plan with the overall grading plan early, because a site layout that ignores the buffers will be redesigned at review, and redesigning around a recorded buffer is far more expensive than laying out around it the first time. The review process itself rewards preparation. City staff review drainage calculations, plans, and details against the Drainage Criteria Manual, and the City Engineer is the authority on technical drainage decisions, including where new conveyances may cross protected zones. Pre-application meetings surface the site's particular issues, karst features, stream buffers, downstream constraints, before the design is locked. For unincorporated Washington County parcels, the county's reviewers apply the county's standards, and state permits may govern stream and wetland impacts regardless of jurisdiction. In every case the AHJ decides what is approved, and a submittal that shows its hydrology, documents its karst investigation, and details its maintenance plan moves faster than one that leaves the reviewer guessing.

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't Fayetteville just infiltrate stormwater into the ground?
Much of Fayetteville sits on karst limestone with voids, conduits, and sinkholes that carry water directly to the aquifer with almost no filtration. Infiltrating polluted runoff over karst risks groundwater contamination and can trigger sinkhole activity by concentrating water along dissolution pathways. Designers therefore favor detention, controlled release, and lined treatment features on karst sites, infiltrating only where the geotechnical investigation shows it is safe.
What are Fayetteville's four drainage standards?
The city's framework covers water quality, channel protection, overbank flood protection, and extreme flood protection. Water quality requires high removal of suspended solids from post-development runoff. Channel protection detains the added volume from frequent storms and releases it slowly to prevent downstream erosion. Overbank and extreme flood standards protect structures and prevent worsened flooding in larger design storms. They apply to developments adding significant impervious area under the Unified Development Code and Drainage Criteria Manual.
Who maintains a detention pond after the developer leaves?
The property owner or the property owners association, in most cases. Fayetteville places maintenance responsibility for private stormwater facilities on the owner, typically noted on the final plat, and the city maintains only public facilities and drainage within its easements. Unmaintained basins silt up, lose storage, and stop meeting their design standards, so the maintenance obligation is a permanent part of the project, not a construction-phase item.
Does Washington County enforce the same stormwater rules as Fayetteville?
No. Inside the city, the Drainage Criteria Manual and city review apply, with the City Engineer as the technical authority. In unincorporated Washington County, the county's own standards and reviewers govern. The engineering principles are the same, but the submittal requirements, design criteria, and review timelines differ, so the engineer confirms the AHJ and designs to the rules in effect for that jurisdiction at the time of submittal.
