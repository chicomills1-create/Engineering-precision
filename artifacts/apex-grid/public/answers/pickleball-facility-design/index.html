What Does Engineering a Modern Pickleball Facility Involve?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Pickleball facility design is the engineering of high-density indoor court buildings purpose-built for pickleball. It covers acoustic isolation and treatment for the sport's distinctive noise, court surfacing and lighting for the smaller fast game, exertion-level HVAC, and the social and support spaces that make these facilities community destinations.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The concise answer

Pickleball facility design is the engineering of high-density indoor court buildings purpose-built for pickleball. It covers acoustic isolation and treatment for the sport's distinctive noise, court surfacing and lighting for the smaller fast game, exertion-level HVAC, and the social and support spaces that make these facilities community destinations.\n\nThe acoustic design is the project. Everything else — structure, lighting, HVAC — follows established sports-building practice, but the noise signature of pickleball is unique and the regulatory and neighbor-relations consequences of ignoring it are severe. I tell every pickleball developer the same thing: budget the acoustic engineering like it's structural, because in permitting and operations, it carries the same weight.

Where the engineering actually lives

Sound isolation starts with the envelope: mass-loaded walls, sealed penetrations, acoustic doors and vestibules, and roof/ceiling assemblies that contain the pop. Inside, reverberation control — absorptive baffles and wall panels — keeps the playing environment tolerable when a dozen courts are going at once; without it, the interior is punishingly loud and speech is impossible. An acoustic consultant should be on the team from schematic design, with pre- and post-construction testing to prove performance.\n\nThe rest is efficient sports-building engineering. Lighting at 50 to 75 footcandles with good uniformity (the ball is smaller and faster than tennis, so contrast matters), HVAC sized for dense exertion-level occupancy with dehumidification, and court surfacing over a properly prepared slab. Because court density is high, the per-court MEP cost is actually favorable — the building systems serve a lot of play per square foot.

What I push for on day one

Pickleball facilities are being built fast, and the fast ones skip the engineering that matters. The checklist that prevents expensive retrofits is short and non-negotiable.\n\nHere's what I require.

  • Engage acoustic expertise immediately: isolation and interior treatment designed, tested, and verified
  • Verify the box before committing: slab condition, structure, and egress for conversion candidates
  • Design lighting for the small fast ball: uniformity and contrast, not just footcandles
  • Size HVAC for dense exertion: ventilation and dehumidification for full courts, all day
  • Prepare the slab properly: flatness and crack control before a dollar of surfacing goes down
  • Plan the social core: lounges, viewing, and food service are revenue, not afterthoughts

What else do project teams ask?

Why is noise the biggest issue in pickleball facility design?
The paddle-ball impact is a sharp impulsive sound around 1,000 Hz that carries surprisingly far — neighbors hundreds of feet away have filed successful complaints against outdoor courts. Indoor facilities engineer for it with mass in the walls, acoustic seals on every door and penetration, absorptive ceilings and wall panels to control reverberation inside, and sometimes floating floor systems. Acoustic design isn't optional here; it's the difference between a permitted facility and a lawsuit.
How many pickleball courts fit in a building?
A regulation court is 20 by 44 feet, and with safety overruns each court wants roughly 30 by 60 feet of clear floor. A 100-by-200-foot building — a common pre-engineered size — holds about 8 to 10 courts comfortably with circulation. Ceiling height can be lower than tennis: 18 to 24 feet clear works well, which makes conversions of warehouses and retail boxes very attractive.
Can a warehouse be converted to pickleball?
It's one of the best conversion candidates in sports: the clear span and height already work, and the structure is usually adequate. The engineering work is in acoustics (warehouse metal buildings are drums without treatment), lighting upgrades to court standards, HVAC upsizing for exertion-level occupancy, court surfacing over the existing slab, and egress and plumbing for the new assembly occupancy. A good acoustic and MEP assessment up front tells you if the box is a bargain or a trap.
What court surface is used indoors?
Usually the same acrylic hard-court systems as tennis, applied over the concrete slab — consistent bounce, durable, and familiar to players. The slab needs to be flat and crack-controlled; existing warehouse slabs often need grinding, leveling, or an overlay before surfacing. Cushioned acrylic systems add player comfort and are increasingly popular for the joint-friendliness older players want.
