How Is a Puget Sound Ferry Terminal Engineered?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A Puget Sound ferry terminal is engineered for berthing and wave loads on pile-supported overwater structures with seismic ductile detailing, accessible loading bridges spanning the full tidal range, vessel shore power, corrosion-zoned materials, and contained stormwater — permitted across transportation, shoreline, and building authorities.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Berthing, Wave, and Seismic Structural Design

The engineer starts with the design vessel and the operator's berthing practice. Berthing energy — a function of vessel mass, approach velocity, and angle — sizes the fender system, and the engineer selects fenders (cone, cell, or pile-supported) for the energy absorption the operations require, then designs the supporting structure for the fender reactions. Mooring loads from wind and current on the berthed vessel add sustained lateral demand on the dolphins and pier; the engineer combines these with wave loads on the piles themselves, calculated for the site's exposure — open Sound versus sheltered inlet changes everything. Scour around the piles is evaluated with the geotechnical engineer, and pile tips are set below the predicted scour depth. Seismic detailing treats the overwater structure as the high-risk element it is. The engineer designs the pile-to-pile-cap connections for ductile hinging, checks the deck diaphragm's ability to distribute seismic forces among the piles, and evaluates liquefaction and lateral spreading of the marine sediments — the failure mode that has destroyed waterfront structures in past earthquakes. Ground improvement or deep founding below the liquefiable layer follows the geotechnical recommendations. The engineer also designs the utility and bridge connections between the fixed shore and the moving overwater structure with the flexibility the seismic displacement demands.

Passenger Bridges, Shore Power, and Environmental Permitting

The loading bridges are accessibility structures first. The engineer designs the span, hinge details, and support geometry so the walking surface stays within the accessible slope limits from extreme high to extreme low water, which for Puget Sound's range means long bridges — often with intermediate floating or guided supports — and rest areas where the length demands them. Live loads account for dense queuing crowds; the dynamic response is checked so the bridge does not bounce uncomfortably underfoot. The bridge-to-vessel interface is coordinated with the ferry operator's gate and apron geometry, and the engineer designs the fenders, guides, and bumpers that protect the bridge as vessels maneuver. Shore power is a marine electrical system, not a building feeder. The engineer designs the medium- or low-voltage connection with the vessel's required configuration, grounding that handles the shore-to-vessel fault paths, and interlocked switching that prevents paralleling with the vessel's generators. Environmental permitting shapes the in-water work: the engineer documents pile-driving methods, noise attenuation, and turbidity controls for the shoreline and fisheries reviews, and schedules construction within the permitted in-water work windows. Stormwater treatment, spill planning, and the no-untreated-discharge design are permit commitments carried into the construction documents.

What else do project teams ask?

How do passengers board across a ten-foot tide range?
On long hinged loading bridges designed so the walking surface stays within accessibility slope limits at every tide stage — extreme high to extreme low. The engineer sizes the span and supports for the steepest low-tide geometry, adds rest platforms where length requires, and coordinates the bridge's movement envelope with the vessel's mooring range so it never binds.
What are berthing loads?
The forces of the vessel's mass decelerating against the fenders as it lands — calculated from the design vessel's displacement, approach speed, and angle. The engineer sizes the fender system to absorb that energy and designs the supporting piles and deck for the fender reactions, plus the sustained wind-and-current mooring loads on the berthed vessel.
What is shore power at a ferry terminal?
Electrical service that lets a berthed ferry shut down its diesel generators and run on grid power. The engineer designs the vessel connection with the required voltage configuration, marine grounding, and safety interlocks preventing paralleling with onboard generation — a specialized marine electrical system sized for the vessel's hotel and charging loads.
Why does in-water construction have work windows?
To protect fish — particularly salmon — during sensitive life stages. Pile driving and other in-water work are restricted to the seasonal windows the fisheries agencies set, with noise attenuation and turbidity controls required. The engineer builds the construction schedule around these windows because missing one can delay the project a full year.
