What Are the Design Requirements for a Fire Station?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

A fire station is designed for minimum turnout time, diesel exhaust captured at the tailpipe in the apparatus bay, hot-warm-cold decontamination zoning that protects living quarters, and alerting plus emergency power that keep the station operational around the clock.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Apparatus bay exhaust and air quality

I design the bay exhaust in layers. The first layer is direct tailpipe capture on every apparatus position, with automatic disconnect on departure and controls interlocked to the bay doors so the system responds to actual operations. The second layer is general exhaust sized to the bay volume with CO and NO2 detection driving variable operation — the system ramps with the real contaminant load instead of running full-bore around the clock. The third layer is separation: the bay is exhaust-ventilated and negatively pressurized to every adjacent space, and the living quarters breathe through their own dedicated outside-air system with good filtration. Commissioning proves the pressure relationships with the bay doors in their real operating states — open, closed, and mid-cycle — because a pressurization strategy that only works with every door closed fails on the first call. I also design for maintenance reality: capture hoses and nozzles take abuse, so the specification favors robust, easily replaced components and the layout gives clear access to every connection point. An exhaust system the crew cannot be bothered to use is a system that does not exist.

Decontamination and crew health

The decon design starts with the traffic pattern: contaminated crews and gear enter through the bay side, pass through the warm zone — gear removal, extractor laundry, showers — and emerge clean into the cold zone. I lay out the plumbing for that workflow: commercial extractors with proper water, drainage, and ventilation; showers with the hot-water capacity for multiple simultaneous users; and floor drains and washable surfaces throughout the warm zone. Gear storage rooms get dedicated exhaust because stored turnout gear off-gasses contaminants continuously. The HVAC zoning mirrors the workflow. The cold zone — bunks, kitchen, dayroom, offices — gets its own system with filtration and no recirculation from the bay side. I keep the kitchen exhaust and the living-quarter ventilation completely independent of apparatus-bay systems, and I design the building controls so the pressure cascade from clean to dirty holds across all operating modes. Crew health is a design deliverable here, not a wellness slogan: the building is part of the department's cancer-prevention strategy, and I treat it that way.

Alerting, power, and turnout layout

The alerting system is the station's nervous system: dispatch tones, visual alerts, and bay lighting that guide each crew to its apparatus without confusion. I coordinate alerting devices with the electrical, lighting, and communications design, keep the entire system on emergency power with UPS ride-through for the switchover, and design the device layout from the bunk and dayroom locations — every sleeping firefighter must be reached instantly, including in the station's quietest corner. The floor plan and site are laid out for the response. Bunk rooms sit adjacent to the bay with the shortest compliant path; the watch office overlooks the apparatus floor; corridors are direct and doors are minimal on the turnout route. On the site, I design apron depth for the longest apparatus to maneuver, separate public and visitor parking from emergency circulation, and coordinate traffic control where trucks enter the public way. In unincorporated county areas, the station may answer to county fire-district standards and county permitting rather than a city process — I confirm the AHJ early, because district standards can add requirements beyond the base code.

What else do project teams ask?

How is diesel exhaust controlled in an apparatus bay?
At the source first, then in the room. Direct-capture systems connect to each apparatus tailpipe — typically a hose or nozzle that seals to the exhaust and releases automatically when the truck pulls out — and carry the exhaust outside before it ever enters the bay air. General exhaust ventilation with CO and NO2 detection handles the residual from door cycles and brief idling. I design the bay negative to the living quarters and give the living areas independent, filtered HVAC. Source capture is the single most effective measure; general ventilation alone cannot keep up with a diesel idling in an enclosed bay.
What are decontamination zones in a fire station?
They are the hot-warm-cold separation that keeps fireground contaminants out of the living spaces. The hot zone is the apparatus bay and gear storage where contaminated equipment arrives; the warm zone is the decon corridor with gear extractors, showers, and cleaning stations where the transition happens; the cold zone is the living quarters, kitchen, and offices that stay clean. I enforce the zones with layout — the traffic pattern forces the transition — and with HVAC pressure relationships so air always moves from clean to dirty. The design also gives the warm zone the floor drains, hot water, and ventilation its work demands.
What is a good turnout time, and how does design affect it?
Turnout time — from dispatch to wheels rolling — is measured in seconds, and the building either helps or hurts. I lay out the station so the path from bunk rooms to the apparatus bay is short, direct, and visible: minimal doors, no maze-like corridors, and the watch office positioned to see the bay. Site design matters equally — apron depth for safe apparatus maneuvering, direct access to the response route, and traffic signals or warning devices where trucks enter public roads. Every unnecessary turn in the plan is seconds added to every call for the life of the building.
How is emergency power sized for a fire station?
For the whole station, not just life safety. A fire station is an emergency facility, so the generator carries alerting and dispatch communications, apparatus bay doors, the full HVAC and lighting load, kitchen and living-quarter essentials, and fuel for extended operation. I size from a connected-load analysis with the station's real operating profile — everything running during a major incident — plus capacity for the alerting system's clean power needs. Fuel storage is sized for the outage duration the AHJ requires, and the transfer and distribution are designed so the station never goes dark during the switchover.
