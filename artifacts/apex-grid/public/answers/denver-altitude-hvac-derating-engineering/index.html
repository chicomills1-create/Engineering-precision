How Does Altitude Derating Affect Denver HVAC Engineering?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

At Denver's 5,280-foot altitude, thinner air cuts fan, cooling, and combustion capacity, so engineers upsize fans and coils to deliver the required mass flow, derate gas-fired equipment per manufacturer altitude tables, resize ductwork, and verify combustion air and venting - with altitude-corrected capacities shown on the permit submittal.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

What Thin Air Does to Airside Equipment

Every airside selection - supply fans, exhaust fans, cooling coils, condensing units - is a mass-flow device wearing a volume-flow disguise. Catalog data is published at standard sea-level density, so the engineer converts the project's required mass flow into the higher volume flow Denver's air demands and selects equipment against that corrected duty. A fan selected at sea-level volume will move the right cubic feet per minute in Denver but deliver roughly one-sixth less heating or cooling, leaving the zones short on the design day. The correction is straightforward arithmetic, but it has to be applied to every schedule entry, not just the big air handlers. Ductwork follows the equipment. Higher volumes at the same velocity need larger ducts, and the engineer rebalances the tradeoff between duct size, fan energy, and available ceiling space. Where the architecture cannot absorb larger ducts, the design may accept higher velocities with the noise and energy consequences documented, or split systems to shorten runs. Variable-air-volume systems get their control sequences checked against the derated reality: a VAV box's minimum and maximum flows are volume settings, but the zone's heating and cooling are mass-flow needs, so the sequence setpoints shift. Commissioning verifies the installed system actually delivers the corrected flows - the test-and-balance report is read against the altitude-corrected schedule, not the catalog page.

Combustion Equipment at a Mile High

Gas-fired equipment loses input capacity at altitude because combustion needs oxygen mass, not air volume. Manufacturers publish altitude-derating tables - typically a percentage reduction per thousand feet above a threshold elevation - and the engineer applies them to every furnace, boiler, unit heater, and water heater on the project, then verifies the derated output still meets the heating load with the code's required margin. Where the derated capacity falls short, the selection moves up a size or the design adds capacity; what it never does is install sea-level-rated equipment and hope. High-altitude conversion kits, where the manufacturer offers them, adjust the gas orifices and controls for the thinner air. Venting and combustion air are rechecked at the derated inputs. Natural-draft venting relies on the buoyancy of hot flue gas, and buoyancy weakens as air density drops, so the engineer verifies vent sizing, height, and configuration against the fuel-gas code's altitude provisions and the manufacturer's instructions. Combustion-air openings are sized for the derated input per the code's requirements. The safety case is explicit on the drawings: derating is not an efficiency tweak but a combustion-safety measure, and the inspector will look for the manufacturer's altitude labeling on the installed equipment.

Documenting Derating for Plan Review

Denver's mechanical plan reviewers see altitude derating on every commercial submittal, and they check it. The equipment schedule is the primary document: each line shows the tag, the service, the nominal catalog capacity, the altitude-corrected capacity with its basis - manufacturer table or calculation reference - and the load it serves. The reviewer traces from the Manual J or energy-model loads through the corrected capacities to confirm the selections are adequate, and any line missing its derating basis comes back as a correction. The supporting narrative matters on complex projects. The mechanical drawings or the accompanying calculations note the design elevation, the density correction applied to airside selections, and the derating table used for each gas-fired appliance. Special systems - commercial kitchen exhaust and makeup air, laboratory exhaust, smoke control, parking garage ventilation - each get their altitude treatment documented, since their performance is life-safety or code-compliance critical. For sites in the surrounding counties rather than the City and County of Denver, the same engineering applies but the submittal goes to the county building division with its own checklists and timelines. Either way, the AHJ approves the design only when the derating is shown, not assumed.

What else do project teams ask?

What is altitude derating?
Altitude derating is the reduction in HVAC equipment capacity caused by thinner air at elevation. Fans and cooling coils move less heat per cubic foot because the air has less mass, and gas-fired equipment produces less heat because each cubic foot of air carries less oxygen. Engineers correct sea-level catalog ratings to the project's elevation using manufacturer tables and engineering calculations before selecting equipment.
How much capacity does HVAC equipment lose at Denver's altitude?
There is no single percentage - the loss depends on the equipment type and the manufacturer. Airside capacity drops roughly in proportion to the air-density reduction, while gas-fired equipment follows each manufacturer's altitude-derating tables, which specify the input reduction per thousand feet above a threshold elevation. The engineer applies the specific manufacturer's data for each scheduled unit rather than using a blanket factor.
Do heat pumps need altitude derating too?
Yes, on the airside. A heat pump's indoor and outdoor coils move heat through air, so the reduced air density at Denver's elevation cuts the delivered heating and cooling capacity just as it does for a conventional air conditioner - the engineer selects from altitude-corrected performance data. The refrigeration circuit itself is less affected, but defrost behavior and outdoor-fan performance still get checked against the manufacturer's elevation guidance.
Does mechanical review differ between Denver and the surrounding counties?
The engineering is identical - altitude derating applies across the whole metro - but the permit path follows the parcel. Projects in the City and County of Denver go through Denver's building department with its mechanical review checklists, while unincorporated sites go through Douglas, Arapahoe, Jefferson, or Adams county building divisions. Each AHJ has its own submittal portal, review timelines, and inspection scheduling, and each decides whether the derating documentation is adequate.
