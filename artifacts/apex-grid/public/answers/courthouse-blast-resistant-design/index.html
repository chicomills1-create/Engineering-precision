How Is Blast-Resistant Design Applied to Courthouse Projects?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers layer standoff distance, progressive-collapse-resistant structure, blast-rated glazing with anchored frames, and hardened MEP — all tied to the facility's defined threat level and security standards.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Standoff, Structure, and Progressive Collapse

Standoff is the most cost-effective blast protection ever designed: distance attenuates blast pressure rapidly, so the site plan pushes unscreened vehicles away from the facade with bollards, reinforced planters, grade changes, and controlled parking setbacks. The engineer works with the security consultant to establish the design-basis threat and the achievable standoff, then sizes everything else against the resulting loads. The structural system is designed so the loss of any single column or wall segment does not cascade. This means redundant framing, ductile connections that deform without fracturing, and floor systems that can span across a lost support. Facade elements are designed as sacrificial where appropriate — cladding may fail, but the structure behind it stands and the debris is managed. The performance objectives are defined up front: life safety for occupants and continued operation for critical functions, with the understanding that the building will need repair after a design-level event.

Glazing, MEP Hardening, and Air Intakes

Windows are the dominant injury source in blast events, so glazing design gets intense focus. Laminated glass with interlayers that hold fragments together, set in frames anchored to the structure with bite depth calculated for the blast load, keeps the pane in the opening instead of turning it into projectiles. Where existing historic courthouses cannot take new blast frames, the design may use fragment-retention films or interior blast curtains as a retrofit measure — less capable than purpose-built glazing, but far better than untreated glass. MEP hardening protects the building's ability to function after an event. Generators and switchgear sit within the protected perimeter or in hardened rooms; fuel systems are secured; and air intakes are elevated above street level, located away from loading docks and parking, and designed so they can be closed quickly. Emergency systems — fire alarm, emergency power, smoke control — are routed and protected to survive the design event, because life safety after a blast depends on systems that still work.

Blast-Resistant Design Checklist

A complete blast-resistant design layers protection from the site to the systems: • Defined design-basis threat and performance objectives per applicable security standards • Maximum achievable standoff enforced with bollards, planters, and site grading • Progressive-collapse-resistant structure with redundant load paths and ductile detailing • Blast-rated laminated glazing in anchored frames; retrofit films or curtains where required • Generators, fuel, and critical distribution inside the protected perimeter or hardened • Elevated, securable air intakes located away from vehicle areas and loading docks • Life-safety and emergency systems routed and protected to survive the design event

What else do project teams ask?

What is standoff distance and why does it matter most?
Standoff is the distance between a potential explosive threat — typically a vehicle — and the building. Blast pressure decays very rapidly with distance, so each additional foot of separation reduces the load on the structure and glazing more than any feasible amount of added material. That is why site design — bollards, setbacks, controlled parking — is the first and most cost-effective layer of blast protection, before any structural hardening is even considered.
What is progressive collapse design?
It is the structural strategy that prevents a localized failure from cascading into a disproportionate collapse of the whole building. The design provides alternate load paths so that if one column or wall segment is lost, the floors above can bridge across the gap without failing. Federal standards for high-risk facilities require it, and courthouses with elevated threat profiles apply the same principles: ductile detailing, redundant framing, and members sized for the abnormal loading.
Can existing courthouse windows be made blast-resistant?
Existing glazing can be improved, though rarely to the level of purpose-built blast windows. Options include fragment-retention films that hold shattered glass together, interior blast curtains that catch debris, and — where the structure allows — replacing the glazing and anchoring new laminated units into reinforced frames. The engineer evaluates the existing frame and wall anchorage first, because a strong pane in a weak frame simply becomes a larger projectile.
Why do air intakes need blast and security protection?
Air intakes are openings into the building's lungs: at street level near vehicle areas they are vulnerable to blast debris, and they are also the pathway for intentional airborne contamination. The design elevates intakes above the street, locates them away from loading docks and parking, secures them against unauthorized access, and provides for rapid closure. Protecting the intake protects both the blast response and the everyday security of the building's air supply.
