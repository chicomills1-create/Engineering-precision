How Is EV Charging Designed for Church Parking Lots and Fleets?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers match Level 2 or DC fast chargers to dwell time and fleet needs, use load management to fit charging within existing electrical capacity, and design parking layout, lighting, metering, and conduit for future expansion.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Charger Selection, Capacity, and Load Management

The use case decides the charger. Congregation charging during services — two to three hour dwells — is the natural home of Level 2: meaningful range added per service at a per-charger electrical load most buildings can absorb. Church fleet vehicles with daytime turnaround, or a church offering public fast charging as outreach, point toward DC fast charging — far faster, far more demanding electrically, and far more expensive to install. The engineer models the actual vehicles and dwell times rather than defaulting to the most impressive hardware. Load management turns a good design into an affordable one. A managed charging system monitors the building's total demand and allocates charging power dynamically, so ten chargers share the available capacity instead of each demanding full power at once — every vehicle still leaves with a useful charge after the service, while the peak demand stays inside the existing electrical service. The design includes the panel, the management controller, and the communication wiring as one system, and it provisions empty conduit and breaker space for the chargers the church will add as EV adoption grows. Designing only for day-one chargers guarantees a second trenching project.

Parking Layout, Lighting, Billing, and Accessibility

Chargers live where the electrical runs are short and the cars already park — typically the rows nearest the building — but the layout must respect everything else the lot does. Charging spaces stay clear of accessible routes and accessible parking unless they are themselves the accessible charging spaces the code increasingly requires; they never block fire lanes or the drive-in service sight lines. Bollards or wheel stops protect the chargers from the bumpers of a busy Sunday lot, and the design keeps the equipment out of drainage paths and snow-piling zones. The charging area gets proper lighting for evening services and midweek events — even, glare-free, on the lot's lighting controls — and clear signage marking the spaces and any time limits the church sets. Metering and billing follow the church's policy: many churches offer free charging as hospitality, some bill the public while members charge free, and networked chargers handle access control and payment either way. Accessible EV spaces, where required, get the full accessible treatment — clearances, reach ranges, and routes — designed from the start rather than retrofitted after an inspection flags them.

Church EV Charging Design Checklist

A complete EV charging design serves the congregation today and the fleet tomorrow: • Charger mix matched to dwell times: Level 2 for services, DC fast for fleet needs • Load management allocating capacity dynamically within the existing service • Empty conduit, breaker space, and panel capacity for future charger additions • Charger locations with short electrical runs, clear of fire lanes and sight lines • Bollard and wheel-stop protection plus drainage and snow-zone planning • Even glare-free lighting and clear signage with the church's time-limit policy • Metering, access control, and billing matched to the church's charging policy • Accessible EV charging spaces designed to full accessibility requirements

What else do project teams ask?

Should a church install Level 2 or DC fast chargers?
For congregation charging during services, Level 2 is almost always the answer — cars dwell for hours, the per-charger power demand fits most electrical services, and the equipment cost is modest. DC fast charging earns its place for fleet vehicles needing quick turnaround or a deliberate public-facing ministry, but it demands major electrical capacity and investment. The engineer sizes from the actual vehicles and schedules; most churches start with Level 2 and provision conduit for whatever comes next.
Will EV chargers require a bigger electrical service?
Not necessarily — that is what load management is for. A managed system shares the available capacity among chargers dynamically, keeping the building's peak demand within the existing service while every vehicle still charges during its dwell. The engineer performs a load calculation with the managed charging profile to prove it. Only when the charging ambition truly exceeds the service — a bank of DC fast chargers, for example — does the design call for a utility service upgrade.
How many EV charging spaces does a church need?
Enough for today's EV-driving members plus growth, which the design estimates from the congregation's size, local EV adoption, and the church's ambitions — often starting with a modest bank of Level 2 chargers and full provisioning for expansion. The honest design sizes conduit, panel space, and load-management capacity for the five-year picture while installing the chargers the budget supports now. Trenching the lot twice is the expensive mistake this planning avoids.
Should churches charge for EV charging or offer it free?
That is a ministry and policy decision the engineering accommodates either way. Free charging is hospitality — many churches treat it like the coffee bar. Billed charging, usually through the chargers' networked payment, recovers electricity costs and manages demand for the spaces. The design provides the metering, access control, and networking for whichever policy the church chooses, and makes it changeable — policies evolve, and the infrastructure should not lock one in.
