How Is HVAC Zoning Designed for Mixed-Use Courthouse Buildings?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Engineers zone by space type and schedule — courtrooms with demand ventilation for peak crowds, chambers for individual after-hours control, holding separately with staff-managed setpoints — each on appropriately selected systems.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The Zone Map: Courtrooms, Chambers, Holding, Public

The zone map is drawn from use, not from floor plates. Courtrooms are high-gain, high-ventilation zones with occupancy that swings from zero to full between sessions — they need responsive cooling, demand-controlled ventilation, and quiet air distribution that does not compete with testimony. Chambers and judicial offices are low-gain, long-hour zones where individual control and after-hours operation matter more than peak capacity. Holding areas are dense, intermittent, staff-controlled zones with elevated ventilation. Public lobbies and jury assembly are surge zones with the highest peak loads in the building. Each zone gets the air handler or terminal equipment its profile demands rather than sharing a one-size system. The engineer resists the temptation to combine unlike spaces on one zone — a courtroom sharing a thermostat with adjacent offices will satisfy neither — and documents the zone map with its design occupancies, loads, and schedules so future renovations do not accidentally merge zones that were separated for good reason.

Ventilation, After-Hours, and Controls Integration

Demand-controlled ventilation is the courthouse's highest-value control strategy: CO2 sensors in courtrooms, jury assembly, and lobbies modulate outdoor air to actual occupancy, cutting the heating and cooling of ventilation air during the long hours these spaces sit partially occupied or empty. The engineer sizes the ventilation for the code-required peak but lets the controls live at the real condition — and verifies the sensors' placement, because a CO2 sensor in a dead-air corner lies to the system all day. After-hours operation is designed, not improvised. The building automation system offers scheduled and on-demand conditioning for chambers and individual courtrooms — a judge working late gets comfort without the central plant running the whole building — with automatic setback when the space is vacated. All zones report to the BAS with trending, so the facilities team can see which courtroom is fighting its setpoint and why. The controls sequences are written for the building's actual operating calendar, including the reality that court schedules change and the system must adapt without a controls contractor on speed dial.

Courthouse HVAC Zoning Checklist

A complete zoning design gives every space type what its use demands: • Zone map by space type and schedule: courtrooms, chambers, holding, public, support • Courtroom zones with peak-occupancy cooling and demand-controlled ventilation • Chambers and offices with individual control and designed after-hours operation • Holding zones with staff-managed setpoints and elevated ventilation rates • Quiet air distribution in courtrooms verified against background noise targets • CO2-based ventilation control with correctly placed sensors in variable spaces • BAS trending and alarming on every zone with sequences written for court operations

What else do project teams ask?

Why can't a courtroom share an HVAC zone with adjacent offices?
Because their loads and schedules are nothing alike. A courtroom swings from empty to a hundred occupants in minutes and needs rapid cooling and high ventilation; the offices next door hold a few people steadily all day. On a shared zone, the thermostat satisfies one space while the other suffers — the courtroom overheats during proceedings or the offices freeze. Separate zones let each space get what its use demands, which is the entire point of zoning.
How does demand-controlled ventilation save energy in courthouses?
Courtrooms, jury assembly rooms, and lobbies spend much of the day far below peak occupancy, but code requires ventilation for the peak. CO2 sensors let the system ventilate for the people actually present — cutting outdoor air, and the energy to heat, cool, and dehumidify it, during partial occupancy. In a courthouse with highly variable court schedules, the savings are substantial because the gap between peak and average occupancy is so large.
How do judges get HVAC after hours?
Through a designed after-hours mode, not a favor from the facilities team. The BAS provides scheduled or on-demand conditioning for chambers and individual courtrooms — typically via a simple interface or scheduled override — that starts only the equipment serving those zones. The design includes automatic shutoff so a forgotten override does not condition an empty chambers suite all weekend, and the energy use is tracked so the court sees what after-hours operation actually costs.
Why must courtroom air distribution be quiet?
Because the courtroom's product is intelligible speech. A noisy diffuser or a rumbling air handler forces people to talk louder, strains the recording system, and degrades the assistive listening feed — and in a proceeding where every word matters, that is a functional failure. The engineer selects low-noise air devices, sizes ductwork for low velocity, isolates mechanical equipment vibration, and verifies background noise against the design target with the system running at its operating condition.
