How Should Climate Be Designed for an Art Gallery or Museum?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Gallery climate design holds tight, stable temperature and humidity in collection zones, limits UV and infrared light exposure on artworks, and protects the envelope with airlocked loading docks so outside air never reaches the collections.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

Temperature and humidity control

I zone the building by collection sensitivity: galleries get tight control, collection storage gets the tightest, and offices, lobbies, and shops get standard commercial comfort — each with its own system or zone so the art never subsidizes the office's comfort or vice versa. Humidification in dry climates uses clean-steam or equivalent systems that do not introduce contaminants, and dehumidification in humid climates is sized for the real latent load including infiltration and occupancy. The control sequence is where stability is won. I design for modulating, gentle control — no bang-bang cycling, no aggressive reset strategies that chase energy savings at the expense of stability. Sensors are located where they represent the collection environment, not where they are convenient to wire, and the building automation system trends temperature and humidity continuously with alarms on drift and on rate-of-change. Redundancy is a design decision I discuss honestly with every museum client: the cost of backup humidification or a redundant air handler is small compared to the value of the collection it protects.

Lighting for conservation

The lighting design starts from the collection's sensitivity classes. I work with the lighting designer to assign each gallery and display zone its illuminance budget, then select UV-filtered LED luminaires, aiming, and controls that deliver the presentation within that budget. Track and adjustable systems get the flexibility curators need for rotating exhibitions, and every adjustable head keeps its UV filtering and aiming discipline — a re-aimed spotlight that now blasts a textile is a conservation incident. Daylight is handled as a managed resource. In public and circulation areas it is welcome; at the collection it passes through UV-filtering glazing, shades, or louvers, and direct sun never lands on artwork. I model the daylight contribution so the electric lighting and controls respond to it rather than fighting it. And the controls turn gallery lighting down or off when spaces are unoccupied — every hour of unnecessary light is unnecessary exposure, and occupancy-based control is pure conservation gain.

Dock airlocks and building envelope

The loading dock gets designed as a conservation airlock: a conditioned vestibule between the exterior door and the collection spaces, with door sequencing or interlocks so both doors are never open together, and pressure relationships that keep dock air out of the galleries. The dock HVAC handles the thermal shock of door cycles, and the space is detailed for the real work — art handling clearances, security screening, quarantine and inspection areas for incoming loans, and pest-management provisions. The envelope strategy extends the same thinking to the whole building: tight construction with verified air barriers, filtered and conditioned ventilation air, and positive pressure in collection zones relative to outdoors so infiltration moves outward, not inward. I pay special attention to the envelope details that fail most often — roof-to-wall transitions, below-grade waterproofing at collection storage, and glazing systems — because a single chronic leak or infiltration path can defeat an otherwise excellent climate system. The building protects the art; the envelope is where that protection starts.

What else do project teams ask?

What temperature and humidity do art galleries need?
Conservation guidance generally points to moderate temperatures and mid-range relative humidity, with the tightest control in collection storage. But the number I design hardest for is the rate of change — collections tolerate a steady condition far better than they tolerate swings, even swings between two acceptable values. I set the control bands with the conservator or collections staff, design the HVAC to hold them without cycling, and include monitoring and alarming so drift gets caught before it becomes damage. The right setpoint is the one the institution commits to holding year-round.
Why do humidity swings damage artwork more than steady humidity?
Because the materials in artwork — canvas, wood panels, paper, ivory, adhesives — absorb and release moisture, expanding and contracting as they do. A rapid swing sets up internal stresses: paint layers move differently than their supports, joints open, veneers crack. Slow seasonal drift is manageable; a fast swing from a cycling HVAC system or a dock door left open is what causes damage. That is why the HVAC design prioritizes stability — gentle control, no overshoot, no short-cycling — over hitting an exact number.
How is lighting limited to protect art?
Three ways: limit the total light dose on sensitive works, eliminate ultraviolet, and keep infrared heat off the surface. I specify UV-filtered LED sources, design illuminance to the sensitivity class of the collection — light-sensitive works get a fraction of what robust materials receive — and use dimming, occupancy-based control, and shading so lights are only as bright as needed, only when visitors are present. Daylight is filtered and controlled, never direct, on collections. The lighting designer and I coordinate on every gallery, because presentation and preservation have to be solved together.
Why do museums need airlocked loading docks?
Because the dock is where the building's climate control meets the outside world, and art moves through it. A single open dock door on a hot, humid day — or a cold, dry winter day — can flood the adjacent collection spaces with unconditioned air faster than the HVAC can respond, and it admits dust, pests, and security risk at the same time. An airlocked dock with interlocked or sequenced doors, its own conditioned vestibule, and proper pressure relationships keeps the collection environment intact during every delivery and pickup. It is one of the highest-value details in the whole building.
