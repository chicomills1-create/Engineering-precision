What Engineering Support Do Developers Need During Entitlement?

By Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder, Apex Grid Engineering — USAF Veteran. · Updated 2026-09-15

Developers need PE-level entitlement support: technical studies, agency letters, and engineering review of the concept while entitlements are pending — retiring engineering objections before they become conditions, and building the record the EOR will inherit at permit design.

I'm Jeremy Mills, CEO & Founder of Apex Grid Engineering and a U.S. Air Force veteran. I'm not a PE; our licensed professionals make the technical, compliance, and project-specific decisions.

The entitlement engineering toolkit

I assemble the toolkit to the agencies in play, but the core pieces recur. The drainage and grading concept shows the agency the site can handle its water — existing versus proposed runoff, detention concept, floodplain compliance — backed by a PE technical memorandum where the jurisdiction requires one. The utility concept confirms service: water, sewer, power, and gas availability letters from the districts, with capacity analysis where the project's demand is large relative to the system. The geotechnical review reads existing data for the foundation and grading implications the concept must respect; if no data exists, I scope the investigation the design phase will need so the entitlement budget carries it. Traffic and access get engineering attention proportional to the project's impact — a trip generation memo for small projects, a full traffic study coordinated with a traffic engineer for large ones. And the entitlement exhibits themselves — the site plan, the massing studies, the phasing concepts — get a PE review pass for engineering feasibility before they go public. Nothing damages entitlement credibility like a concept plan the agency's engineer can see will not work. I would rather find the problem in my review than have the public works director find it at the hearing.

Working the agencies before the hearings

The highest-value entitlement engineering happens before the application is filed, in pre-application meetings with planning, public works, and the utility districts. I attend these with the developer's team because the agencies' engineers speak plainly in pre-app about what will concern them — the drainage standard they will apply, the utility upgrade they anticipate, the traffic threshold that triggers a study — and that intelligence shapes the application before positions harden. A pre-app meeting that surfaces the sewer capacity question lets us get the district's letter and engineer the solution into the concept; the same question raised at the hearing becomes a continuance. I also manage the technical correspondence during the review: responding to agency engineers' comments with memoranda and revised exhibits, negotiating conditions of approval that are engineerable (some proposed conditions are not, and the time to say so is during negotiation, not after adoption), and documenting every commitment. The entitlement record I build — every study, every letter, every condition with its engineering implications noted — is the most valuable document the developer owns at the end of entitlement. It becomes the EOR's briefing package, and it is why the permit phase starts fast instead of starting over.

Entitlement-phase engineering checklist

Line these up while entitlements are pending and the permit phase inherits momentum. • Engage the PE at pre-application — agency engineers reveal their real concerns in pre-app, not at the hearing • Prepare the core exhibits: drainage/grading concept, utility capacity confirmation, geotechnical review, traffic concept • Get stamped technical memoranda where agencies require a licensed professional's statement behind the engineering • Negotiate conditions of approval with engineering input — flag unbuildable conditions before adoption, not after • Build the entitlement record deliberately: every study, letter, and commitment documented as the EOR's starting package

What else do project teams ask?

What engineering exhibits do planning commissions usually want?
Concept-level civil exhibits: site grading and drainage concept, utility service concept with capacity confirmation from the districts, traffic generation and access concept, and floodplain or environmental constraint mapping where applicable. Agencies want to see that the engineering is feasible, not that it is final — a PE-reviewed concept with a technical memorandum behind it answers the commission's questions credibly. I prepare these exhibits to the level the specific agency expects; a pre-application meeting tells you exactly which exhibits will be scrutinized, and I never skip that meeting.
Can a PE stamp entitlement exhibits?
A PE can stamp technical memoranda, studies, and reports — putting a professional statement behind the engineering conclusions — but entitlement concept exhibits are generally not "sealed drawings" in the permit sense, because they are not final construction documents. The distinction matters: the PE's stamp on a drainage study certifies the study's engineering, not a promise about the final design. I am explicit with developers about what each stamped deliverable certifies. Agencies understand the difference; what they want is a licensed professional standing behind the technical claims, and that is what the stamped memorandum provides.
How does entitlement engineering differ across Arizona, Texas, and Florida?
The agencies and the issues differ more than the engineering does. Arizona entitlement often runs through city planning with county overlays, and water/drainage questions dominate — assured water supply concepts get real scrutiny. Texas entitlement varies dramatically: Houston has no zoning (deed restrictions and chapter 42 platting rule instead), while Dallas, Austin, and San Antonio run full zoning processes with their own technical expectations. Florida layers water management district review over local entitlement, and drainage/floodplain engineering can drive the entire site concept. I scope entitlement support to the specific agencies in play — the wrong state's playbook applied to the wrong jurisdiction is how hearings go badly.
When should the developer transition from entitlement support to the EOR?
At design development, when the entitled concept becomes the building being designed — and ideally it is the same PE or the same firm, for continuity. The transition deliverable is the entitlement record: the studies, the agency letters, the conditions of approval, and the engineering commitments made during hearings. I formalize the handoff as a new engagement (EOR review-and-seal for permit design) with the entitlement record as its starting package. Developers who treat it as one continuous relationship get permit designs that honor the entitlement commitments; developers who switch firms cold often redesign things the entitlement already settled.
