Biotech Research Lab Engineering

BSL Labs, Vivariums & Genomics Facilities — Engineered for Speed

Biotech runs on ventilation. A research lab is a building where the air itself is engineered: directional airflow that keeps pathogens inside containment, fume-hood exhaust that keeps chemists safe, and pressure cascades that hold steady while doors open and sashes move. Generic MEP firms treat lab HVAC as comfort cooling with extra air changes — that is how labs fail inspection, fail commissioning, and fail their scientists. Apex Grid Engineering designs the mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and structural systems of research labs as one coordinated package, from BSL-2 startup suites to vivariums to genomics facilities.

And we do it at startup speed. Biotech companies race to occupancy: the lease is signed, the venture round has a clock on it, and every month of delay burns runway. The multinational giants quote quarters; our integrated MEP-plus-structural team delivers permit-ready lab drawings in weeks. As a veteran-owned firm licensed in 49 states with 10+ licensed professional engineers, we seal lab drawings in every major biotech hub in America — Boston, the Bay Area, San Diego, Research Triangle, the BioHealth Capital Region — and in every state with a university research lab, which is all of them.

Biotech lab engineering questions

What does biotech lab engineering cost?
Engineering fees for a research lab typically run 8–12% of the lab construction cost for full MEP + structural design, but the honest driver is complexity: a BSL-2 genomics suite in existing shell space is a fundamentally different effort than a ground-up BSL-3 facility with a vivarium. What moves the number is air-change rates, exhaust quantities, redundancy, and how much of the program is containment vs. open lab. Share the target square footage, biosafety level, and schedule for a project-specific scope and fee.
How fast can a biotech lab be engineered?
Speed is the reason startups hire us instead of the multinational giants. A BSL-2 lab fit-out in existing shell space can go from basis of design to permit-ready drawings in weeks, not quarters — VC-backed timelines cannot wait on bureaucratic firms. The integrated MEP-plus-structural team eliminates the inter-discipline coordination delays that stall most lab projects. Tell us the lease commencement date and we will work backward from it.
What is the difference between BSL-2 and BSL-3 lab design?
BSL-2 is the workhorse of biotech: directional airflow into the lab, biosafety cabinets for aerosol-generating procedures, an eyewash, and an autoclave for decontamination. BSL-3 is a different building — sealed penetrations, directional airflow with visual verification, exhaust that cannot recirculate, and typically redundant supply and exhaust fans so containment never depends on a single motor. The MEP cost roughly doubles from BSL-2 to BSL-3, and the controls complexity more than doubles.
Do you engineer vivariums?
Yes. Vivarium HVAC follows the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals: 10–15 air changes per hour, tight temperature and humidity control, pressure relationships that protect both animals and staff, and cage-wash and bedding-disposal infrastructure that most MEP firms have never designed. ABSL-2 and ABSL-3 vivariums layer biosafety containment on top of animal-care requirements — the two codes have to be satisfied simultaneously.

The ventilation moat: why lab engineering is a specialty

Anyone can draw ductwork. Almost no one can engineer a lab air system that holds containment, protects people, and passes commissioning — here is what the specialty actually covers.

Mechanical: the air is the project

In a lab building, HVAC is not a comfort system — it is the primary containment and safety system. We design VAV lab air systems with 6–12 air changes per hour for BSL-2 (higher for BSL-3), pressure-independent controls that hold room differentials of −0.02 to −0.05 inches w.c. against doors opening and sashes moving, and exhaust systems that never recirculate containment air. Energy recovery is engineered around the constraint that lab exhaust cannot cross-contaminate supply — runaround loops and heat-pipe recovery where codes allow, never enthalpy wheels on containment exhaust.

Electrical: power for instruments that cannot blink

Genomics sequencers, ultra-low freezers, mass spectrometers, and vivarium life-support loads need clean, backed-up power. We design dedicated lab panels, isolated grounding for sensitive instruments, emergency power for freezers and critical ventilation, and lighting controls that serve both the science and the energy code. A freezer farm losing power over a weekend is a seven-figure loss — the electrical design treats it that way.

Plumbing: more than sinks

Lab plumbing covers acid-waste and neutralization systems, reverse-osmosis and deionized water distribution, emergency eyewash and shower tempering, cage-wash steam condensate and drainage, and lab gas manifolds (nitrogen, CO2, compressed air, vacuum). Each is a specialty system with its own code path — we coordinate them as one package so the lab works on day one.

Structural: heavy equipment, vibration, and exhaust

Lab buildings carry real weight: rooftop exhaust fans and scrubbers, heavy fume-hood ductwork, freezer farms, and vivarium rack systems. We engineer floor loading for current equipment plus growth, vibration criteria for sensitive instruments (microscopes, sequencers), seismic anchorage for lab casework and gas cylinders, and roof structure for exhaust stacks that must clear the building's aerodynamic wake.

Biosafety levels, in engineering terms

The BMBL — Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories — defines four biosafety levels; commercial biotech work lives at BSL-1 through BSL-3. BSL-1 is basic microbiology with standard lab practices: the engineering is essentially a well-ventilated lab with good plumbing. BSL-2, where most biotech startups operate, adds directional airflow into the laboratory, Class II biosafety cabinets for any procedure that can create aerosols, an eyewash station, and an autoclave for decontaminating waste. The HVAC must guarantee air always flows from clean areas toward the lab — never the reverse.

BSL-3 is a step change, not a step up. The lab envelope is sealed: every penetration caulked, every door self-closing, the room verified to hold its pressure cascade. Supply and exhaust are interlocked so exhaust can never stop while supply runs — that failure mode pressurizes the lab and pushes containment into the corridor. Exhaust air cannot be recirculated to any other space, and the design typically carries N+1 redundancy on both supply and exhaust fans, because containment cannot depend on a single motor. Access is controlled, and the ventilation system alarms — visibly and audibly — the moment a pressure relationship decays.

Across all levels, the engineering details that decide pass-or-fail: air-change rates matched to the hazard (6–12 ACH for BSL-2 labs, higher for BSL-3 and vivariums), pressure differentials held by pressure-independent controls rather than hope, and a commissioning plan that physically proves every airflow relationship before the first experiment runs. We engineer all of it, and we stay through commissioning to prove it.

Pressure cascades and emergency purge

A lab building is a topography of air pressure. Corridors sit at the highest pressure; general labs slightly negative to the corridor; BSL-2 labs negative to the general lab; BSL-3 suites at the bottom of the cascade, typically −0.05 inches w.c. relative to adjacent spaces, stepping down through anterooms. Each step is small — a door opening can erase it — which is why the cascade is held by fast-acting VAV controls with room-pressure sensors, not by balancing dampers set once and forgotten.

Emergency purge is the lab's fire alarm for air: on activation — a chemical spill, a release alarm — the system drives the affected lab to 10–12 air changes per hour of 100% exhaust, clearing the space in minutes. Purge mode has to be designed into the air handlers, the exhaust capacity, and the controls sequence from the start; it cannot be bolted on after the fact. We design the normal, setback, and purge modes as one sequence of operations, so the building automation system knows exactly what to do in every scenario.

Genomics, vivariums, and the labs beyond BSL

Not every biotech lab is a containment lab. Genomics facilities are engineered around contamination control in the opposite direction: PCR clean rooms where the enemy is stray DNA, with unidirectional workflow from clean to dirty, separate air systems for pre- and post-amplification spaces, and pressure relationships that protect the sample instead of the worker. Sequencing suites need vibration control, tight temperature stability for the instruments, and emergency power for the freezer farms holding irreplaceable sample libraries.

Vivariums answer to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals: 10–15 air changes per hour, temperature held within a degree or two, humidity controlled, and — critically — pressure zoning that isolates animal rooms from procedure rooms, cage wash, and corridors. At ABSL-2 and ABSL-3 the animal-care requirements and the biosafety requirements apply simultaneously, and the MEP design has to satisfy both without compromise. Cage-wash areas need steam, high-temperature drainage, and waterproof construction; bedding disposal needs its own ventilation. This is the most specialized MEP scope in the building industry, and it is exactly the work generic firms decline.

Codes & standards we design biotech labs to

Lab design is code-dense. We engineer to the standards below and confirm adopted editions with the authority having jurisdiction on every project.

BMBL — Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories

The CDC/NIH biosafety bible. Defines BSL-1 through BSL-4 practices, facility requirements, and the containment principles behind every pressure cascade and exhaust decision we engineer.

ASHRAE Laboratory Design Guide & ANSI/AIHA Z9.5

The ventilation engineering standards: lab air-change rates, fume-hood face velocities (typically 80–120 fpm), VAV control strategies, and exhaust stack design against re-entrainment.

Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals

The vivarium standard: 10–15 ACH, temperature/humidity ranges by species, and the environmental requirements that shape every animal-room air system we design.

IBC, IMC, IPC + local amendments

The building, mechanical, and plumbing codes as adopted — plus state lab-safety rules and institutional biosafety committee requirements where they apply.
