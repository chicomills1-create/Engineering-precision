Plumbing Engineering Services

Domestic water, sanitary, storm, and fuel-gas systems engineered by calculation — fixture units, pressure budgets, and slope checks documented so plan reviewers approve and installers don't improvise.

Sized by Calculation, Not Habit

Plumbing looks simple until it fails plan check — or worse, until the top-floor fixtures starve at peak demand. Our domestic water design starts with a fixture-unit count per the adopted plumbing code (UPC or IPC), converts it to demand flow, and works through a documented pressure budget: street pressure, meter and backflow losses, elevation head, and pipe friction, ending with the residual pressure at the most remote fixture. If the math says a booster pump or a pressure-reducing valve is needed, it appears on the drawings — not as a field surprise.

Sanitary design assigns drainage fixture units, sizes branches and stacks per the code tables, and verifies slope and cleanout placement so the system is maintainable, not just compliant. Venting is engineered — conventional, circuit, or air-admittance where the jurisdiction allows — because improper venting is the most common source of failed inspections and trap-siphoning callbacks. Grease interceptors for food service are sized to the local pretreatment authority's formula and coordinated with the civil site design for access and pumping.

Fuel-gas piping is calculated on total connected BTU load with the longest-run method, documented pipe-by-pipe, with regulator selection and vent terminations located per code clearances. Storm drainage covers roof drain and overflow sizing for the local rainfall intensity, piping to the point of discharge, and coordination with the civil stormwater system.

Water Heating, Backflow, and Specialty Systems

Water heater design is matched to the demand profile: storage versus tankless, recovery rates for high-demand uses like restaurants and salons, hot-water recirculation to meet the energy code's dead-leg limits, and thermal expansion control. Mixed-use and multifamily buildings get central-versus-distributed analyses so developers can see the tradeoff in riser space, metering, and maintenance before committing.

Backflow prevention is engineered to the water purveyor's cross-connection control program — assembly type, location, and access documented for the district's inspector. We also design specialty scopes: medical gas systems coordinated with NFPA 99 requirements, compressed air for shops, and acid-waste systems for labs, each with the code documentation the specialty inspector expects to see.

Where Plumbing Projects Go Wrong — and How We Prevent It

The most expensive plumbing problems are elevation problems. A sanitary main that arrives at the street sewer below the district's invert forces an ejector pump and a lifetime of maintenance; a grease interceptor set without checking the kitchen's slab elevations ends up with fixtures that cannot gravity-drain to it. We design waste systems backwards from the point of connection — verified invert, not assumed — through every interceptor, cleanout, and fixture, with slopes checked at each segment. On tenant improvements, we pothole or camera-verify the existing building drain before committing a layout to it, because record drawings of under-slab piping are wrong more often than they are right.

The second failure mode is jurisdictional. UPC and IPC states size differently; water purveyors run their own backflow programs with their own approved-assembly lists; pretreatment authorities each publish their own interceptor formula; and health departments layer requirements onto food-service plumbing that the plumbing code never mentions. Our multi-state practice means we check each of these authorities by name for every project, and the drawings cite the specific standards each inspector will enforce — so the health inspector, the cross-connection inspector, and the building inspector are all reading a set that already speaks their language.

The engagement runs like our other disciplines: backgrounds in, fixed-fee proposal out within about a day, then design coordinated live with the mechanical, electrical, and civil teams — gas loads onto the mechanical equipment schedule, water heater circuits onto the panel schedule, and the building sewer aligned with the civil site utilities at an agreed point of connection five feet outside the building. Permit sets include isometric diagrams where jurisdictions require them, and after permit we review fixture and equipment submittals, answer RFIs with sketches, and support the rough-in, top-out, and final inspections through closeout.

Water efficiency has quietly become a design driver alongside code compliance. Modern energy and green codes cap fixture flow rates, many western jurisdictions add their own conservation ordinances, and owners increasingly ask for the operating-cost math: a hot-water recirculation system tuned with a demand-controlled pump can cut both water waste and heater runtime, while an oversized one wastes energy around the clock. We design to the strictest applicable standard, document the fixture flow rates on the schedules where inspectors look for them, and flag the incentive programs — utility rebates for high-efficiency water heaters and low-flow retrofits are common — that can offset the cost of doing it right. Efficient plumbing is not a premium add-on; engineered correctly, it is simply what the calculation produces. The same discipline applies to material selection — copper, PEX, CPVC, and cast iron each have jurisdictions and applications where they belong, and our specifications say which and why.

Plumbing Engineering FAQs

UPC or IPC — does it matter which code my state uses?
Yes — sizing tables, venting rules, and fixture requirements differ between the Uniform Plumbing Code and International Plumbing Code. We design to whichever your jurisdiction has adopted, and with licensure in 49 states we track which is which.
Why did the city ask for a grease interceptor calculation?
Food-service uses discharge grease that municipal treatment can't accept, so pretreatment authorities require an interceptor sized by their published formula — typically based on fixture flow or meals served. We produce that calculation and coordinate the interceptor's location with the site design.
Can you tell me if my water pressure will support an addition?
Yes — a fixture-unit and pressure-budget analysis against your street pressure answers it definitively. If the numbers fall short, the fix is usually a larger meter, a pressure-reducing valve reconfiguration, or a booster pump, and we'll show you which.
Do you design gas piping for generators and rooftop equipment?
Yes. New gas loads change the total connected demand, which can require re-sizing runs back to the meter. We calculate the whole system, not just the new branch, so the existing equipment keeps working when the new load fires.
What's required for hot-water recirculation?
Modern energy codes limit how much water can sit stranded between the heater and the fixture. Recirculation loops, demand-controlled pumps, or point-of-use heaters are the standard solutions, and we select based on the building's layout and hours of use.
