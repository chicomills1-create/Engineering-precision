Electrical Engineering Services

Power distribution, lighting, and life-safety electrical systems engineered to the National Electrical Code — with the load calculations, one-line diagrams, and fault-current data reviewers and utilities actually ask for.

Distribution Design That Survives Plan Check

Electrical plan review turns on documentation: a one-line diagram that matches the panel schedules, NEC Article 220 load calculations that justify the service size, and fault-current values that prove the equipment ratings. We produce all of it as a matter of course. Service and feeder design covers utility coordination for new or upgraded services, transformer and switchboard selection, grounding and bonding per Article 250, and voltage-drop checks on long feeder and branch runs so circuits perform at their rated load, not just on paper.

Panel schedules are built from connected and demand loads with the code-permitted demand factors applied and shown — the difference between a service that passes review and one that draws a correction letter asking where the numbers came from. Short-circuit calculations establish available fault current at each bus, and we document series ratings or fully rated equipment accordingly. For projects with generators or larger services, we address selective coordination where the code requires it.

Lighting design pairs photometric layouts with the energy code's lighting power allowances and mandatory controls: occupancy sensors, daylight-responsive dimming, automatic shutoff, and exterior lighting curfews, each documented in a controls narrative the inspector can verify. Egress and emergency lighting is designed to the illumination levels the building code requires, with battery or generator backup circuits identified on the plans.

Specialty Scopes and Existing Buildings

Beyond core distribution, we engineer EV charging infrastructure with load management where the service can't simply be doubled, photovoltaic interconnections with the required placards and rapid-shutdown documentation, generator and transfer-switch systems for standby power, and motor and equipment circuits for industrial tenants. Each of these scopes has its own code articles and its own reviewer expectations, and each package we deliver is written to them.

Existing-building work — service upgrades, panel replacements, tenant fit-outs on aging infrastructure — starts with a field-verified assessment of what is actually installed. We document existing conditions, calculate spare capacity honestly, and tell you before design begins whether the existing service can carry the new load or an upgrade is unavoidable.

From Utility Coordination to Final Inspection

Electrical projects have a stakeholder no other trade deals with as directly: the serving utility. New services, capacity increases, and transformer changes all run through the utility's own engineering queue, which in many territories is now the longest lead item on the entire project. We start that process early — load letters, site plans showing equipment locations and clearances, and coordination on primary routing — while the customer-side design proceeds in parallel, so the utility's timeline and the permit timeline converge instead of stacking.

The design phase is anchored by calculations that must agree with each other: the Article 220 service calculation, the panel schedules that roll up to it, the one-line diagram that depicts it, and the fault-current study that proves the equipment can safely interrupt what the utility can deliver. Reviewers check for exactly this internal consistency, and so do we — a one-line showing a 400-amp main over panel schedules that sum to 600 amps of demand is the kind of contradiction that turns one review cycle into three. Voltage drop on long runs, conductor derating for conduit fill and ambient temperature, and working-clearance layouts drawn to scale round out the package.

Coordination with the other trades is continuous because electrical touches everything: mechanical equipment schedules feed the load calculation, so a late RTU substitution gets re-checked against its circuit and breaker; the structural team confirms roof and pad support for transformers and generators; the plumbing team's water heaters and pumps appear on our panel schedules. After permit, we review switchgear and fixture submittals, confirm that substituted equipment preserves the fault-current ratings and coordination assumptions in the study, respond to inspector questions during rough and final, and update the one-line to as-built conditions — because the next engineer to touch the building will start from that drawing.

Capacity planning is where electrical design earns its keep over the life of a building. Electrification is pushing loads upward everywhere: heat pumps replacing gas furnaces, EV chargers appearing in lease requirements, induction replacing gas ranges, and battery storage arriving alongside solar. A service sized to yesterday's load with no spare capacity converts each of those upgrades into a five-figure service replacement. We design distribution with documented spare capacity and physical space for future breakers, and when budgets are tight we at least rough in the pathways — conduit stubs and panel space cost hundreds during construction and thousands after it. Owners planning a phased buildout get a load roadmap showing exactly what the service can absorb before an upgrade becomes unavoidable, with each phase's demand documented against the service rating.

Electrical Engineering FAQs

How do I know if my existing service can handle new equipment?
With an NEC Article 220 load calculation against the existing service rating — either from historical demand data or a documented connected-load analysis. We do this before design, so you know whether a service upgrade is in the budget on day one.
Do you coordinate with the utility company?
Yes. New services, upgrades, and transformer changes all run through the serving utility's engineering process, and we prepare the load letters and site documentation they require while designing the customer side to match.
What do you need for an EV charging design?
The charger count and levels you want, the panel and service information for the building, and the site plan. We design the distribution, apply load-management where it saves a service upgrade, and document the ventilation and signage requirements that come with it.
Can you stamp electrical drawings for my solar installer?
Yes — we produce or review one-lines, calculations, and interconnection documentation for PV systems, coordinated with the AHJ's and the utility's submission requirements.
Do your lighting designs handle energy-code controls?
Every lighting package includes the mandatory controls for the adopted energy code — occupancy sensing, daylighting zones, automatic shutoff — documented in a narrative format inspectors can walk and verify.
